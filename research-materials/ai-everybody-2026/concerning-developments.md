# The Concerning: Real Risks Without the Hype
## Research for "AI For Everybody 2026" Talk

---

## Framing for Speakers

The goal is honest concern, not panic. These are real problems, many well-documented, some speculative. Dismissing them is intellectually dishonest; catastrophizing them serves no one. Each concern below comes with: what's actually happening, what's contested, and what can realistically be done.

---

## 1. Job Displacement: Who's Actually at Risk

### The Honest Picture

This is the most personally charged concern for most audiences, and the most frequently misrepresented in both directions.

**The overhyped claim**: "AI will take all the jobs." McKinsey, Goldman Sachs, and others published analyses estimating 300 million to 800 million jobs globally "exposed" to automation. These headlines are real; what they mean is contested.

**The underhyped reality**: Exposure ≠ elimination. "Exposed" means the tasks in that job can theoretically be automated. Whether they will be, and when, and at what cost to workers, depends on economics, regulation, employer choices, and tasks that prove harder to automate than predicted. But the direction of pressure is real.

**Key statistics (verified 2025-2026 data):**
- WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025: **92 million roles displaced** by 2030; **170 million new roles** emerge — net gain of 78 million, but the new jobs cluster differently (sectors, skills, geography)
- McKinsey estimate: today's technology could automate approximately **57% of current US work hours** (not 57% of jobs — hours)
- First six months of 2025: **77,999 AI-attributed tech job losses**; **20% decline in employment** for software developers aged 22–25 vs. late-2022 peak
- Challenger, Gray & Christmas: **55,000 job cuts directly attributed to AI** in 2025 (out of 1.17M total layoffs)
- NBER 2025: **3.9% of US workers** sit at the intersection of high AI exposure and low adaptive capacity
- **79% of employed US women** work in high-automation-risk jobs vs. 58% of men
- **6.1 million workers** (4.2% of workforce) face both high AI exposure and low adaptive capacity; ~86% are women; concentrated in smaller metro areas in Mountain West and Midwest

**Who's actually at risk first?** The counterintuitive answer: white-collar cognitive workers, not blue-collar physical workers — reversing 200 years of automation history:
- Entry-level writers, editors, and content creators
- Paralegals and entry-level legal associates
- Junior data analysts and business intelligence roles
- Customer service representatives
- Medical transcriptionists and medical coders
- Entry-level accounting and bookkeeping
- Graphic designers doing production (not concept) work
- Certain junior engineering roles (code review, documentation)

**Who's more protected?**
- Workers in highly physical, variable environments (trades, construction, care work, repair)
- Workers whose value is relationship-based (therapists, social workers, key account managers, teachers)
- Workers whose value is judgment in complex novel situations (senior lawyers, senior doctors, executives)
- Workers who can successfully use AI as a productivity multiplier (becoming more valuable, not less)

### The Wave Structure

Research from labor economists describes this as playing out in waves:
- **Wave 1 (now–2028)**: Cognitive routine tasks. Standardized knowledge work that follows rules. This is already happening.
- **Wave 2 (2026–2030)**: Agentic AI taking multi-step workflows. An AI that doesn't just draft the email — it handles the whole correspondence thread.
- **Wave 3 (2030+)**: Physical-cognitive hybrid tasks, once robotic AI matures.

### The Distribution Problem

The most important and under-discussed dimension: who bears the cost and who captures the gains?

History shows productivity gains from automation don't automatically distribute broadly. In recent decades, productivity gains from technology went overwhelmingly to capital owners and highly skilled workers, while median wages stagnated. AI is currently following this pattern.

The worker whose job is eliminated needs a new job. The company that eliminated it captured the value. Without policy intervention (retraining programs, taxation of AI-generated productivity, extended safety nets), the gains are concentrated and the costs distributed.

## 2. Misinformation at Scale

### What's Actually Happening

The capability to produce convincing false content — text, image, audio, video — has shifted from expensive and specialized to cheap and ubiquitous. This changes the information environment in several measurable ways:

**Synthetic media in the wild:**
- The 2024 US election saw an estimated 500,000+ pieces of AI-generated political content
- AI-generated audio of politicians saying things they never said was distributed in multiple countries' elections
- Several countries' elections in 2024-2025 saw documented cases of AI deepfake disinformation campaigns

**The volume problem**: Human fact-checkers cannot scale to match AI-generated content. There are several thousand professional fact-checkers globally. AI can generate thousands of pieces of false content per hour. This is not a fixable problem with more fact-checkers.

**The trust erosion problem**: The existence of deepfakes — even when rarely encountered — degrades trust in *all* media. When any video could be fake, people become more likely to dismiss real evidence as potentially fabricated. This "liar's dividend" (term coined by law professors Bobby Chesney and Danielle Citron) may be as damaging as the deepfakes themselves.

### Deepfake Scale Data (Current)

- **8 million** deepfakes projected to be shared in 2025 (European Parliamentary Research Service, July 2025) — up from 500,000 in 2023
- **AI scams surged 1,210%** in 2025; GenAI-enabled scams rose **456%** between May 2024 and April 2025
- Deepfake fraud attempts increased **2,137% over three years**, rising to about 1 in 15 detected fraud cases
- FBI 2025 Internet Crime Report: victims lost **$893 million** to AI-related scams in 2025
- Deepfake-enabled scams projected to cause **$40 billion in global losses by 2027**
- Voice cloning can be generated from **as little as 3 seconds of audio**
- **1 in 3** people who engage with AI-powered scam calls end up losing money; average loss **$18,000**
- Arup (engineering firm): finance employee transferred **$25.6 million** across 15 transactions after a fake video call where everyone — including the CFO — was AI-generated deepfake (2024)

### 2024 Election Deepfake Specifics

- 80%+ of countries experienced observable AI usage relevant to their elections
- 38 countries faced deepfake incidents in elections since 2021 (affecting 3.8 billion people)
- India 2024 election: political parties spent an estimated **$50 million** on AI-generated content
- New Hampshire primary 2024: AI-generated robocalls using Biden's voice urged Democrats to skip the primary
- Content creation accounted for **90%** of all observed AI election use cases
- **Meta's assessment**: less than 1% of all fact-checked misinformation in 2024 election cycles was AI content (suggests impact was more about spread of real content, not synthetic)

### Deepfake Detection Limitations

- Sensity AI benchmarks: accuracy 95–98% in controlled conditions — but "controlled conditions" is the problem
- Intel FakeCatcher uses blood-flow (photoplethysmography) signals rather than visual artifacts — one of the most technically distinctive approaches
- Deepfake detection market: 23 US companies, 7 UK, spread globally as of 2025
- The market has shifted from single-model video detectors to multimodal platforms covering video, image, audio, and text

### What Can Be Done

- Media literacy education that specifically addresses synthetic media
- Watermarking/provenance standards (C2PA — Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity — developing industry standards)
- Platform policies requiring disclosure of AI-generated content
- Voter education about AI-generated political content

## 3. Privacy Erosion

### The Data Reality

AI systems are trained on data. The most powerful AI systems are trained on virtually everything humans have published on the internet. This includes:
- Writing people did not intend to be used for AI training
- Images published with copyright expectations
- Personal information that is technically public but was never meant to be aggregated and analyzed at scale

**Surveillance amplification**: AI makes surveillance vastly more effective. Face recognition, gait recognition, behavioral pattern analysis — capabilities that previously required significant human analyst time now run automatically at scale. In countries with weak privacy protections (and in corporate contexts in the US with weak privacy laws), this enables unprecedented monitoring.

**The inference problem**: AI can often infer private information from non-private data. Health status from shopping patterns. Sexual orientation from browsing history. Financial stress from payment timing. These inferences may be wrong — but they are acted upon by algorithmic systems making consequential decisions.

**Data minimization is not AI-compatible**: The privacy principle "collect only what you need" is incompatible with how large AI systems work — they benefit from more data, in more contexts, combined across sources. The incentive structure of AI development directly conflicts with privacy protection.

### For Lay Audiences

Practical implications people can relate to:
- Your voice assistant conversations are (likely) being used in some form for AI improvement
- Your health data, if shared with apps, may inform AI models beyond your care
- Your face may be in a training set if you've ever posted photos publicly
- AI-powered hiring screening tools analyze candidates' online presence in ways that may be invisible to the candidate

The challenge: opt-out is largely theoretical. Most of us cannot practically remove our data from the systems that now contain it.

## 4. The Power Concentration Problem

### A Few Companies, Enormous Influence

By April 2026, the frontier AI systems that most people interact with come from a small number of companies:
- OpenAI (majority Microsoft-backed)
- Google DeepMind (Alphabet)
- Anthropic (significant Amazon investment)
- Meta AI
- xAI (Elon Musk)
- Mistral (European, with Microsoft investment)
- DeepSeek (Chinese)

The capital requirements for training frontier models — estimated at hundreds of millions to billions of dollars per training run — mean that state-of-the-art AI is only accessible to entities with enormous resources.

**What this means:**
- The values, priorities, and governance decisions of a handful of companies and their leaders shape the AI systems billions of people use
- There is no democratic process for deciding what AI should optimize for, what content it should decline to produce, or whose values it should reflect
- Disagreements within AI labs about safety and capability are resolved by internal deliberation, not public process
- The economic returns from AI deployment accumulate primarily to shareholders of these companies

### The Billionaire Problem (Plain Language)

It is accurate to say that individual billionaires — Sam Altman, Elon Musk, Sundar Pichai, Jeff Bezos (through Amazon's Anthropic stake), Mark Zuckerberg — have more direct influence over the trajectory of AI development than any government, regulatory body, or citizen process.

This is not an argument that any of these individuals has bad intentions. It is a structural observation about who is making decisions that affect everyone, without broad accountability. The same argument applies to pharmaceutical companies, media conglomerates, and financial institutions — AI simply makes the concentration starker because the technology is newer and the incumbents fewer.

## 5. The Regulation Gap

### Where Policy Is

**European Union**: The AI Act (passed 2024, enforcement phased 2025-2026) is the world's most comprehensive AI regulation. It creates a risk-tiered framework:
- Unacceptable risk (banned): real-time biometric surveillance in public, social scoring, manipulation of vulnerable groups
- High risk (regulated): AI in hiring, credit, healthcare, education, law enforcement — requires transparency, human oversight, audit
- Limited risk: disclosure requirements (you must know if you're talking to AI)
- Minimal risk: no requirements (spam filters, AI games, etc.)

**Assessment**: The EU AI Act is real regulation with real teeth — companies non-compliant face fines up to 7% of global revenue. Its limitations: it's primarily procedural (requires documentation and oversight) rather than outcome-based (requires good outcomes). Enforcing it across a complex tech supply chain is genuinely hard.

**United States**: A patchwork of executive orders, agency guidance, and state-level laws (Colorado, California leading). No comprehensive federal AI legislation has passed as of April 2026. The US approach has prioritized voluntary commitments from major AI companies — which have had mixed results.

**China**: Heavy government involvement in AI development, with specific restrictions on AI content that challenges CCP narratives, and requirements for algorithmic recommendation systems to serve "positive" values as defined by the government.

**The Gap**: The most consequential decisions — training data, safety testing, deployment contexts, content policies — remain largely unregulated in the US. This matters because US companies deploy AI globally, setting de facto global standards.

## 6. Social Fabric Stress

### The Connection Displacement Question

This one is genuinely uncertain and worth flagging as such. Preliminary evidence and strong theoretical grounds suggest:

- AI companions (chatbots designed for emotional connection) are being used by tens of millions of people daily, including many who are isolated, grieving, or disabled. The relationship between AI companionship and human social connection is not yet well understood — is it a bridge to human connection or a substitute for it?

- Students who heavily use AI for writing may not be developing the cognitive and expressive skills that come from struggling with writing. The "productive struggle" hypothesis in education suggests that AI-assisted homework completion may shortchange skill development.

- The availability of instant, competent AI answers may be reducing tolerance for the slower, more uncertain, but more rewarding process of human expert consultation.

None of this is proven at scale. But the direction of these social-technical interactions warrants attention and ongoing research.

### The Meaning-of-Work Question

Work is not only economic — it provides structure, social connection, identity, and meaning for most adults. The displacement of cognitive work raises questions that economics alone cannot answer: what happens to people who find meaning in work that AI can now do better? What new forms of contribution replace them?

These questions are not rhetorical. Communities built around specific industries (coal mining, auto manufacturing) have experienced severe social disruption when those industries shrank — not just income loss but community identity loss. Cognitive work is more geographically distributed, but the identity dimension is similarly real.

## Talking About Concerns Without Inducing Paralysis

For speakers: the goal is not to make people afraid. It's to make people informed enough to:
1. Make personal choices (privacy settings, AI tool selection, career planning)
2. Engage politically (support for AI regulation, worker protection, data rights)
3. Support the community around them (neighbors, colleagues, family navigating these changes)

The frame that lands best: "These are design and policy problems, not destiny." AI does not inherently displace workers, erode privacy, or concentrate power — those are choices being made right now, by specific actors, that can be made differently with different incentives and rules.
