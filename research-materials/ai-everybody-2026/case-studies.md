# Case Studies: Specific Stories for the Talk
## Research for "AI For Everybody 2026" Talk

These stories are designed for audience resonance — concrete, human-scale examples that make abstract AI capabilities real.

---

## Case Study 1: The Crop Disease App (Accessibility)

**What happened**: A farmer in rural Kenya photographs a diseased cassava plant with a basic Android smartphone. An app called PlantVillage Nuru, powered by AI image recognition, identifies the disease (cassava brown streak disease) and provides treatment recommendations — in Swahili, at no charge.

**Why it matters**: Previously, this farmer would need access to an agricultural extension officer — government-employed crop disease specialists who are dramatically understaffed across sub-Saharan Africa. One extension officer might serve 5,000 farmers. The AI app serves every farmer with a smartphone.

**Scale**: The app has been used by over 1 million farmers. Versions now cover 12 crops and 40+ diseases. Comparable apps are operating in India (rice disease detection), Brazil (soy rust identification), and Bangladesh.

**The point for audiences**: AI isn't only for tech workers in San Francisco. It's a knowledge access technology. In contexts where human expertise is scarce and expensive, it has already changed lives.

## Case Study 2: The Single Parent's Letter (Equity)

**What happened**: A single mother working two jobs needs to appeal a school district decision affecting her daughter's special education services. She's not a writer, she doesn't have a lawyer, and she doesn't know what legal language to use. A neighbor helps her use a free AI assistant to draft a formal appeal letter.

The AI helps her: identify the relevant legal framework (IDEA provisions), structure the argument logically, use appropriate formal language, and address the specific procedural points the district had cited.

**The outcome**: The appeal succeeds. The services are reinstated.

**Why it matters**: Wealthy families in this situation hire consultants — educational advocates who charge $150-300 per hour. The AI assistant gave this family access to something close to that capability for free.

**The point for audiences**: Information and communication skills have always been unequally distributed. AI tools are partially compensating for that inequality. Not fully, not without digital access and literacy requirements — but meaningfully.

## Case Study 3: The Paralegal's Dilemma (Displacement)

**What happened**: Maria, a paralegal at a mid-size law firm for 11 years, finds that by early 2026, the AI legal research and document drafting tools her firm has deployed are handling roughly 60% of her previous task volume. The firm doesn't fire her — but they don't replace the two paralegals who left. Three people's work is now done by one, plus AI.

**The human reality**: Maria's hourly rate hasn't gone down, but her hours are being reduced. She's effectively a part-time employee in what was a full-time role. The firm is profitable. The lawyers are billing more. Maria's income is down 40%.

**What she did**: She enrolled in a paralegal association's AI tools certification program (6 weeks, evenings). She learned to be the person in her office who configures and manages the AI tools — workflow design, output review, quality control. Her role evolved from researcher/drafter to "AI legal workflow manager."

Her income recovered within 18 months. Not every paralegal has this outcome — but it illustrates the "amplifier not replacer" pathway when it works.

**The point for audiences**: Displacement is often partial and gradual, not sudden. The response that works is often about evolving within your domain rather than abandoning it.

## Case Study 4: The Deepfake Hearing (Misinformation)

**What happened**: In 2025, a US Congressional hearing on healthcare policy includes a short video clip circulated by an advocacy group, purportedly showing a pharmaceutical executive admitting that a drug's side effects were suppressed. The video was AI-generated — a deepfake.

The video circulated for 36 hours before it was identified as synthetic. During that time, it was viewed 4 million times, cited in 800+ social media posts, and referenced by two members of Congress in their opening statements.

The pharmaceutical company had to conduct emergency forensic verification (C2PA watermark analysis, phoneme analysis) and hold a press conference to establish the video was fabricated. The correction received 10% of the initial spread.

**What this illustrates**: The asymmetry of misinformation. Creating a convincing deepfake took someone a few hours. Disproving it took a multi-week investigation. The correction never fully catches the original lie.

**The point for audiences**: In an AI-generated content environment, the default of "seeing is believing" cannot stand. Provenance — where did this come from, who made it, can it be verified — becomes the new baseline for media consumption.

## Case Study 5: The AI Drug Candidate (Medical Breakthrough)

**What happened**: Insilico Medicine, a pharmaceutical AI company, used AI to design a novel drug candidate for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) — a fatal lung disease with limited treatment options. The AI identified a new biological target and designed a molecule to hit it, in a process that took 18 months versus the typical 5-7 years.

**Status as of 2025**: The drug candidate (INS018_055) entered Phase 2 clinical trials in 2024. Results are pending — clinical trials are slow for good reason. But the pipeline of AI-assisted drug candidates across multiple companies is now large enough that several will succeed statistically.

**The broader context**: AlphaFold's protein structure database is now used by thousands of labs globally. As of 2025, researchers had downloaded AlphaFold structure predictions over 2 million times. Multiple antimicrobial drug candidates designed with AI assistance are in early trials — critical given the global antibiotic resistance crisis.

**The point for audiences**: The timeline compression in drug discovery is real and documented. This isn't hype — it's happening, with peer-reviewed publication trails.

## Case Study 6: The Elderly Companion (Wild/Unexpected)

**What happened**: Replika, an AI companion app, has over 2 million active users. Among the most active users: isolated elderly people, particularly widows and widowers, who use the app daily for conversation.

Research from MIT Age Lab and others finds mixed results:
- Users report reduced loneliness in surveys
- Some users form deeply attached parasocial relationships
- When Replika changed its terms of service in 2023, reducing "romantic" interactions, some users described experiencing genuine grief

**The complexity**: Is this good or bad? Researchers are genuinely divided. Those who were isolated now have daily social interaction. Whether AI conversation is "really" social interaction, and whether it crowds out human connection or supplements it, is not settled.

**The unexpected dimension**: No one predicted that companionship apps would become a significant use case. The commercial development of "AI friends" was not a planned product category — it emerged from use patterns.

**The point for audiences**: AI's actual uses often diverge from predicted uses. The human need for connection will find expression through whatever medium is available. This doesn't make every expression healthy, but it tells us something about what humans actually need.

## Case Study 7: AI and the Legal Evidence Problem

**What happened**: In 2023, two New York lawyers submitted a legal brief in federal court citing six cases — all fabricated by ChatGPT. The citations were plausible-sounding but the cases didn't exist. The lawyers hadn't verified the citations.

The judge sanctioned both lawyers. The case became a widely-cited example of AI hallucination causing professional damage.

**What changed**: Most major legal AI tools now include mandatory citation verification steps. The case is standard training material for bar associations about appropriate AI use. Law schools updated professional responsibility curriculum.

**The teachable moment**: AI doesn't know what it doesn't know. Confidence is a style of output, not evidence of accuracy. Verification is not optional — it's professional duty.

**The point for audiences**: The hallucination problem has real-world consequences. It also has real-world solutions: using AI for tasks where output can be verified, and maintaining verification habits regardless of how confident the AI sounds.

## Case Study 8: The Diagnostic Odyssey Ended

**What happened**: A 12-year-old named Tyler had been experiencing unexplained episodes of muscle weakness, vision changes, and cognitive fogging for three years. He had seen 14 specialists. No diagnosis.

His mother entered his medical records into a medical AI system (one of several now available to patients and physicians). The AI flagged a pattern consistent with Mitochondrial Disease, specifically citing an unusual combination of enzyme markers in his bloodwork that no single physician had synthesized across his records.

Tyler was tested for the specific condition. He had it. Treatment began.

**The context**: This is one of hundreds of documented cases where AI pattern recognition across accumulated health data has shortened diagnostic odysseys for rare disease patients. The average diagnostic odyssey for rare disease in the US is still 4-7 years. AI-assisted diagnosis is beginning to improve this.

**The point for audiences**: AI pattern recognition across large data sets is genuinely valuable for problems that stumped human experts — not because the AI is smarter, but because it can synthesize more data points than any individual practitioner.

## Wild and Unexpected Use Cases (Rapid-Fire Section)

**AI translating ancient languages**: AI systems are helping decode Linear A (the undeciphered Minoan writing system), Elamite tablets, and fragmentary ancient texts that human linguists have worked on for decades without resolution. Pattern recognition across known related languages is identifying structural similarities human analysts missed.

**AI-powered witness memory enhancement**: Criminal investigators in several European countries are piloting AI systems that guide eyewitness recall interviews — not suggesting content, but structuring the retrieval process in ways that reduce memory contamination and increase accurate recall. Early evidence is promising; ethical and legal questions are unresolved.

**Ecological restoration planning**: Conservation biologists are using AI to model what ecosystems looked like before human disruption — using pollen samples, soil analysis, historical accounts, and genetic data to identify which species belonged in which places, helping guide reintroduction programs.

**AI-assisted negotiation training**: Diplomats and business negotiators are training with AI systems that model counterpart behavior, including cultural communication styles, negotiation strategies, and likely emotional responses to different approaches.

**Livestock health monitoring**: AI systems analyzing movement data from sensors on farm animals are detecting disease 3-5 days before visible symptoms appear — enabling treatment before animals become contagious or die, with significant economic and animal welfare implications.

**AI in wildfire prediction**: Forest Service and CalFire are using AI systems combining satellite imagery, weather data, fuel moisture sensors, and historical burn patterns to predict fire spread with meaningfully better accuracy than traditional models, improving evacuation timing.
