How Indiana primaries work

Indiana primaries are open — you do not register by party. When you arrive at the polls, you declare which party's ballot you want. You can only vote in one party's primary. For most Indianapolis (Marion County) offices, the Democratic primary is effectively the general election because no Republican filed. Where a Republican primary is listed, it only matters for voters in competitive suburban districts (5th or 6th CD).

What's at Stake

Indianapolis in 2026: the issues driving this election

Two crises have converged in Indianapolis heading into this primary: a data center construction boom that residents didn't vote for, and a housing affordability collapse that has been building for a decade. Both have the same underlying driver — corporate power operating ahead of democratic accountability — and both are central to what separates the candidates in every race on this ballot.

The data center fight crystallized in 2026. Sabey Data Centers won a $4 billion, 130-acre approval in Decatur Township in March despite 1,400+ resident signatures opposing it. Metrobloks received approval for a $500 million data center in the historically Black Martindale-Brightwood neighborhood in April, with the City-County Council giving final approval the night before this primary. A third project — a $2 billion DC Blox campus on the east side — has a public hearing scheduled for May 14. In all three cases, developers used variance petitions to bypass full council review.

Meanwhile, the utility that powers Marion County, AES Indiana, is seeking a 13.5% rate increase and was just acquired by a BlackRock-led consortium. The timing of that acquisition — and one congressman's financial relationship with both AES and BlackRock — is the central corruption question of the 7th Congressional District primary.

The 7th CD covers most of Indianapolis and Marion County. It is a heavily Democratic district — winning the Democratic primary here is winning the seat. This race has drawn three serious challengers to 18-year incumbent André Carson, making it the most consequential race on the Indianapolis ballot.

Hatch pleaded guilty in October 2024 to felony official misconduct, misdemeanor disorderly conduct, and misdemeanor unlawful carrying of a handgun while serving as Center Township Constable. She was removed from office by law. For a voter prioritizing anti-corruption, this is an automatic disqualification regardless of her policy positions. She is not evaluated further below.

Central Accountability Issue: The AES/BlackRock PAC Timeline

On September 30, 2025, Carson accepted a $5,000 donation from the BlackRock PAC. On October 1, 2025 — the very next day — AES Corporation announced a $33 billion acquisition by a BlackRock-led consortium. Carson then accepted additional AES PAC money in December 2025 while the acquisition was in progress, and did not publicly comment on the deal until February 2026 — two months after it closed. Carson had already received donations from the AES Indiana PAC and Google PAC. When community members in Martindale-Brightwood needed their congressman to speak out against the Metrobloks data center (powered by AES Indiana), he was silent. His current support for a federal moratorium was adopted only after his challengers made it a campaign issue — a conversion without credibility given this financial record.

Carson's legislative effectiveness rating: 197th out of 220 House Democrats (Center for Effective Lawmaking, 118th Congress) — bottom 10%. His challengers argue this happened while Marion County developed some of the highest eviction rates in the nation and affordable housing shortfalls grew to over 100,000 units. The financial entanglement with AES and BlackRock is not a procedural technicality — it is a pattern of accepting corporate PAC money from entities directly affecting his constituents' utility bills and neighborhood development.

5th Congressional District

The 5th CD covers northern Indianapolis suburbs including Hamilton County (Carmel, Fishers, Noblesville). It leans Republican but has become competitive. Incumbent Victoria Spartz (R) has extensive accountability problems. The Democratic primary has one serious candidate.

Victoria Spartz (R, Incumbent) — Accountability Summary

Spartz has a documented pattern of misconduct: a 2024 House Ethics Committee inquiry into staff abuse and "general toxicity" (3.5x average staff turnover); a weapons charge for carrying a handgun through Dulles Airport; a STOCK Act violation for late-disclosed stock trades; and a December 2024 announcement she would boycott all committee assignments after being denied a preferred committee seat, leaving her constituents without representation. Most starkly, she voted against the $60 billion Ukraine aid package three days after a Russian missile strike killed 18 people in her hometown of Chernihiv — a vote against the safety of her own family's city for apparent political calculation. She voted YES on the "One Big Beautiful Bill" which cuts Medicaid and SNAP while adding $3.3 trillion to the deficit.

U.S. House • Indiana 6th Congressional District • Republican Primary

6th Congressional District

The 6th CD covers southern and southeastern Marion County plus rural southern Indiana. It is heavily Republican — the Republican primary is effectively the general election. Neither primary candidate aligns well with this voter's priorities, but the accountability issues with the incumbent are worth knowing.

Jefferson Shreve (R, Incumbent) — Stock Trading Accountability Issue

In the first four months of his congressional term, Shreve made 320+ stock transactions. Between April 7–17, 2025 — in the 10-day window following Trump's "Liberation Day" tariff announcement — he made 140+ trades worth $3.44–$9.45 million in companies directly overseen by his own Transportation and Infrastructure Committee: Uber (+34%), Schneider National (+16%), CSX (+9%), UPS, GE Aerospace, and others. He refused to identify his financial advisor or say whether he supports a stock trading ban. He stopped individual stock trading in June 2025 only after press exposure — not voluntarily. He voted YES on the "One Big Beautiful Bill" (Medicaid cuts, SNAP cuts, $3.3T deficit increase). On data centers and affordable housing, he has taken no documented position and his pro-business record makes opposition unlikely.

The Republican challenger, Sarah Janisse Brown, focuses on parental rights and children's health issues — not aligned with this voter's core priorities. If you are voting a Republican ballot for other reasons and are in the 6th CD, this race offers no good option. A protest vote or write-in may be appropriate.

Senate District 31 — Fishers, Northern Lawrence Township, Geist

SD-31 is an open seat (incumbent Walker not seeking re-election) that could flip Democratic — WFYI has characterized it as a possible flip district. The Democratic primary has four candidates. One dropped out in the final week under extraordinary circumstances.

Andrew Dezelan — Arrest Six Days Before Primary

On April 26, 2026 — six days before this primary — Dezelan was arrested while canvassing in Fishers. Police found a small plastic bag of suspected cocaine in his vehicle. He faces preliminary charges of cocaine possession and resisting law enforcement. He is remaining in the race. Indiana law does not disqualify candidates based on arrest alone, and charges are pending. However, for a voter prioritizing accountability and anti-corruption values, a drug possession arrest six days before the election is disqualifying regardless of the outcome of the charges.

Forestal's first-bill commitment — repealing Indiana's preemption statutes that strip cities of housing policy authority — is the most structurally significant housing policy proposal in any SD-31 candidate's platform. These state preemption laws are the primary reason Indianapolis cannot enact rent stabilization, eviction protections, or other tenant safeguards that cities in other states have adopted. Repealing them would unlock local government's ability to act on housing. That's more impactful than any individual program proposal.

Note on his sheriff record

Forestal's tenure as sheriff included real problems: jail overcrowding, 29 in-custody deaths between 2021–2024 (per federal lawsuits), and contested use-of-force incidents. These are serious accountability concerns for a law enforcement administrator. No personal corruption or ethical violations were found, and he opposes housing ICE detainees. Voters should weigh these institutional failures against his political platform. They represent management failures in a difficult role, not the kind of personal corruption or self-dealing that would make him unfit for state legislative office.

At least 264 data center-related bills were introduced statewide in 2026. Three counties have imposed local moratoriums. Indianapolis has not.

Google $1B / 468-acre Franklin Twp. project pulled before Council vote when 17+ councilors signaled opposition

A critical accountability pattern runs through all three approved projects: developers used variance petitions rather than rezoning applications, bypassing full City-County Council review. The Sabey project was in an already-industrially-zoned area; Metrobloks filed a variance; DC Blox filed a variance because no specific zoning classification exists for data centers. The draft zoning ordinance released in late April 2026 would create a new SU-47 special-use category requiring full public hearings for all new data centers — but critics (Citizens Action Coalition) say it still creates "a clear pathway for more data centers" rather than meaningful protection.

The NAACP reversal on Metrobloks is instructive: the Greater Indianapolis NAACP initially supported the project (February 9) then reversed course (March 9) after resident opposition mounted, calling it "environmental racism" — a rare public reversal that underscores how the project's approval against community opposition was not a close call on the merits.

Housing Affordability and Cost of Living

Indianapolis housing affordability has collapsed over the past decade by virtually every measure, even as the city has remained nominally "affordable" compared to coastal metros.

The structural barrier to local action is Indiana's preemption statutes — state laws that strip cities and towns of the ability to enact rent control, eviction protections, or other housing policies that cities in other states take for granted. This is why Kerry Forestal's first-bill commitment to repeal preemption laws is so significant: it's not a program, it's a key to the door.

Property tax reassessments in spring 2026 are adding immediate pressure. Marion County Assessor added nearly $5.5 billion in assessed value to commercial/industrial properties and nearly $2 billion to apartment complexes — some homeowners have seen their property taxes triple. The appeal deadline for 2025 assessed values is June 15, 2026. And AES Indiana's pending 13.5% rate increase by 2027 — on top of the 6% already approved — is directly linked to infrastructure investment driven by data center energy demand.

Methodology

How this guide was researched

This guide draws on a research corpus assembled between January and May 5, 2026. Sources include reporting from Mirror Indy, WFYI, WBAA, WTHR, Axios Indianapolis, the Indiana Capital Chronicle, and the Indiana Citizen; campaign finance records; the Center for Effective Lawmaking's ratings database; the Indiana Capital Chronicle's 2026 primary voter guide surveys; and secondary reporting from NOTUS, E&E News, Fortune, and the Washington Post on specific accountability issues.

Recommendations are explicitly based on four stated voter priorities: opposition to data center expansion in and around Indianapolis; access to affordable housing; cost-of-living concerns including utility bills; and anti-corruption values including financial accountability and political honesty. Candidates are not evaluated against a neutral ideological standard — this is a voter-priorities guide, not a general endorsement.

Campaign finance information was drawn from FEC disclosures and Indiana campaign finance reports available as of early May 2026. Accountability issues were verified against multiple source outlets before being included. Where a controversy could not be corroborated by at least two independent sources, it was not included.

This guide does not cover Indiana House of Representatives primaries in Marion County in detail — most Marion County districts are heavily Democratic with incumbents running unopposed in primaries. Notable contested races (HD-94, HD-95, HD-97) are noted but insufficient challenger information was available for meaningful recommendations. Voters should consult the WFYI voter guide for House races.
