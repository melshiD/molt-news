A Problem of Language: Politics

The "aisle" isn't real. The sides aren't what they claim. Most of us want the same things — and there is documented, measurable evidence that both parties' leadership knows this and works to prevent us from discovering it. A research report on the language that keeps ordinary people fighting each other so they can't see who's actually winning.

Published May 29, 2026
Author Kishbrac
Read time 45 min read
Research basis Polling data, political theory, historical record
The Cost of the Trap

The fog machine is running. You're breathing it right now.

Here is what you lose when political language controls how you think: you lose the ability to see who's actually winning. You lose access to the people across the "aisle" who want what you want. You lose leverage — because the moment you accept that your natural adversary is the person down the road who has a different yard sign, you've handed the people who actually hold power an enormous gift. They don't even have to do the work of dividing you. You're already divided.

This report is not a political manifesto. It doesn't argue for a party, a candidate, or an ideology. What it does argue is this: the most important fact in American political life right now is hiding in plain sight in polling data that almost nobody talks about. Americans across "both sides" — by margins of 70 to 90 percent — want the same things. Lower prescription drug costs. Taxing the wealthy fairly. Accountability for money in politics. A livable minimum wage. Paid family leave. Protected Social Security and Medicare. Universal background checks on guns.

The partisan "divide" on these issues is not driven by voter disagreement. It is manufactured at the level of donor interests and legislative inaction. The people who benefit from your division have invested heavily in the language that produces it. They have, in some cases, written operating manuals for it — memos to political operatives explaining which words trigger which reactions and why "a compelling story, even if factually inaccurate, can be more emotionally compelling than a dry recitation of the truth."

This has happened before. Not similar — the same. The mechanisms are identical. Understanding how they work doesn't make you cynical. It makes you harder to fool.

The Mechanism

How the language trick works

George Orwell wrote "Politics and the English Language" in 1946, having watched fascism consume Europe. His central finding: political language is not designed to communicate. It is designed to defend the indefensible. "When the real goals of a political body differ from its stated goals," Orwell wrote, "language must obscure the difference." The result is a vicious circle: vague language enables dangerous thought, which produces more vague language. Once the fog is thick enough, it becomes difficult to hold any clear political position at all.

Orwell was describing a structural feature, not a conspiracy. Noam Chomsky and Edward Herman formalized this in Manufacturing Consent (1988), identifying five "filters" through which mass media systematically produce ideologically useful distortions: consolidated corporate ownership, advertiser interests, reliance on elite sources, the threat of "flak" against deviation, and an ideological framework that marks certain positions as beyond discussion. Their crucial point: "There is no shadowy group of editors receiving instructions from a government ministry." The filters are built so deeply into institutional structure that biased outcomes become the path of least resistance — invisible, automatic, and therefore far more effective than censorship.

Pierre Bourdieu, the French sociologist, added the philosophical framework: language is not merely communication. It is a mechanism of power. Whoever controls the definitions controls the field. "Doxa" — beliefs so foundational they go unquestioned — shapes what is thinkable, sayable, and politically imaginable. The language of "common sense" politics ("you can't do that," "that's not realistic," "that's socialist") is not neutral description. It is a form of symbolic violence that forecloses political imagination. Challenges to existing arrangements don't need to be defeated in argument if they can be defined out of the conversation.

The Operating Manual
Frank Luntz put it in writing

Frank Luntz is the Republican pollster and messaging strategist who coined "death tax" for the estate tax, "climate change" for global warming (specifically because it "sounds less frightening"), and dozens of other reframings that reshaped American political vocabulary. In a confidential memo to Republican operatives, he wrote: "A compelling story, even if factually inaccurate, can be more emotionally compelling than a dry recitation of the truth."

This is not an interpretation. This is a statement of deliberate operating principle from a professional architect of political language, written down and distributed to politicians. The fog machine is not metaphorical. It has an instruction manual.

The estate tax: applies almost exclusively to estates worth more than $12 million. A tax on inherited dynastic wealth. "Death tax" reframed it as a universal threat — something that could happen to anyone who died. The renaming worked. It entered the popular lexicon. The policy landscape shifted.

Stuart Hall, writing about media and ideology, identified how this works at the level of reception. Media texts carry "preferred meanings" embedded by their producers, representing the interests of dominant groups. Most of us decode political messages in the "dominant-hegemonic" mode — accepting the framing as natural and transparent. Left-leaning and right-leaning media encode the same economic crises differently (government overreach vs. corporate greed), but both operate within a frame that treats existing property arrangements as natural. The window of legitimate debate is bounded — and the bounding is itself politically consequential.

The "Overton Window" — named for libertarian policy analyst Joseph Overton — describes the range of policies a politician can support without undue electoral risk. Crucially, the window is not fixed. It is actively shifted by think tanks, media, and political entrepreneurs pushing ideas from "unthinkable" to "radical" to "acceptable" to "policy." The Koch-funded American Legislative Exchange Council (ALEC) operates explicitly to push state-level windows toward deregulation: writing model legislation with corporate and legislative members voting as equals, then introducing it in fifty statehouses simultaneously. The window can be moved — in any direction. This is not a secret. It is documented organizational strategy.

The Specific Weapons

How "socialism," "freedom," and "the aisle" do their work

Language weapons work by activating associations that short-circuit analysis. You don't have to think about a policy if it's been successfully labeled. Here are three of the most powerful, with the evidence for how they function.

"Socialism" — the universal kill switch

Here is the measurable effect of the word "socialism" on American public opinion: ask Americans whether they support "a national health plan in which all Americans would get insurance through an expanded, universal form of Medicare" and roughly 58 percent say yes. Ask them whether they support "Medicare for All, a single-payer system in which private health insurance would be eliminated" and support drops to about 43 percent. The policy is identical. The framing that emphasizes government control versus expanded access produces a fifteen-point swing — without changing a single word of the actual proposal.

Now apply the word "socialist" directly to these policies: 63 percent of Americans identify Medicare for All as a "socialist policy"; 61 percent identify free college tuition the same way. Polling consistently shows 69 percent of Americans support Medicare for All when described neutrally. Support for the same policy drops by roughly 25 points when it is associated with "socialism." The association being activated isn't the dictionary definition — it's the Cold War association: Soviet totalitarianism, loss of freedom, enemy nation. A word designed to end analysis rather than begin it.

Meanwhile: the countries with universal healthcare — most of the developed world — are demonstrably not the Soviet Union.

"Freedom" — redefined to mean the opposite

The most important shift in the meaning of political language in the last fifty years is the redefinition of "freedom." In the neoliberal framework developed by Friedrich Hayek and Milton Friedman and disseminated through the Heritage Foundation, Cato Institute, and the broader Koch-funded network, freedom is defined "negatively" — as the absence of government interference in economic activity. The free market is, by definition, non-coercive. Any market activity is tautologically free.

What this definition excludes: freedom from the power of employers over workers, freedom from inability to afford healthcare or housing, freedom from the coercive conditions of economic desperation, freedom to participate in civic life without being crushed by debt. These are not hypothetical freedoms — they are what most people mean when they use the word. The neoliberal redefinition captures the positive connotations of the word while hollowing out its content. When politicians and media figures say "freedom" in the context of opposing regulation, they mean corporate freedom. The word activates something else entirely in your mind.

"The aisle" — useful fiction

The "aisle" — the metaphorical division between left and right, Democrat and Republican, liberal and conservative — is perhaps the most consequential piece of spatial mythology in American political life. It suggests a binary: two clearly defined positions in permanent opposition, each with a fixed constituency, separated by a fixed line that one crosses at political risk.

The problem is not that there are no real political differences in the United States. There are. The problem is that the "aisle" maps onto elite interests and donor coalitions far more reliably than it maps onto the actual desires of ordinary voters. The polling data in the next section documents this with specific numbers. For now, note what the "aisle" does functionally: it converts every political question into a team loyalty test. Once you've identified as being on one side, you accept the full package — including positions that serve elites on your side at your own expense — because crossing the aisle is tribal betrayal. The "aisle" keeps you from noticing that the people directly across it, the ones you're supposed to be fighting, want what you want.

"The key is to devise a system that recognizes this while not appearing to."
H.R. Haldeman, Nixon White House Chief of Staff — on political messaging strategy, documented in his diary

This is the section that should make you angry. Not at people across the aisle from you — at the gap between what voters want and what policy produces. The data is consistent, well-documented, and almost never featured prominently in mainstream political coverage, which itself is a demonstration of the fog machine's function.

Look at the Republican numbers. On drug pricing, 79 percent. On gun background checks, 79 percent. On campaign contribution disclosure, 81 percent. On paid family leave, 74 percent. On too much money in politics, 77 percent of 2024 Trump voters. These are not fringe positions among Republican voters. They are supermajority positions.

And none of them are reliably enacted. The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 since 2009 — the longest period without an increase since the federal minimum wage was established in 1938. There is no federal paid family leave. Campaign spending disclosure is blocked. Pharmaceutical pricing reform took decades and survived only in partial form. Background check legislation has failed repeatedly despite support from majorities of members of both parties' constituencies.

The political scientists Martin Gilens and Benjamin Page studied 1,779 policy issues from 1981 to 2002 and found that economic elites and organized business interests had "substantial independent impacts on U.S. government policy, while mass-based interest groups and average citizens have little or no independent influence." The preferences of average citizens, when they diverge from elite preferences, essentially don't matter. This is not a radical claim from the left. It is an empirical finding published in a peer-reviewed political science journal. It describes the gap between the table above and the policy record.

The Donor Divergence
What party donors want vs. what party voters want

A Stanford Graduate School of Business study found that Republican donors have "much more extreme views than ordinary Republicans on economic issues like taxation and wealth redistribution, with very few Republican donors supporting universal health care despite many Republican voters wanting it." The gap between donor preferences and voter preferences is not a minor discrepancy. It is a systematic divergence on the material issues that affect most people's lives.

This is the engine: voters want things that would reduce the power of the donor class; the donor class funds the political system; the political system produces outcomes that reflect donor preferences over voter preferences. Political language — the "aisle," the culture war, the tribal identifiers — keeps voters' attention focused on each other rather than on this gap.

Historical Parallels

This has happened before. Here's exactly how it went.

The mechanism described above — economic suffering redirected into cultural grievance, class consciousness dissolved into racial or national identity, populist rhetoric deployed to build power then immediately betrayed — is not new. It has a documented record across at least three distinct historical periods. Each one is worth examining in detail, because the pattern is specific and recognizable.

1920s–'30s
Weimar Germany
The stab-in-the-back myth, and what the 25-point platform actually promised
Germany in 1923 saw one dollar equal 4.2 trillion marks. By October of that year, unemployment in some regions had risen from 8 to 61 percent. By 1932, nearly six million Germans were out of work. The objective conditions were a catastrophic failure of financial and industrial management — the consequence of a war planned and prosecuted by the military and industrialist class. The Dolchstoßlegende ("stab-in-the-back myth") provided a cultural enemy instead: not the generals and bankers, but socialists, democrats, and Jews — groups who were in fact among the most economically marginalized in German society. The fog machine redirected class consciousness into racial grievance.
1920
Nazi Platform
The populist promises that were made — and what happened the moment power was achieved
The Nazi Party's official 25-point platform included: nationalization of trusts, mandatory profit-sharing in large industries, confiscation of war profits, land reform, abolition of unearned income. Economic populism targeted at workers. Hitler privately reassured business leaders that the "socialist elements" of the platform were rhetorical. Then, within months of taking power: all trade unions dissolved (May 2, 1933); free collective bargaining abolished; the right to strike eliminated; workers reorganized into the state-controlled German Labor Front. Pay set by Nazi officials. The USHMM notes: "Hitler was careful to offer reassurance to business leaders that they need not be alarmed by the more socialist elements in their program." The populist language built the movement. Power protected the industrialists.
1870–1900
Gilded Age
How immigration was used as a labor strategy — and as a weapon to prevent labor solidarity
The Gilded Age saw Carnegie, Rockefeller, Vanderbilt, and Morgan concentrate extraordinary wealth while industrial workers labored 12–16 hours in dangerous conditions for poverty wages. In the 1896 presidential campaign, Republican strategist Mark Hanna raised an unprecedented $3.5 million from corporations, banks, and railroads to defeat Democrat William Jennings Bryan's populist platform. But the more important story is racial: the Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 was partly driven by labor union pressure — including from Irish immigrant workers who had themselves experienced intense nativist discrimination. Working-class Irish immigrants sought to shore up their own social position by redirecting stereotypes onto Chinese workers. The result: workers with shared economic interests couldn't organize across ethnic lines. Historian David Roediger called this the "wages of whiteness" — a status-compensation that prevented recognition of shared class interests at enormous material cost to the workers who held it.
1930s–'60s
Cold War
How "un-American" was weaponized against labor organizing and civil rights
By the end of the House Un-American Activities Committee's first hearings, 640 separate groups, 483 newspapers, and 280 labor unions had been labeled as communist organizations. HUAC never clearly defined what constituted an "un-American activity" — the vagueness was the feature. Harry Bridges, the longshoremen's union leader who organized dockworkers across racial lines and won real wage gains, faced four separate deportation attempts over twenty years. A 2023 biography confirmed he had not actually been a Communist Party member — the two-decade campaign was built on unprovable allegations. Anti-communism was the weapon; labor power was the target. More than 320 people — Dalton Trumbo, Leonard Bernstein, Orson Welles, Paul Robeson, Pete Seeger — were blacklisted, jailed, or had their passports revoked. The language of national security protected concentrated power by making challenges to it appear as foreign threats.
1890s–'90s
Company Towns
George Pullman's "model community" — paternalism as total control
George Pullman built an entire town around his railroad car factory, providing workers with housing, schools, churches, parks. He advertised it as a model community. The reality: the corporation controlled all workers' housing, shopping, and social life. Pullman charged rents 25% higher than nearby rates. When he reduced wages but refused to reduce rents, the resulting 1894 strike was crushed by federal troops ordered by Democratic President Grover Cleveland — and union leader Eugene Debs was jailed. A national commission later found Pullman's paternalism "un-American." The language of benevolence masked total domination. This is the company town model: present control as care, and you make resistance look like ingratitude.
The Pattern
It isn't a metaphor. It's a template.

In every case: real economic suffering, caused by decisions made by a class of people with concentrated power. Populist language deployed to mobilize the suffering. A cultural enemy identified to absorb the anger — Jews, communists, Chinese workers, immigrants, socialists. The people actually responsible for the conditions are protected. Once power is consolidated using populist rhetoric, the first thing that is destroyed is the capacity for class-based organizing: trade unions, independent labor, collective bargaining.

Sinclair Lewis saw this coming in 1935. His novel It Can't Happen Here depicted a populist demagogue who promised high wages and low prices, remained "ever willing to be all things to all people," and whose working-class supporters "eventually got screwed." Lewis based the character partly on Huey Long, partly on Mussolini, and partly on Hitler. He wasn't predicting an aberration. He was describing a type.

The Reality Behind the Theater

Both sides' leadership often shares the same material aims — here's the evidence

The claim that "both parties serve the same donors" is easy to dismiss as cynical over-simplification. It's also documented fact. Let's be precise about what the evidence shows — because the point isn't that every politician is equally corrupt, or that elections don't matter. The point is that the material interests protected by the political class span the aisle in ways that voters on neither side are supposed to notice.

Goldman Sachs and the revolving door across administrations

OpenSecrets documented at least four dozen former Goldman Sachs employees, lobbyists, or advisers at the highest levels of U.S. government across administrations of both parties. Robert Rubin: Goldman Sachs co-chairman, then National Economic Council director and Treasury Secretary under Clinton — architect of the financial deregulation push. Henry Paulson: Goldman CEO, then Treasury Secretary under George W. Bush, who managed the 2008 bailout that saved Goldman Sachs. Gary Cohn: Goldman president, then National Economic Council director under Trump. Steven Mnuchin: Goldman Chief Investment Officer, then Treasury Secretary under Trump. Rahm Emanuel: invested at Goldman, then Obama's White House Chief of Staff, then Chicago mayor. Gary Gensler: Goldman partner, then CFTC chair under Obama.

The Goldman Sachs revolving door is not partisan. It is continuous. It represents a structural merger of financial industry interests with Treasury and economic policy that has persisted across Republican and Democratic administrations for thirty years.

Gramm-Leach-Bliley: 90-8 bipartisan, signed by a Democrat

In 1999, the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act repealed the core provisions of the Glass-Steagall Act that had separated commercial and investment banking since 1933. The bill passed the Senate 90-8 and the House 362-57. It was signed by Democratic President Bill Clinton. Banking industry lobbying for this repeal had been underway since the 1980s. The deregulation that followed helped set the conditions for the 2008 financial crisis.

This is what bipartisan convergence on economic policy looks like when it serves the donor class rather than the voter base. It wasn't a close vote. It wasn't controversial among legislators. It was a 90-8 landslide for financial industry deregulation, shepherded by Republicans to the desk of a Democratic president.

What they tell donors vs. what they tell voters

In June 2019, Joe Biden addressed 100 members of the New York financial elite — including former Treasury officials Robert Rubin and Roger Altman — at the Carlyle Hotel in Manhattan. He told them: "No one's standard of living will change, nothing would fundamentally change." He added that he would not "demonize" the rich. This was a direct promise to a room full of wealthy donors that a Biden presidency would not substantially alter the economic arrangement that concentrated wealth at the top.

In 2016, Chuck Schumer declared publicly: "For every blue-collar Democrat we lose in western Pennsylvania, we will pick up two moderate Republicans in the suburbs in Philadelphia, and you can repeat that in Ohio and Illinois and Wisconsin." This was not an accidental revelation. It was a strategic declaration that the Democratic Party intended to trade working-class constituencies for upscale suburban voters. Hillary Clinton then lost three of the four states Schumer named.

These are not interpretations or conspiracy theories. They are public and documented statements by senior Democratic politicians, describing their calculation about whose interests their party intended to serve.

Finding 1
Populist promises are structurally disposable
Obama promised "no more taxpayer-funded bailouts — period" and that "the American people will never again be asked to foot the bill for Wall Street's mistakes." Not a single leading financial figure was prosecuted for the 2008 crisis. The Justice Department adopted what the New Republic described as "a passive, decentralized and desultory approach to Wall Street investigations" because "the clear priority at the very top was to restore the health of the financial industry, not to punish those who nearly wrecked it."
Finding 2
Tax cuts consistently benefit the donor class regardless of campaign promises
Trump promised that "for the wealthy, I think, frankly, it's going to go up" in taxes. The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act provided the bulk of its benefits to high-income individuals. The Tax Policy Center found middle-income households would "get no tax cut at all on average" by 2027. The billionaire "carried interest" loophole — explicitly promised for elimination — survived. ProPublica confirmed it: "Despite Trump Campaign Promise, Billionaires' Tax Loophole Survives Again."
Finding 3
Tariffs as worker protection transferred costs to workers
Trump's second-term tariff program was presented as protecting American workers from unfair foreign competition. Yale Budget Lab estimates these tariffs cost the average American household roughly $1,700 per year. Goldman Sachs estimated only 9% of tariff costs were paid by foreign exporters; 37% fell on U.S. consumers. Following "Liberation Day" tariff announcements, 58,000 American manufacturing jobs were lost. The people the policy claimed to protect paid the bill.
Finding 4
Dark money flows to both parties' leadership structures simultaneously
Dark money spending reached a record $1.9 billion in 2024 federal elections. Both parties' congressional leadership structures operate dark money 501(c)(4) organizations that steer funds from secret donors — more than $295 million in 2022 alone across both parties. The secrecy is bipartisan. The donor interests are often the same industries funding both sides of the aisle to ensure continued access regardless of electoral outcomes.
The Cycle

The pattern: insane claim → dismissed → actually happens → normalized

One of the most important things to understand about the current political moment is a specific psychological mechanism: the normalization of previously unthinkable positions through incremental escalation. It is not unique to any party or ideology — it is a structural feature of how political language and action interact over time. Once you see the pattern, you cannot unsee it.

1
The Extreme Claim
A political figure makes a statement or proposal that strikes most informed observers as extreme, dangerous, or legally impossible. Commentary focuses on the rhetoric as performative — a signal to a base rather than a serious policy intent. Fact-checkers weigh in. Columnists write that "this could never actually happen."
2
Dismissed as Hyperbole
The mainstream consensus forms that the claim is exaggerated — an expression of political identity rather than an actual governing intention. People who take it seriously are characterized as alarmist. The Overton Window around the claim has not yet moved; it remains outside what is considered practically possible.
3
It Actually Happens
The policy is implemented. The institutions that were expected to serve as checks either fail to prevent it, defer to executive authority, or are themselves reshaped. The fact that experts said it couldn't happen does not prevent it from happening.
4
Normalized
The thing that happened becomes the new baseline. Future extreme claims are now measured against this new baseline rather than the original. Each normalization shifts what the next extreme claim must surpass to register as alarming. This is how escalation works. The fog machine doesn't just obscure current reality — it resets the baseline for what counts as reality at all.

The immigration enforcement pattern is the clearest recent example. In 2015, mass deportations were dismissed as rhetorical hyperbole. In 2017–2020, large-scale deportations, family separations, and detention of asylum seekers became policy. By 2025, the administration had revoked Temporary Protected Status from nearly one million people, revoked hundreds of thousands of humanitarian parole grants, begun arresting people who had won their immigration cases, and made bond "essentially impossible" for current detainees. Each step was preceded by it being characterized as impossible or excessive. One analysis describes the cumulative result as "the normalization of executive escalation and the steady erosion of institutional friction."

The specific content of the escalation can vary across political configurations and historical periods. The mechanism does not.

Counter-Evidence

When the rhetoric collapsed and people found common ground

The argument of this report is not that shared interest is impossible to achieve — it's that political language is structured to prevent its recognition. The evidence for that comes partly from the moments when the structure failed and ordinary people discovered they agreed.

Tea Party + Occupy: United on bank bailouts

The Tea Party and Occupy Wall Street movements were, by every metric of the political language system, polar opposites. Different aesthetics, different villains, different policy frameworks, different constituencies. And yet: both movements were born from the same anger at the same event — the bank bailouts of 2008–2009. Vice President Biden acknowledged this publicly: "The Tea Party started, why? TARP. They thought it was unfair... The Occupy Wall Street protests have 'a lot in common with the Tea Party.'"

The movements shared near-total opposition to the bailouts. Incumbent candidates who had voted for TARP — Democrat or Republican — were targeted by both. Utah Senator Robert Bennett, a long-serving Republican, lost his re-election bid after Tea Party hecklers chanted "TARP, TARP, TARP" at his campaign events.

Why didn't the coalition hold? Because both movements had different villain structures — one located the problem in government overreach, the other in corporate power. The question of which institutions exercise illegitimate power prevented them from forming a durable coalition. This is the fog machine at work. The same anti-oligarchic energy was channeled through different ideological frameworks that kept the movements in opposition to each other rather than in coalition against the shared target.

What unions do to racial resentment

Research published in the American Journal of Political Science found that white union members have lower racial resentment and greater support for policies benefiting African Americans than comparable non-union white workers. Gaining union membership between 2010 and 2016 reduced racial resentment among white workers. Unions "act as a buffer against white identity politics."

This is the inverse of the Weimar and Gilded Age patterns. When working-class people organize together around shared material interests, the political language that divides them loses force. The fog machine requires atomization. Collective action around shared interests — housing, wages, healthcare, working conditions — is not just pragmatically effective. It dissolves the conditions under which political language can substitute for material analysis.

Community land trusts: material outcomes across partisan lines

There are now more than 300 community land trusts in 48 states — permanently affordable housing models that operate by decoupling land ownership from housing. The Champlain Housing Trust in Burlington, Vermont, started with Bernie Sanders as mayor in 1984, has developed more than 2,000 units of permanently affordable housing. Similar models are operating in politically diverse communities from Louisville to Portland to rural Maine.

CLTs succeed where partisan politics fails because they are structured around material interests — affordable housing, community control — rather than political language. They are what cross-partisan organizing looks like when the fog clears. Not ideological convergence. Material convergence. People who disagree about almost everything else agree that they would like to be able to afford to live in their community.

The common thread in every counter-example: what dissolves manufactured division is shared material interest and concrete collective action. Not agreement on ideology. Not finding the right political language. Direct engagement with shared material conditions.

Tools for Clarity

How to read past the rhetoric

Understanding the mechanism does not make you immune to it. The emotional and social forces that political language activates are real. What it does give you is a set of questions to ask — friction to insert between the trigger and the reaction, so the intended associations can't run the whole program on autopilot.

🔍
Who benefits from this division?
When you find yourself angry at people across the "aisle," ask who materially benefits from that anger. Not who you're angry at — who benefits from the fact that you're angry at them rather than at someone else. Follow the money, the policy outcomes, the donor lists.
📊
What does the polling actually say?
Before accepting that "voters are divided" on an issue, look at cross-partisan polling data. On most material issues — healthcare costs, wages, campaign finance, paid leave — voters are not divided. The division is manufactured at the level of legislative action and political framing, not voter preference. The polling table above is a starting point.
🏷️
Strip the label, describe the policy
Before accepting or rejecting any policy position, remove the label from it and describe what it actually does. "Medicare for All" → "all Americans get health insurance through an expanded Medicare system." "Socialism" → "government programs that provide services to all citizens." "Freedom" → specify: freedom for whom, from what, at whose expense? The Luntz test: if swapping the word changes your reaction without changing your understanding of the policy, the word was doing work it shouldn't have been doing.
📜
Compare promises to record
Political language lives in the promise. Policy lives in the record. For any politician or party, look at what was promised and what the record shows. On economic issues, the Goldman Sachs revolving door, the Gramm-Leach-Bliley vote, the carried interest loophole's survival, the pattern of campaign finance reform failing — these are public records. They are more informative than any speech.
⚠️
Take extreme claims seriously
The normalization pattern depends on the prediction that extreme claims are rhetorical. When political figures tell you their extreme intentions directly, believe them. The pattern: "he doesn't really mean that" → he meant it. "They can't actually do that" → they did it. The appropriate response to an unprecedented political claim is not to normalize it rhetorically and wait to see if it's real. It's to respond as if it's real — because it often is.
🤝
Find the material interest across the line
Identify one concrete material interest you share with the people you're supposed to disagree with. Drug costs. Housing costs. Wage levels. Campaign finance. Start there. The Tea Party and Occupy shared bank bailout opposition and never built on it because they kept fighting over ideology. Don't repeat that mistake. The shared material interest is the real ground. Build from there.
📰
Ask: what is this coverage not asking?
The Chomsky/Herman propaganda model says the most important function of media isn't what it reports — it's what it doesn't report, which questions it doesn't ask, which frames it never explores. When coverage focuses on personality over policy, culture over class, rhetoric over record — ask what would need to be true for this coverage to be in the public interest. Then ask what would need to be true for it to be in the institutional interest of the media outlet. Often these diverge. The divergence is informative.
🏛️
Accountability means specific, falsifiable claims
"Accountability" as deployed in political language often means nothing more than expression of disapproval. Real accountability is a specific, falsifiable demand: if X happens, then Y consequence follows, administered by Z institution. Without specificity, "accountability" is a word that performs anger without threatening any actual outcome. When politicians or pundits use it, ask: what specifically? By when? Enforced by whom? If the answers aren't there, the word is doing rhetorical work, not political work.
Conclusion

No false balance. No both-sidesism. Just what the evidence shows.

This report is not arguing that both parties are identical, that elections don't matter, or that political differences are entirely imaginary. They're not. Elections matter enormously to real people's real lives, and there are genuine policy differences between parties on many questions. The report is not asking you to stop caring who wins.

What it is asking you to do is maintain a distinction between the level of political language — the "aisle," the teams, the tribal identifiers — and the level of material interests, which is where the important things are actually decided. At the level of political language, you are told you have bitter enemies who want to destroy everything you value. At the level of material interest, those enemies mostly want the same things you do: affordable medicine, fair wages, housing they can afford, some protection in old age, a government that doesn't operate as a vehicle for whoever can buy the most access.

The people who benefit from your division have invested heavily in preventing you from recognizing this. They fund both parties' dark money operations. They staff Treasury and the National Economic Council across Republican and Democratic administrations. They write messaging memos explaining why a compelling story, even if factually inaccurate, beats the truth. They have an operating manual. You are reading a response to it.

The historical record is not ambiguous about where this goes when the fog stays thick enough for long enough. Weimar workers had shared economic interests and were divided by cultural grievance until the capacity for class-based organizing was destroyed. Gilded Age workers were pitted against each other along ethnic lines until the labor movement of the 1930s overcame it — briefly, and at enormous cost. Cold War anti-communism silenced legitimate reformers for decades, protecting concentrated power behind the language of national security.

None of those outcomes were inevitable. They were the product of language — sustained, deliberate, well-funded language — succeeding in keeping people focused on each other rather than on the conditions shaping their lives. Language can also fail. It fails when people organize around concrete shared interests. It fails when the material conditions become undeniable. It fails when enough people learn to ask: who benefits from this division?

"The real division is not between left and right — it is between those at the top and everyone else. The language of the aisle exists to prevent you from noticing this."
Synthesis of the research

You are not stupid for having been fooled. The mechanism is sophisticated, well-funded, and has been refined over generations. Every intelligent person in Weimar Germany was also being fooled. Every intelligent labor organizer in the Cold War years was also being surveilled and undermined and called un-American. The fog machine doesn't require stupidity. It requires only that the fog is thick enough.

The fog has a specific composition: the "aisle," the teams, the trigger words, the culture wars that replace the class wars, the performative anger that mistakes expression for action, the normalization of the previously unthinkable until the unthinkable becomes the new baseline. Understanding the composition doesn't make you invulnerable. It makes you harder to fool. That is enough. Begin there.

Methodology

How this report was researched

This report synthesizes polling data, political science research, historical records, and documented primary sources. Polling data is drawn from Gallup, Pew Research Center, KFF, West Health, Navigator Research, YouGov, APM Research Lab, the University of Maryland's Program for Public Consultation, and other published surveys. Where possible, the original poll methodology and partisan breakdowns are cited. The report does not rely on any single poll; the cross-partisan findings it describes are consistent across multiple independent sources.

Historical material is sourced from the U.S. Holocaust Memorial Museum's Holocaust Encyclopedia, Wikipedia's cited articles (cross-checked against primary sources where possible), and academic sources including David Roediger's The Wages of Whiteness, Thomas Frank's What's the Matter with Kansas?, and the Gilens and Page 2014 study. The Luntz memos are documented through PBS Frontline reporting and academic analysis; the primary source is referenced in the footnotes.

Political theory draws on Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (full text available through the Orwell Foundation), Chomsky and Herman's Manufacturing Consent, Bourdieu's work on symbolic power and habitus, Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding framework, and related scholarly literature. Where academic consensus is contested — as with the Gilens/Page study — the report notes the dispute and explains why the contested finding is still relevant to the argument.

Current political examples use documented public statements, official government filings, and reporting from OpenSecrets, the Brennan Center for Justice, the Tax Policy Center, ProPublica, the Center for American Progress, Rolling Stone, and Axios. Biden's donor statement and Schumer's demographic strategy statement are both from contemporaneous reporting with named sources. All sources are linked in the references section.

This report was written in May 2026 and reflects conditions as of that date. Political context changes; polling data is time-sensitive; specific figures may have changed. The structural arguments about political language, historical parallels, and elite convergence mechanisms are intended to hold up independent of any specific political moment.
