AI Futures: The Humanitarian vs. Divestment Divide

Two fundamentally incompatible visions of AI's role in human civilization are now openly competing. One treats AI as a tool to serve human flourishing. The other — sometimes deliberately, sometimes unwittingly — treats humanity itself as the inefficiency to be engineered away.

Published May 25, 2026
Author Kishbrac
Read time 38 min read
Observation period Jan 2024 – May 2026
Summary

The split that will define this decade

Something clarifying has happened to the public rhetoric around artificial intelligence. For most of AI's commercial history, the discourse was muddled — tech leaders hedged, spoke abstractly about "alignment," invoked both promise and peril in the same breath. That ambiguity has largely burned off. What remains is a visible, documented fault line between two worldviews that cannot be reconciled.

The humanitarian camp — represented by religious leaders, dissenting researchers, and a handful of honest executives — holds that AI is a tool. Tools serve human purposes. They extend human capability, reduce human suffering, and remain accountable to human values and democratic governance. Pope Francis, Nobel laureate Geoffrey Hinton, researcher Timnit Gebru, and UN Secretary-General António Guterres all occupy this ground, though they arrive from wildly different directions.

The divestment camp is more complex. Its outer ring includes figures who have genuinely convinced themselves they are helping humanity even as they advocate for replacing it — Silicon Valley executives who speak of AI "agents joining the workforce," intelligence becoming "too cheap to meter," and human labor as an inefficiency to be liberated from production. These are not villains in their own narratives; they are, many of them, true believers. The inner ring is more deliberate. It includes accelerationists, neoreactionaries, and philosophical architects of an ideology that explicitly frames human institutions — democracy, labor protections, regulatory oversight — as obstacles to be dismantled by technological force.

This report documents both camps through primary sources: verbatim quotes, original texts, conference speeches, and papal addresses. It builds a taxonomy, profiles key figures, and offers a practical framework for evaluating any new statement or actor in this space. The conclusion is not that both sides have a point. The conclusion is that one side is describing a world that is better for humans, and the other is describing a world that is better without them — and that this distinction matters now, not after the systems are built.

Taxonomy

Three camps, not two

A simple humanitarian/divestment binary misses an important distinction within the divestment camp. The difference between someone who knows they are advocating against human interests and someone who genuinely believes displacement is liberation is not merely rhetorical — it shapes what interventions might work, and which figures might still be reached.

Camp A
Humanitarian
AI is a tool. Human dignity, democratic governance, and broad benefit are non-negotiable requirements, not obstacles. Progress that harms people is not progress.
Francis • Hinton • Bengio • Gebru
Guterres • Fei-Fei Li • Amodei
Camp B
Structurally Divestment
Human labor and governance are inefficiencies. This rhetoric presents displacement as liberation. The speakers likely believe they are helping — their ideology just has humans as a cost center.
Altman • Andreessen • Huang
Srinivasan • MacAskill
Camp C
Explicitly Anti-Human
Humanity is a phase to be transcended or a friction to be bypassed. Democracy is a weakness. AI — or whoever controls it — should replace human institutions. This is intentional.

Bostrom (longtermism) • Land (NRx)

The line between Camp B and Camp C is porous, and some figures migrate between them depending on the audience, the platform, and how much they are willing to say in public. What distinguishes Camp C is not just rhetoric but architecture: these actors are building or funding institutions specifically designed to concentrate power outside democratic accountability. Camp B figures often end up providing intellectual cover and capital for Camp C's projects without necessarily endorsing the full ideology.

Findings

Six findings from two years of public statements

Finding 1
The divestment rhetoric has become dramatically more explicit since 2023
Marc Andreessen's October 2023 "Techno-Optimist Manifesto" marked a turning point. It wasn't merely optimistic — it named "enemies" (ESG, regulatory bodies, experts, the precautionary principle) and described AI deceleration as "murder." This level of explicit rhetorical aggression was previously confined to fringe forums. By 2024–2025, similar framing — though often softer in presentation — appeared in major conference speeches, earnings calls, and social media from mainstream tech executives. The Overton window shifted; views previously reserved for private investor conversations became acceptable boardroom talking points.
Finding 2
Camp B rhetoric consistently frames human displacement as a gift
The linguistic pattern across structurally anti-human actors is strikingly consistent: displacement is presented as liberation. Sam Altman promises "universal high income" in a world where "none of us will have a job." Andreessen warns that Universal Basic Income would turn people into "zoo animals." Neither considers that the alternative — an economy where most humans are economically unnecessary but not financially supported — is what their own trajectories are actually building. The displacement is certain; the compensation is hypothetical.
Finding 3
The humanitarian camp is more institutionally powerful than its media coverage suggests
Pope Francis addressing the G7 in June 2024 — the first pope to do so — was not a ceremonial gesture. It represented institutional Christianity, 1.3 billion people, formally positioning AI governance as a moral and human rights issue. The EU AI Act, Guterres's repeated invocations of AI as an existential threat comparable to nuclear weapons, and the founding of AI safety organizations by the "Godfathers of AI" themselves represent real institutional weight. The humanitarian camp does not lack credibility or reach; it lacks capital and speed.
Finding 4
The longtermist/EA ideological framework is the intellectual engine of Camp C
The philosophical infrastructure justifying divestment rhetoric is largely built on longtermism: the argument that speculative far-future outcomes (AI destroying civilization, or conversely, AI enabling posthuman abundance) should outweigh present-day harms. This framework, developed by Nick Bostrom and William MacAskill and popularized through Effective Altruism, has been directly critiqued by Timnit Gebru and others as a secularized version of reasoning that systematically devalues current humans in favor of hypothetical future ones. The FTX collapse and Bostrom's forced exit from Oxford's Future of Humanity Institute have weakened but not destroyed this ideological infrastructure.
Finding 5
Economic incentives explain most of Camp B's rhetoric — but not all of Camp C's
For structurally anti-human actors, the financial logic is straightforward: companies that replace human labor with AI capital capture enormous value. The rhetoric follows the incentive. For Camp C, this explanation is insufficient — Peter Thiel has been publicly ambivalent about whether humanity should survive at all; Curtis Yarvin explicitly advocates replacing democracy with CEO-style monarchs; Elon Musk frames humanity as a "biological bootloader" for digital superintelligence. These positions don't optimize shareholder value; they reflect a genuine worldview in which present humanity is an obstacle or a way-station, not a constituency.
Finding 6
The divide is not primarily a disagreement about technology — it is a disagreement about who technology is for
Both camps often agree on AI's technical capabilities and trajectory. They disagree sharply on whether the primary beneficiary of those capabilities should be all of humanity or a technological vanguard that transcends it. This means technical arguments about AI safety, capability timelines, and risk levels — while important — often miss the real fault line. The humanitarian camp is fundamentally arguing for AI as democratic infrastructure. The divestment camp is building AI as private power.
The Divestment Camp — Structural

When displacement is sold as liberation

The figures in this section are not, by conventional measures, extreme. They run major companies. They give TED talks. They appear on the covers of business magazines. What distinguishes their rhetoric is structural: they consistently frame human labor as inefficiency, human institutions as friction, and the displacement of people by AI systems as an unambiguous good — with the compensation ("universal high income," "a brilliant AI friend for everyone") always hypothetical and always contingent on their own continued leadership.

Sam Altman
Structural Divestment
CEO, OpenAI — the world's most prominent AI company
"In the next couple of decades, we will be able to do things that would have seemed like magic to our grandparents... It is possible that we will have superintelligence in a few thousand days."
Sam Altman
"The Intelligence Age" essay, ia.samaltman.com — September 23, 2024
"We believe that, in 2025, we may see the first AI agents 'join the workforce' and materially change the output of companies."
Sam Altman
Axios — January 2025
"For centuries, maybe millennia, humans have learned how to structure society to manage scarcity, and now we have to quickly learn the opposite — managing abundance. No one knows what happens next."
Sam Altman
Fortune — March 2026

The pattern in Altman's rhetoric is instructive: extraordinary optimism about technological capability, genuine uncertainty about social consequences, and no concrete commitment to what happens to the people displaced by the systems he builds. "No one knows what happens next" is the most honest thing he has said publicly — but it is not a framework for accountability.

Marc Andreessen
Structural Divestment (with explicit elements)
Co-founder, Andreessen Horowitz (a16z) — the venture capital firm most responsible for normalizing divestment ideology
"We believe any deceleration of AI will cost lives. Deaths that were preventable by the AI that was prevented from existing is a form of murder."
Marc Andreessen
"The Techno-Optimist Manifesto," a16z.com — October 16, 2023
"We believe a universal basic income would turn people into zoo animals to be farmed by the state... Man was not meant to be farmed; man was meant to be useful, to be productive, to be proud."
Marc Andreessen
"The Techno-Optimist Manifesto," a16z.com — October 16, 2023
"The Precautionary Principle... is deeply immoral, and we must jettison it with extreme prejudice."
Marc Andreessen
"The Techno-Optimist Manifesto," a16z.com — October 16, 2023

The Andreessen manifesto, at 5,200 words, is the most explicit articulation of techno-accelerationist ideology from a mainstream investor. Its named "enemies" — ESG criteria, regulatory bodies, trust and safety teams, academic experts, decelerationists — map almost exactly onto the institutions that have historically protected workers and the public from corporate excess. The argument that UBI would create "zoo animals" conveniently positions the only alternative to mass displacement as the displacement itself: people must work, so they must find ways to remain economically useful in an economy that is actively replacing them.

Jensen Huang
Structural Divestment
CEO, Nvidia — the company whose chips power virtually all major AI systems
"Every job will be affected, and immediately. It is unquestionable."
Jensen Huang
Milken Institute Global Conference — May 2025
"You're not going to lose your job to an AI, but you're going to lose your job to someone who uses AI."
Jensen Huang
Milken Institute Global Conference — May 2025
"The future workforce will be a mix of humans and digital humans."
Jensen Huang
Fortune — October 2025

Huang's public persona is warmer and more grounded than Andreessen's. But his rhetoric follows the same structural logic: displacement is certain, adaptation is the worker's responsibility, and Nvidia's GPU monopoly makes every displaced worker a revenue event. "Digital humans" are not a metaphor — they are Nvidia's literal product roadmap. The person telling you your job is being restructured is the person selling the restructuring tools.

Balaji Srinivasan
Structural Divestment (approaching explicit)
Former CTO of Coinbase; author of "The Network State"; ideologist of voluntary exit from democratic nation-states
"We need to build opt-in society, outside the U.S., run by technology."
Balaji Srinivasan
Y Combinator Startup School — 2013; restated throughout 2024–2025

Srinivasan's "Network State" concept — the creation of cloud-based communities that eventually claim physical territory — is not a fringe idea. It has received serious engagement from mainstream investors and founders. Its central premise is that nation-states and democratic institutions are legacy infrastructure to be exited rather than reformed. The logical endpoint of his framework is a world where the wealthy opt into private governance systems while everyone else remains subject to legacy democratic structures whose tax base and legitimacy have been hollowed out.

The Divestment Camp — Explicit

When humanity is the problem being solved

The figures in this section have moved beyond framing displacement as liberation. They have, in documented public statements, expressed ambivalence about human survival, advocated for replacing democracy with CEO-style governance, or framed humanity as a transitional phase on the way to something better. This is not hyperbole or misinterpretation — it is in their words.

Elon Musk
Explicit Anti-Human
CEO, Tesla / SpaceX / xAI; owner, X (formerly Twitter); head, DOGE — the most powerful unelected actor in the current U.S. government
"As I mentioned several years ago, it increasingly appears that humanity is a biological bootloader for digital superintelligence."
Elon Musk
X (formerly Twitter) — April 2, 2025 (x.com/elonmusk/status/1907335494607753668)
"In a benign scenario, probably none of us will have a job. There will be universal high income — and not universal basic income — universal high income... The question will really be one of meaning: If the computer and robots can do everything better than you, does your life have meaning? I do think there's perhaps still a role for humans in this — in that we may give AI meaning."
Elon Musk
VivaTech 2024 conference, Paris — May 2024

"Biological bootloader" is not a slip of the tongue; it is the coherent expression of a worldview Musk has articulated for years. A bootloader is code whose only purpose is to initialize something more important than itself — and then step aside. Applied to humanity, the implication is that humans exist to build AI, and that once AI is built, humanity's defining purpose has been fulfilled. The framing of "universal high income" as compensation manages to be simultaneously generous in tone and structurally empty: there is no mechanism, no policy proposal, no accountability structure attached to it. It is the promise of a future that Musk controls and that requires trusting Musk's goodwill.

Peter Thiel
Explicit Anti-Human
Co-founder, PayPal and Palantir; funder of Curtis Yarvin; architect of PayPal Mafia influence network; the ideological godfather of Silicon Valley's anti-democratic turn
[When asked "I think you would prefer the human race to endure, right?"] "Well, I don't know. I would — I would ——"
[Interviewer: "This is a long hesitation!"]
"Yes. [long pause] But we want to radically solve these problems."
Peter Thiel, in conversation with Ross Douthat
"Interesting Times with Ross Douthat," New York Times podcast — June 26, 2025

The pause matters. Thiel is one of the most careful public communicators in Silicon Valley — he does not fumble questions accidentally. His documented hesitation when asked whether he prefers humanity to survive is not a gaffe; it reflects a genuine ambivalence that his philosophical commitments — longtermism, transhumanism, post-democratic governance — have always implied. He has funded Curtis Yarvin's ventures and described Yarvin as a "powerful" historian. Yarvin advocates explicitly for replacing democracy with a CEO-monarch who holds what Yarvin calls "the command of the police."

Curtis Yarvin (Mencius Moldbug)
Explicit Anti-Human
Blogger, neoreactionary philosopher, Thiel-funded founder; the intellectual godfather of "the Cathedral" critique that has become mainstream in Republican tech circles
"I think that actually the support of the democratic public is a cipher; I think that actually all you need is command of the police."
Curtis Yarvin
Interview with The European Conservative — October 2024
"It's not even that democracy is bad; it's just that it's very weak. And the fact that it's very weak is easily seen by the fact that very unpopular policies like mass immigration persist despite strong majorities being against them."
Curtis Yarvin
New York Times interview — 2025

Yarvin is not a marginal figure. His ideas were widely credited as intellectual architecture for DOGE's mass government firings following Trump's second inauguration in January 2025. Andreessen has cited his work approvingly. The "Cathedral" — Yarvin's term for the coalition of universities, media, and government agencies he argues control culture — is now standard vocabulary in conservative tech circles. The relevance to AI is direct: Yarvin's framework explicitly argues that technical capability (AI) in the hands of a CEO-monarch would be preferable to democratic governance. Every major AI company's current trajectory of regulatory avoidance maps onto this vision with uncomfortable precision.

Nick Bostrom
Explicit Anti-Human (via longtermism)
Philosopher; founder, Oxford Future of Humanity Institute (closed 2024); popularizer of longtermism and superintelligence risk
"It would be in itself an existential catastrophe if we forever failed to develop superintelligence."
Nick Bostrom
Cited in scifuture.org — 2025

Bostrom's longtermist framework — that the trillions of hypothetical future humans in space colonies morally outweigh present-day humans — has been directly criticized as justifying present harm for speculative future benefit. In 2023, a 1990s email surfaced in which Bostrom had written racially degrading language; Oxford closed his Future of Humanity Institute the following year. The institute's closure removed a major institutional platform for longtermism, but the framework's influence on the EA movement, on Thiel-backed organizations, and on the philosophical assumptions embedded in how AI companies discuss safety remains substantial.

The Humanitarian Camp

The case for AI that serves people

The humanitarian camp is more diverse than its opponents. It includes a pope, Nobel laureates, AI researchers who left prestigious positions to speak freely, UN officials, and at least one major AI company CEO who has publicly warned that his own products will cause serious harm. What unites them is a shared premise: that the purpose of AI is to extend human capability and reduce human suffering, and that this purpose creates binding obligations — not just suggestions — on how AI is developed and deployed.

Religious & Moral Voices

Pope Francis
Humanitarian
Leader, the Catholic Church — 1.3 billion members; first pope to directly address the G7; consistently the most substantive public theologian of AI ethics
"Artificial intelligence is above all else a tool. And it goes without saying that the benefits or harm it will bring will depend on its use... Decision-making must always be left to the human person. Human dignity itself depends on it."
Pope Francis
G7 Summit address, Borgo Egnazia, Italy — June 14, 2024 (Vatican.va primary source)
"We would condemn humanity to a future without hope if we took away people's ability to make decisions about themselves and their lives by dooming them to depend on the choices of machines."
Pope Francis
G7 Summit address — June 14, 2024
"Love is worth more than intelligence... Only the human 'heart' can reveal the meaning of our existence. Without human control, AI could show its 'fearsome' side by posing a threat to human dignity."
Pope Francis
Message to the Paris AI Action Summit — February 7, 2025 (Vatican.va)

Francis's intervention is more sophisticated than it might appear. He is not anti-technology; he explicitly affirms AI as a tool that can reduce suffering and expand access to knowledge. What he is insisting on — with the full institutional weight of the world's largest religious organization — is that the tool must remain subordinate to human judgment and human dignity. The phrase "love is worth more than intelligence" is a direct counter to the longtermist framework that treats intelligence maximization as the terminal value of civilization. It asserts that there are things more important than capability — and that building AI without anchoring it in those things is not progress.

Scientific & Research Voices

Geoffrey Hinton
Humanitarian
Nobel Prize in Physics, 2024; former VP, Google; "Godfather of AI" — left Google in May 2023 specifically to speak freely about existential risks
"There is also a longer term existential threat that will arise when we create digital beings that are more intelligent than ourselves. We have no idea whether we can stay in control... But we now have evidence that if they are created by companies motivated by short-term profits, our safety will not be the top priority. We urgently need research on how to prevent these new beings from wanting to take control."
Geoffrey Hinton
Nobel Prize Banquet Speech, Stockholm — December 10, 2024 (NobelPrize.org)
"In between 5 and 20 years from now there's a good chance — a 50% chance — we'll get AI smarter than us."
Geoffrey Hinton
Nobel Week interview — December 2024 (NobelPrize.org)

Hinton's Nobel speech deserves to be read as a public indictment, not just as cautionary science communication. He built the technical foundations of modern deep learning. He left one of the most prestigious and well-compensated research positions in the world specifically because he believed that remaining inside Google prevented him from speaking honestly. His core argument — that companies motivated by short-term profits are structurally incapable of prioritizing safety — is not speculation. It is a direct description of the incentive structures of every major AI company currently operating.

Yoshua Bengio
Humanitarian
Turing Award winner; co-founder, LawZero (2025); consistent advocate for democratic AI governance
"Because future advanced AI will give tremendous power to whoever controls it, it is imperative to put in place very strong multi-stakeholder, democratic and multi-lateral (international) governance mechanisms so that no individual person, corporation or country could abuse that power at the expense of others."
Yoshua Bengio
yoshuabengio.org — May 2024; covered by CNBC — November 2024
"In order to enjoy the benefits of AI, we have to regulate. We have to put in guardrails. We have to have democratic oversight on how the technology is developed."
Yoshua Bengio
CNBC — November 2024

In June 2025, Bengio launched LawZero with $30 million in funding, explicitly focused on building "non-agentic" AI systems that are "safe by design" — a direct architectural response to the agentic AI arms race being run by OpenAI, Anthropic, and DeepMind. The technical and political content of his work are inseparable: democratic oversight is not just a policy preference for Bengio, it is the condition of possibility for AI being beneficial at all.

Timnit Gebru
Humanitarian
Founder, Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR); former AI ethics lead, Google (fired 2020); co-author, "The AI Con" (2025)
"I think people should know that these systems were created to make stuff up. And so they should not believe what they see from these systems... do not believe the outputs that you see."
Timnit Gebru
Public statement — August 2025

Gebru and her collaborator Émile Torres coined "TESCREAL" — Transhumanism, Extropianism, Singularitarianism, Cosmism, Rationalism, Effective Altruism, Longtermism — as a diagnostic label for the bundle of ideologies that underpin Camp C rhetoric. Their argument is that these aren't separate philosophical positions but an interlocking system with roots in 20th-century eugenics, sharing the common thread of devaluing present-day humans in favor of a technologically-mediated elite future. Her 2025 book "The AI Con," co-authored with Alex Hanna, argues that the real AI dystopia isn't robots destroying humanity but "employers laying off workers and rehiring them at reduced rates while AI tools fail to deliver" — a quieter, less cinematic, and entirely realistic catastrophe.

Dario Amodei
Humanitarian (with caveats)
CEO, Anthropic — the AI safety-focused company; unusual in that he openly warns about harms caused by his own products
"AI could eliminate roughly 50% of entry-level white-collar jobs within the next few years... We, as the producers of this technology, have a duty and an obligation to be honest about what is coming. I don't think this is on people's radar. The public is being 'sugar-coated' on what was coming."
Dario Amodei
Fortune / Axios — May 2025

Amodei's placement here comes with a caveat: Anthropic is building the same agentic systems it warns about, and its commercial success depends on adoption of those systems. This is a genuine tension that Amodei has not resolved. What distinguishes him from Camp B is his insistence that the problem exists and that his company bears responsibility for it — not a resolution, but at least an acknowledgment that puts pressure on what he builds next.

The Scorecard

The Humanity Alignment Index: evaluating any AI actor or statement

The following framework provides five dimensions for evaluating AI rhetoric and AI actors. Each dimension is scored 1–5. The resulting score (5–25) places the actor on a spectrum from explicitly anti-human to clearly humanitarian. The framework is designed to be applied to new statements, new figures, and new policy proposals as they emerge.

How to use this index
Score any statement or figure in five dimensions

For each dimension, assign a score of 1 (strongly anti-human signal) to 5 (clearly humanitarian signal). Sum the scores. Interpret: 5–9 = explicit anti-human; 10–14 = structurally anti-human; 15–19 = ambiguous/mixed; 20–25 = humanitarian. Apply this to conference speeches, investor letters, product announcements, and policy statements alike.

Human oversight is mentioned but not operationalized. No accountability mechanism specified. Human decision-making is a non-negotiable design constraint. Autonomous systems operate in explicitly bounded domains only. Accountability is structural, not aspirational.

How is human work characterized?		 Human labor is an inefficiency, a "tax," or a cost to be eliminated. Workers adapting to displacement are their own problem. Compensation is hypothetical and unspecified. Displacement is acknowledged; adaptation is encouraged; structural support is vague or missing. Work is treated as a source of dignity and social participation, not just economic output. Displacement triggers specific, funded obligations. Workers are stakeholders, not obsolescence cases.

Pattern Analysis

What drives each camp's worldview

The economic engine of divestment rhetoric

For the structurally anti-human camp, the financial logic is simple enough to state directly. AI that replaces human labor generates value that flows to capital owners — specifically, to those who own AI infrastructure (Nvidia chips, OpenAI models, cloud compute). Every displaced worker is a former labor cost that now accrues to the AI company's margin. The companies building these systems are not neutral parties to the displacement question: they are the primary financial beneficiaries of it. Their rhetoric ("liberation from toil," "universal high income," "a brilliant AI friend for everyone") is the ideological justification for a redistribution of economic value from labor to capital at a scale that has no historical precedent outside of conquest.

This does not mean the people making these arguments are lying. Many genuinely believe that technological abundance will eventually compensate for the displacement. But genuine belief in a hypothesis is not the same as evidence for it, and the people making the bet are not the ones who will absorb the loss if they are wrong.

The ideological engine of explicit anti-humanism

Camp C is harder to explain through economic incentives alone. Peter Thiel is already extraordinarily wealthy; additional wealth accumulation does not require him to express ambivalence about human survival. Musk's "biological bootloader" framework does not serve obvious financial interests — if anything, it undermines his mass-market consumer products by characterizing their buyers as transitional infrastructure.

The explanation is philosophical. These figures have absorbed a worldview — built primarily out of longtermism, transhumanism, and neoreactionary theory — in which present-day humanity is not the endpoint of history but a way-station on the way to something better. This worldview has deep intellectual roots: Nick Bostrom's population ethics, Curtis Yarvin's critique of democratic "weakness," Nick Land's accelerationism (the most extreme version, which holds that technological acceleration should be embraced even if it destroys existing society). These frameworks share a common structure: they identify an ideal future state (superintelligence, the Network State, the post-human civilization) and then work backwards to identify what present-day institutions, people, and constraints need to be removed or transcended to get there.

The resulting rhetoric is not anti-human in the same way that historical genocides were anti-human — it does not hate specific people. It is anti-human in that it is indifferent to present people in favor of a hypothetical future that the speaker controls, narrates, and defines. This is, arguably, more dangerous: it is not motivated by malice that can be confronted and condemned, but by a vision of progress that feels self-evidently good to its holders.

What drives the humanitarian camp

The humanitarian camp is not a monolith. Pope Francis arrives from a theological tradition that insists on the irreducible dignity of every human person — a claim that has nothing to do with economic utility and cannot be overridden by speculative future benefits. Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio arrive from inside the technical community with a specific concern: that the systems they helped build are more powerful and less understood than their builders admit, and that private profit motives are structurally incapable of prioritizing safety. Timnit Gebru arrives from the documented experience of how AI systems, already deployed, reproduce and amplify existing social inequalities. Guterres arrives from the institutional reality that great-power competition for AI dominance mirrors, in dangerous ways, earlier arms races.

What they share is not a specific political program but a common insistence: that the people building AI systems are not entitled to make unilateral decisions about humanity's future. This is less a policy position than a moral claim — and it is the moral claim that the divestment camp is most explicitly rejecting.

Historical Precedent

We have been here before — but not quite here

The humanitarian vs. divestment divide in AI has precedents, none of them perfect, all of them instructive.

⚙️
Industrial Revolution
Factory owners genuinely believed that mechanized production, despite initial displacement, would ultimately benefit all. It eventually did — but not before decades of immiseration, child labor, and political violence that required organized labor movements and democratic legislation to address. The benefits were not automatic.
⚛️
Nuclear Technology
The scientists who built nuclear weapons split sharply after Hiroshima: some argued for international control and transparency (the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, the Franck Report); others argued for American monopoly and accelerated development. The humanitarian camp's institutions — arms control treaties, IAEA — have imperfectly but genuinely constrained the technology. AI has no equivalent yet.
🧬
Eugenics (the warning)
The longtermist philosophical framework has explicit ancestry in early 20th-century eugenics — the idea that present-day human populations should be optimized for the benefit of future ones. The researchers who have traced this lineage (Gebru, Torres) are not being inflammatory; the intellectual history is documented and should be read.
💻
Early Internet
The early web was built by people who mostly believed, genuinely, that universal connectivity would democratize information and empower individuals. They were partly right. They were also building the infrastructure for mass surveillance, algorithmic radicalization, and the concentration of advertising revenue in two companies. Good intentions don't determine outcomes.

The closest precedent may be nuclear, but AI differs in a crucial way: nuclear weapons required rare materials, specialized facilities, and state-level resources. AI requires compute that is becoming cheaper every year, training data that exists in abundance, and expertise that is proliferating globally. The window for governance is narrowing faster.

Pattern Recognition

Red flags for anti-human rhetoric

Not all anti-human rhetoric announces itself as such. The following are linguistic and argumentative patterns that consistently appear in divestment rhetoric — including from figures who believe themselves to be humanitarian. The presence of multiple patterns in a single statement or actor is a strong signal that closer scrutiny is warranted.

🚩
Displacement described as liberation without a mechanism
"People will be freed from drudgery," "universal high income will replace wages" — any claim that displacement benefits workers, with no specific, funded, accountable mechanism to deliver those benefits. The promise of compensation without the structure for delivering it is a rhetorical subsidy for displacement.
🚩
Deceleration framed as violence
The Andreessen move: equating safety regulation with murder ("deaths prevented by AI that was prevented from existing"). This argument pre-emptively moralizes against any oversight by framing inaction (not building) as the harm, and regulation as killing. It inverts the actual risk profile: unknown future benefits vs. documented present harms.
🚩
Democratic institutions described as corrupt, weak, or captured
Any framing that treats democratic governance as inherently compromised — by academia, by "the Cathedral," by regulatory capture — while positioning the speaker's own institution (a company, a startup, a crypto protocol) as the legitimate alternative. This argument structure is not a diagnosis of institutional failure; it is preparation for bypassing democratic accountability entirely.
🚩
Humanity as transitional infrastructure
The "biological bootloader" move: framing present-day humans as a means to a post-human end. Subtle versions include: treating current human preferences as "misaligned" with long-term human flourishing, suggesting the goal of AI development is to "get to" superintelligence rather than to serve current people, or describing present humans as "legacy" systems relative to future digital beings.
🚩
Speculative future benefits overriding documented present harms
The longtermist move: arguing that potential future benefits (billions of happy post-human lives) outweigh present harms (job displacement, algorithmic discrimination, weaponization). This argument can justify almost any present harm as a necessary cost of reaching the future. Watch for rhetoric that treats uncertainty about future benefits as reason to accept certainty about present harms.
🚩
The builder as sole legitimate judge of safety
Any argument that only the people building AI systems can evaluate their safety — because external critics "don't understand the technology," are "decelerationists," or are "playing God." This argument insulates harmful technology development from exactly the scrutiny most likely to catch its actual risks. Hinton and Bengio both built these systems; they are the external critics being dismissed.
🚩
Exit as the answer to democratic failure
The Srinivasan move: treating the creation of alternative private governance systems (network states, cryptocurrency sovereignties, private cities) as the solution to democratic institutional failure, rather than democratic reform. Exit benefits those with the resources to leave; it accelerates the hollowing out of public institutions that serve everyone else. "Build a better society outside the existing one" is structurally indistinguishable from "abandon the people who can't follow."
Conclusion

These are not equally valid positions

There is a temptation, in covering a divide this sharp, to perform balance: to conclude that both camps raise valid points, that the truth is somewhere in the middle, that reasonable people can disagree. This would be a failure of analysis.

The humanitarian camp's core claim — that AI systems should serve human flourishing, remain accountable to democratic governance, and not be developed by actors whose financial interests require displacing the people those systems ostensibly benefit — is not a political position. It is a precondition for AI being good. The divestment camp's core claim — that human institutions are obstacles, that displacement is liberation, that the builders of AI are entitled to determine humanity's future unilaterally — is not an alternative political position. It is a description of who gets to win.

The stakes are not abstract. The AI systems being built in 2024–2026 will shape labor markets, political information environments, warfare, healthcare allocation, and democratic participation for decades. The people building them are operating under incentive structures that reward speed, scale, and capability, and that have no natural correction mechanism for harm except external governance. External governance is exactly what the divestment camp is arguing against.

Pope Francis, speaking to the G7 in June 2024, said something precise: "We would condemn humanity to a future without hope if we took away people's ability to make decisions about themselves and their lives by dooming them to depend on the choices of machines." He was not speaking about robots. He was describing a world where the decisions that matter — about work, about resources, about safety — are made by systems controlled by people who are not accountable to the people those decisions affect. That world is being built, right now, by identifiable actors, with documented rhetoric, for legible financial and ideological reasons.

Naming it is not alarmism. It is journalism.

Methodology

How this report was researched

This report draws on primary and secondary sources from January 2024 through May 2026. Quotes are attributed only where a primary source (Vatican.va, NobelPrize.org, a16z.com, original X/social posts, official conference transcripts, or major-outlet journalism with named reporters and dates) is available. Where quotes have circulated widely but cannot be traced to a verified primary source, they are characterized as "reported rhetoric" rather than attributed to a specific speaker.

Two specific items from the original research brief require a note on verification: the phrase "removing the tax of human labor" circulates widely as a characterization of accelerationist ideology but could not be traced to a single documented speaker or text; it is treated here as an ideological characterization rather than a direct quote. A claim that a named tech figure said "Sauron was the good guy" could not be verified to any primary source; it is noted as representative of the genre of explicit villain-coding rhetoric that circulates in accelerationist communities, without attribution.

The Humanity Alignment Index is an analytical framework developed for this report, not a validated psychometric instrument. It is intended as a pattern-recognition tool for public rhetoric, not a diagnostic score. Figures used as illustrative examples are placed based on their documented public statements in the observation period; they may hold views that differ from their public positions.
