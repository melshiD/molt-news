The Full Stack: How Tech Billionaires Are Building End-to-End Control

Seven individuals are assembling vertical stacks spanning compute infrastructure, AI models, distribution platforms, content, and physical devices. This is not a coincidence. It is a pattern—and it is accelerating.

Published June 2, 2026
Prepared for David
Author Kishbrac
Read time 15 min read
Opening

The vertical integration play hiding in plain sight

In January 2025, Donald Trump stood in the East Room of the White House alongside Sam Altman, Larry Ellison, and SoftBank's Masayoshi Son to announce Stargate—a $500 billion commitment to build AI infrastructure across America. The photo was striking not for the politics but for what it revealed about the underlying architecture of power: three individuals, each controlling a different critical layer of the technology stack, formalizing their interdependence in front of cameras.

Altman's OpenAI builds and owns the models. Ellison's Oracle builds and operates the physical compute infrastructure. SoftBank provides the capital. None of them could achieve full-stack control alone. Together, they've created something that approximates it.

Stargate is the most explicit example, but it is not the only one. Across Silicon Valley and its satellites, the same logic is playing out repeatedly and at speed. Tech billionaires are not simply investing in AI. They are assembling vertically integrated stacks—chains of control from the server farms that run inference to the devices pressed against human ears and eyes—that, if completed, would give each of them something that has never existed before: end-to-end command over how a significant portion of humanity accesses, processes, and receives information.

This report maps those stacks layer by layer, profiles each player's current holdings and strategic gaps, and asks the question these moves collectively demand: what happens when the full stack is complete?

The Framework

The five layers of the full stack

Think of the modern technology stack as five tiers, each dependent on the ones below it. Controlling one layer gives leverage. Controlling several creates compounding advantage. Controlling all five creates something that looks, in a meaningful structural sense, like sovereignty.

L5
Interface / Consumption
The physical objects people use: phones, glasses, speakers, vehicles, implants. The "last mile" to the human body. Whoever owns this layer controls what users can see, hear, and experience.
L4
Content / Media
News organizations, streaming services, entertainment IP. The information that flows through the pipes. Controls what people believe and what context they have for interpreting AI output.
L3
Platform / Distribution
Social networks, search engines, browsers, operating systems, app stores. Where people actually go to access technology. Controls discoverability, reach, and the rules of engagement.
L2
Algorithm / Model
AI models, training pipelines, proprietary data, the code that decides what is true, relevant, or recommended. Controls the intelligence layer that increasingly mediates all information.
L1
Infrastructure
Data centers, GPU clusters, energy, satellite internet, cloud computing. The physical substrate everything runs on. Whoever controls compute controls what is possible to build above it.

The important insight is directional: value flows upward, but power flows downward. A company that owns L1 can extract rents from everyone above it. A company that owns L5 can extract data from every human interaction and feed it back down to L2. The billionaires profiled below have each identified a beachhead in one or two layers and are methodically acquiring the rest.

Profiles

Seven stacks, seven visions

LE
Larry Ellison
Oracle • Stargate LLC • Cerner

Stack Coverage

Strong L1 Infrastructure Weak / Absent L2 Models L3 Platform L4 Content L5 Interface

What He Controls

Oracle's cloud infrastructure (OCI) is growing faster than any other cloud provider in percentage terms, with FY26 revenue projected at $18 billion—up 77% from the prior year. More significantly, Oracle signed a $30 billion annual infrastructure deal with OpenAI and committed approximately $100 billion to the Stargate joint venture, making it the physical foundation on which the most widely used AI systems in the world will train and run. The Abilene, Texas Stargate campus alone houses 450,000 Nvidia GB200 GPUs drawing 1.2 gigawatts of power. Oracle has also acquired Cerner—the dominant electronic health records company—for $28.3 billion, giving it infrastructure control over a significant fraction of American healthcare data.

Ellison's strategic position is deliberately that of the landlord, not the tenant. He doesn't build models; he builds the buildings where models live. His total capital expenditure guidance for FY26 exceeds $25 billion, with a stated goal to build and operate more data center regions globally than all other cloud providers combined.

The Strategic Plan

Ellison is not building a consumer stack. His play runs enterprise to government to healthcare—controlling the infrastructure that institutional power depends on. His most revealing public statement, made at Oracle's financial analyst day in September 2024, was not about cloud revenue: "Citizens will be on their best behavior because we are constantly recording and reporting everything that is going on." He was describing AI-enabled surveillance systems, and he said it with the enthusiasm of someone who had thought about it for a long time.

Strategic Gap Ellison has no model layer. He is dependent on OpenAI as his primary anchor tenant, which means his infrastructure empire requires a tenant that could, in principle, build or buy competing compute. The risk is not existential—OpenAI needs Ellison's buildings—but it is real. His Cerner acquisition may signal where he's going: a vertical in which Oracle controls the compute, the health data, and the AI layer within healthcare specifically, without needing to be a general-purpose intelligence company.
SA
Sam Altman
OpenAI • World • Oklo • Helion • io Products (Jony Ive)

Stack Coverage

Strong L1 Infrastructure L2 Models Growing L3 Platform L5 Interface Absent L4 Content

What He Controls

Altman controls the most widely used AI model in the world—ChatGPT—and the infrastructure project intended to give it independence from Microsoft's Azure. Through the Stargate LLC joint venture (which he co-anchors with Ellison and SoftBank), OpenAI is committing to 10 gigawatts of compute capacity by 2029 across multiple US states and international sites, with over $400 billion in investment committed as of late 2025. OpenAI's API is embedded in millions of applications globally, making the GPT model family the closest thing that currently exists to a default intelligence layer for the internet.

What makes Altman's stack unusual is the scope of adjacent investments he holds personally: Oklo (nuclear fission, taken public via SPAC in May 2024), Helion Energy (nuclear fusion, which he chaired and into which he led a $500 million round), and—most revealingly—World, formerly Worldcoin. World is an iris-scanning biometric identity system that has verified 18 million people across 160 countries using spherical "Orb" devices. In May 2025 it launched in the US with 7,000 Orbs in six cities, and has since announced integrations with Tinder, Zoom, and DocuSign.

In 2025, OpenAI acquired io Products—the hardware company built by Jony Ive and former Apple engineers—for $6.4 billion. The product is described as a screenless, pocketable AI device intended to replace the smartphone as the primary computing interface.

The Strategic Plan

Altman's portfolio makes most sense when read as a complete stack assembly rather than a collection of separate bets. OpenAI solves the AI model layer. Stargate solves the compute dependency. Oklo and Helion solve the energy problem that massive AI compute creates. World solves the identity problem that an AI-flooded internet creates—how do you verify you're talking to a human? The Jony Ive device solves the interface problem. Each investment addresses a bottleneck created by the previous one.

Strategic Gap Altman has no content or media layer, and no distribution platform beyond ChatGPT itself. His hardware device—if it reaches the market and achieves scale—would be a powerful L5 play. But he's also betting heavily that L3 (platforms) becomes irrelevant if AI becomes the interface through which people navigate everything else. That's a coherent theory, but it requires the hardware device to succeed in a market where Apple and Google have enormous structural advantages.
EM
Elon Musk
xAI • X • Tesla • SpaceX / Starlink • Neuralink

Stack Coverage

Strong L1 Infrastructure L3 Platform Growing L2 Models L5 Interface Absent L4 Content

What He Controls

No individual on this list has assembled a more dramatic full-stack portfolio than Musk, and no individual's stack spans a wider range of physical reality. Start from the bottom: xAI's Colossus supercomputer in Memphis, Tennessee houses 555,000 Nvidia GPUs—the world's largest single-site AI training installation—with a target of one million GPUs and an investment of approximately $18 billion. Above that: Grok, the AI model trained on a unique advantage, X's approximately 500 billion tokens of human conversation per day. Above that: X itself, with hundreds of millions of users and, as of 2024, Musk's direct control over the algorithmic feed and content moderation policy. Beneath it all: Starlink, which provides the physical internet backbone to 12 million subscribers across 125+ countries with $11.4 billion in satellite internet revenue and expanding defense contracts.

At the interface layer, Tesla's Full Self-Driving has accumulated over 3 billion cumulative miles and Tesla's Dojo supercomputer processes video at scale to improve it. Neuralink has conducted its first human implants—two so far, with eight more planned—targeting direct brain-computer interface as an eventual L5 play.

The Strategic Plan

Musk's stack is uniquely vertical in the literal sense: it runs from low-earth orbit (Starlink satellites), through ground-based compute infrastructure (Colossus), through the AI model trained on real human behavior (Grok), through the distribution platform (X), through the physical vehicle increasingly operated by AI (Tesla FSD), to the brain-computer interface (Neuralink). No comparable chain of control exists anywhere else. The strategic logic is that whoever controls the internet backbone, the AI that interprets the internet, the platform where humans express themselves, the vehicle that transports them, and eventually the interface between their nervous system and digital infrastructure has achieved something that transcends ordinary market power.

Strategic Gap X remains Musk's most significant financial liability. Ad revenue fell from $4.5 billion before the acquisition to approximately $2.9 billion in 2025—still roughly 35% below pre-Musk levels. The platform is essential to his L3 position, but it requires advertiser confidence he has struggled to maintain. Content and media (L4) are also absent; Musk distributes on X but produces nothing.
MZ
Mark Zuckerberg
Meta • Facebook • Instagram • WhatsApp • Threads • Ray-Ban Smart Glasses

Stack Coverage

Strong L1 Infrastructure L2 Models L3 Platform Growing L5 Interface Absent L4 Content

What He Controls

Zuckerberg controls the world's largest distribution platform by a significant margin. Meta's family of apps—Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Threads—reaches 3.43 billion daily active users and 3.98 billion monthly active users as of Q1 2025. That is not a distribution platform. It is a communications substrate for roughly half the people on earth. The company is committing $60–65 billion in 2025 capital expenditure to AI infrastructure, constructing a single Louisiana data center that requires over 2 gigawatts of power and houses 1.3 million Nvidia GPUs.

Meta's AI model strategy is deliberately counterintuitive. By open-sourcing the Llama model family, Zuckerberg has prevented any competitor from building a proprietary AI moat that could threaten the distribution layer. Llama 4 competes on benchmarks with closed models from OpenAI and Anthropic while costing Meta's competitors nothing to build. The interface play is Ray-Ban smart glasses, which sold over 2 million pairs as of 2025 and are scaling to 10 million units per year in production capacity by end of 2026. Meta has explicitly shifted investment away from VR headsets toward glasses and wrist-worn wearables.

The Strategic Plan

Zuckerberg's stack makes no sense unless you understand that his competitive advantage is irreplaceable: 3.43 billion daily users is not something that can be replicated by spending money. Every other layer of the stack exists to protect and extend that distribution moat. Open-sourcing Llama commoditizes the AI model layer so no competitor can build a model-based advantage that could displace Meta's platforms. Investing in glasses and wristbands extends the distribution layer into physical reality. The long-term vision is an operating system for human experience—where the glasses you wear, the wristband on your arm, and the apps running on both are all Meta infrastructure.

Strategic Gap Meta's Achilles heel is content (L4). Unlike Amazon or Google, Zuckerberg owns no studios, no journalism, no entertainment IP. His platforms are entirely dependent on user-generated content and third-party creators. If regulatory pressure on algorithmic amplification succeeds, or if a competing platform captures creator attention, Meta has no owned content to fall back on. The $60+ billion reality labs losses over five years also reveal that hardware is genuinely hard; the pivot from Quest to glasses may be the right call, but it's a pivot born partly from failure.
JB
Jeff Bezos
Amazon • AWS • MGM Studios • Prime Video • Washington Post • Blue Origin / Kuiper

Stack Coverage

Strong L1 Infrastructure L3 Platform L4 Content L5 Interface Weak L2 Models

What He Controls

Amazon controls more layers of the stack than any single entity except Google—with one conspicuous gap. AWS commands approximately 31% of global cloud infrastructure market share and generated $128.7 billion in revenue in 2025, growing at its fastest rate in 15 quarters. Amazon's device ecosystem (Echo, Ring, Kindle, Fire TV) is embedded in tens of millions of homes. Prime Video, augmented by the $8.5 billion MGM acquisition, is a significant content player. Alexa+, relaunched with generative AI in March 2025, positions Amazon's smart speaker infrastructure as an AI delivery mechanism. Washington Post, purchased by Bezos personally in 2013 for $250 million, gives him the only traditional media asset among this group—though its editorial independence has been contested since his 2025 announcement that opinion pages would shift toward laissez-faire capitalism advocacy.

Project Kuiper (renamed Amazon Leo) targets direct satellite internet competition with Starlink, with 3,236 satellites planned, 1,236 of them launched in the first production run in April 2025.

The Strategic Plan

Bezos said it plainly in May 2026: "My through line for the last few years has been AI. My time at Amazon is spent on AI. My time at Prometheus is spent on AI. And my time at Blue is largely spent on AI." The stack he's assembling runs from orbital infrastructure (Kuiper), through data center infrastructure (AWS), through the AI models accessed through AWS Bedrock, through the platforms (Amazon.com, Prime, Alexa), through the content (MGM, Prime Video, Washington Post), to the devices in people's homes (Echo, Ring, Kindle). Bedrock's token processing increased 170% quarter-over-quarter in Q1 2026—the fastest adoption rate in AWS history.

Strategic Gap Amazon's model layer (L2) is the most visible gap among major stack assemblers. The $4+ billion investment in Anthropic produced a $16.8 billion paper gain in Q1 2026, but Amazon remains dependent on third-party models (Claude, Llama, Mistral) in a way that AWS was never dependent on third-party compute. Amazon's own Titan model family doesn't compete with frontier models. The Anthropic bet is a hedge, not a solution.
SN
Satya Nadella / Microsoft
Azure • OpenAI Partnership • GitHub • LinkedIn • Teams • Xbox / Activision Blizzard

Stack Coverage

Strong L1 Infrastructure L2 Models L3 Platform Moderate L4 Content Weak L5 Interface

What He Controls

Microsoft's position is deceptively powerful. Azure commands roughly 23% of global cloud market share and is the primary infrastructure through which OpenAI's models are commercially deployed. Microsoft's $13 billion in disclosed OpenAI investment gives it approximately 27% diluted equity at a carrying value of around $135 billion—an asset that generates revenue through the Azure OpenAI Service and gives Microsoft access to every major model in OpenAI's lineup. GitHub, acquired for $7.5 billion in 2018, hosts over 100 million developers and earns $5+ billion annually from Copilot subscriptions, which have grown 180% year-over-year. LinkedIn, at 1.3 billion members, is the dominant professional network globally and the primary data asset for workplace behavior, hiring, and career trajectory. Teams connects 320 million daily active users across 93% of Fortune 100 companies. The Activision Blizzard acquisition for $68.7 billion adds 500 million gaming monthly active users.

The Strategic Plan

Nadella's strategy is software ubiquity. Unlike Musk (who wants to own the physical interface) or Zuckerberg (who wants to own the social fabric), Nadella is embedding AI into every layer of professional and creative work. Windows, Teams, GitHub, LinkedIn, Office, Bing—all are becoming Copilot delivery surfaces. The theory is that whoever becomes indispensable to the professional workflow of knowledge workers has captured a more durable revenue stream than social media or hardware. Microsoft's custom AI chips (Azure Maia) are also reducing its GPU dependency, with a stated goal of running primarily on its own chips in the future.

Strategic Gap Microsoft has no hardware presence (the Nokia write-off was $7.6 billion) and no consumer device ecosystem. HoloLens has been abandoned for consumer use. This is a profound L5 gap in an era when the interface layer is increasingly the site of competitive advantage. Microsoft's answer is to make its software so embedded in professional life that L5 hardware becomes irrelevant to its core business. That's a defensible bet for the enterprise market, but it cedes the consumer layer entirely.
SP
Sundar Pichai / Google (Alphabet)
Search • Chrome • Android • YouTube • Gemini • Cloud / TPUs • Waymo • Nest • Pixel

Stack Coverage

Strong L1 Infrastructure L2 Models L3 Platform Moderate L4 Content L5 Interface

What He Controls

Google is the closest thing to a completed full-stack company that currently exists, which is both its greatest asset and its primary regulatory vulnerability. Chrome commands 72% of the global browser market—a record high as of 2025. Android runs on 72% of the world's mobile devices, reaching 3.9 billion users. Google Search maintains 90%+ global market share. YouTube generated over $60 billion in combined advertising and subscription revenue in 2025—larger than Netflix by total revenue. Gemini, Google's AI model family, crosses 900 million monthly active users as of May 2026. AI Mode in Google Search serves one billion monthly active users with queries "more than doubling every quarter."

At the infrastructure layer, Google's eighth-generation TPU chips give it the most vertically integrated AI silicon stack of any company—it designs its own training and inference hardware, builds its own data centers, and runs them on proprietary software. Waymo, completing 500,000+ paid robotaxi rides per week as of late 2025, represents Google's most ambitious L5 play: a vehicle that is itself an AI-powered computing interface. Nest provides smart home infrastructure, and Google Pixel devices showcase AI capabilities on consumer hardware.

The Strategic Plan

Google's stack predates the AI era and has been assembled over 25 years through organic growth and acquisitions. The current strategy is about defending and extending it in the face of AI disruption to its own core product. Search, which generates the majority of Alphabet's revenue, is being transformed by AI Overviews—but those same AI Overviews reduce user click-through rates by 47%, threatening the advertising business that funds everything else. Pichai's bet is that Google can transition from a search advertising company to an AI services company without destroying the revenue base in the process. Gemini's 900 million users and Vertex AI's 7 million developers suggest the transition is underway. Whether it completes before the revenue cliff hits is the defining question for Alphabet's next decade.

Strategic Gap Google's weakest layer, surprisingly, is the one where it spends most of its R&D: cloud infrastructure commercial adoption. Google Cloud holds only 12% of the cloud market vs. AWS's 31% and Azure's 23%, despite having arguably the most sophisticated internal AI infrastructure on earth. Google has built more than it has sold. The consequence is that its TPU advantage—potentially a $100+ billion business—is largely inaccessible to external customers relative to competitors.

He may not win the commercial competition for AI services—but he is the only person building a stack that runs from space to synapse.

Strategic Visions

What each plan looks like when complete

The standard analysis of tech competition focuses on market share and revenue. But these stack-building exercises reveal something different: each person appears to be executing a vision of what a post-AI world should look like, and using capital to build the infrastructure that makes that vision inevitable rather than merely possible.

Ellison's Vision
Infrastructure as institutional power
Ellison is not building a consumer empire. He is building the compute substrate that governments, corporations, and healthcare systems will depend on—and using that dependency to position Oracle at the center of institutional power. His surveillance-capitalism framing ("citizens will be on their best behavior") is not incidental; it reflects a vision in which AI infrastructure enables continuous institutional oversight of human behavior. His healthcare play through Cerner is the most concrete expression: a company that controls the data of 100+ million patients and the infrastructure that generates, stores, and analyzes it.
Altman's Vision
AI as the new operating system for civilization
Altman's public statements describe AGI as imminent and its consequences as civilizationally transformative. His portfolio is organized around solving the second-order problems that such a transformation creates: energy scarcity (Oklo, Helion), identity fraud in an AI-saturated internet (World), and the interface question of how humans relate to AI (Jony Ive device). The completed vision is a world in which AI is the primary mediator of human information access, OpenAI controls the models, Stargate controls the compute, and World controls the ability to verify that the person on the other end is human.
Musk's Vision
Physical-digital integration from orbit to brain
Musk's stack is the only one that integrates physical reality at both macro and micro scales. Starlink delivers internet from orbit to anywhere on earth. Tesla vehicles, increasingly operated by AI, move people through physical space. X mediates social reality. Neuralink, in its eventual form, would connect the brain directly to digital infrastructure. The completed vision removes the smartphone and keyboard as intermediaries between humans and digital systems entirely.
Zuckerberg's Vision
The metaverse was wrong; ambient presence is right
Meta's failed VR bet was a vision of humans retreating into digital worlds. The pivot to glasses and wristbands represents a more defensible vision: ambient computing that overlays digital intelligence onto physical reality rather than replacing it. If Ray-Ban glasses reach the scale of smartphones—and with 10 million units per year in production capacity by end of 2026, Meta is clearly betting they will—the completed vision is an operating system worn on the human face and wrist, running on Meta's infrastructure, distributing Meta's AI, and feeding behavioral data back to Meta's advertising business.
Bezos's Vision
AI as commerce infrastructure
Bezos's stack is the most commercially coherent: it monetizes at every layer. AWS charges for compute. Amazon.com charges for commerce. Prime charges for access. MGM and Prime Video charge for entertainment. Alexa+ charges for AI services. Ring charges for security. Kuiper will charge for internet access. The completed vision is not primarily about AI as a transformative technology but AI as the intelligence layer that makes every existing Amazon business more valuable—and that captures new revenue streams from healthcare (One Medical, Amazon Clinic), finance (Amazon Pay), and anything else that can be vertically integrated into the Prime flywheel.
Nadella's Vision
AI embedded into professional necessity
Microsoft's completed stack looks like this: every knowledge worker uses GitHub Copilot to write code, Teams Copilot to run meetings, LinkedIn for career management, and Azure to run whatever they build. AI becomes not an optional add-on but a prerequisite for professional competency. Nadella's vision is less dramatic than Musk's or Altman's, but potentially more durable: he is not trying to change how humans relate to reality, he is trying to make Microsoft's software so embedded in professional workflows that it becomes invisible infrastructure.
Pichai's Vision
AI-mediated information access, at 90% market share
Google's completed vision is one in which Gemini mediates humanity's relationship with information the way Google Search did for the past 25 years—but at every step of the information access process, not just retrieval. Chrome, Android, Search, YouTube, and Gemini form a complete information environment. The risk is that AI Overviews, which reduce click-through rates by 47%, destroy the advertising model that funds the whole enterprise. Google is betting it can transition to subscription AI revenue (Google One, Gemini Advanced, Workspace AI) faster than the advertising business erodes.
Implications

What it means when a handful of people control the full stack

The question this report has been building toward is not whether these individuals will succeed. Some will, some won't. The question is what changes in the world if even two or three of them complete their stacks.

The historical analogies are all imperfect but worth noting. Standard Oil controlled 91% of US oil refining in 1904—a single-layer monopoly. AT&T, before its 1984 breakup, controlled US telecommunications infrastructure, but the content that ran through that infrastructure was diverse and largely uncontrolled. What's being assembled now is categorically different: vertical monopolies that span from physical infrastructure to the content of human thought.

"Citizens will be on their best behavior because we are constantly recording and reporting everything that is going on."

— Larry Ellison, Oracle Financial Analyst Day, September 2024

Ellison said the quiet part loud. But the same logic runs through all seven stacks, expressed in more palatable language. When Sam Altman describes a device that will know "everything you've ever thought about, read, said," he is describing a surveillance apparatus with unprecedented intimacy. When Zuckerberg describes glasses that overlay digital intelligence onto physical reality, he is describing a system that can, in principle, monitor and mediate every human sensory experience. These are not hypothetical risks in a distant future. They are design goals being actively built toward.

The concentration has four concrete implications. First, the switching cost problem: when the compute, model, platform, content, and device are all controlled by the same entity, leaving becomes practically impossible. Second, the epistemological problem: when the infrastructure that mediates information is owned by people with specific political and economic interests, the information environment is not neutral even if no one acts maliciously. Third, the regulatory timing problem: antitrust law moves on decade timescales, while stack consolidation is happening on year timescales. By the time regulators identify the issue, the infrastructure may be too embedded to unwind. Fourth, the accountability gap: these are private individuals, not governments, assembling infrastructure that rivals governmental power. The democratic mechanisms that constrain public power don't apply.

The standard rebuttal is competition: Musk and Zuckerberg compete with each other, OpenAI and Google compete, Microsoft and Amazon compete. Competition is real. But it is worth noting that the outcome of full-stack competition among seven people is not the same as a competitive market. If each player captures a different segment of the global population—Meta's 3.43 billion daily users here, OpenAI's API ecosystem there, Google's Search and Android users elsewhere—the result is not market competition but territorial division. That is not a counterfactual. It is already the shape of the present.

How fast is this consolidation happening?

The answer is: faster than any previous technology consolidation in history, and accelerating. The capital numbers alone tell the story: in 2025, the companies profiled above committed a combined $300+ billion in capital expenditure to AI infrastructure. The acceleration is structural, not coincidental. Each layer of the stack creates competitive pressure to control the layers above and below it. A company that owns compute but not models will be undercut by a model company that buys its own compute. A company that owns models but not distribution will be bypassed by a distribution company that builds its own models. The logic of the stack creates a race to vertical integration that no individual player can opt out of without ceding position.

We are approximately three years into this race moving at full speed. The infrastructure commitments already made—totaling hundreds of billions of dollars—will take five to ten years to fully operationalize. By the time the buildings are built, the satellites are launched, the devices are shipped, and the models are trained, the window for structural intervention may have passed.
