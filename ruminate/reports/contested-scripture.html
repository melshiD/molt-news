Contested Scripture

A Historical Analysis of the Bible's Most Contentious Passages

Published June 5, 2026
Reading Time ~30 minutes
Scope Ten passages, twenty centuries
Approach Non-confessional, historically grounded

The history of biblical interpretation is, in large measure, a history of power. Texts do not speak; people speak through them. Every translation is an argument, every commentary a negotiation between what words say and what readers need them to mean. This is not a counsel of despair — it is an invitation to intellectual honesty that the best biblical scholars have always extended to their readers.

The passages gathered here are not chosen for obscurity. They are, by any measure, among the most familiar words in the Western canon. And precisely because they are familiar, they have been unusually available for service — pressed into the defense of slavery, the suppression of women, the justification of conquest, the persecution of minorities, and the obstruction of economic reform. They have also, in the hands of others, served liberation, dignity, resistance, and hope.

The same verse. Different centuries. Different power arrangements. Opposite conclusions.

What follows is an attempt to trace that history with rigor and without false comfort. The goal is not to declare any interpretation correct — readers and traditions will continue to disagree, as they should — but to make visible the interpretive machinery that has too often operated in darkness. When we understand why a reading emerged, who it served, and what it displaced, we become harder to manipulate. That is a worthy ambition for any study of any text.

A Note on Method

This analysis draws on historical-critical scholarship, textual criticism, archaeological evidence, and the history of interpretation. It is non-confessional: neither affirming nor denying the theological claims of any tradition. Biblical references use multiple translations to surface meaningful differences; key terms are provided in their original Hebrew (עִבְרִית), Aramaic (ארמית), and Greek (Ἑλληνικά) where they matter to the argument.

The Ten Passages
Passage I • Genesis 1–2

In the Beginning: The Cosmological Divide

On the curious problem of two creation stories and one very contested word for "day"

In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth. The earth was without form and void, and darkness was over the face of the deep. Genesis 1:1–2 (ESV)

Few readers of Genesis 1 notice that Genesis 2 tells a different creation story. In the first account, God creates by divine fiat over six days, with humans appearing last (Genesis 1:1–2:4a). In the second, there are no "days," the man is formed before plants and animals, and the narrative proceeds as intimate clay-shaping rather than cosmic declaration (Genesis 2:4b–25). For most of Christian history, commentators harmonized these accounts through elaborate allegory. What changed was not the text but the interpretive framework readers brought to it.

The Hebrew word at the center of the modern controversy is יוֹם (yom), translated as "day." In Hebrew, yom can mean a literal twenty-four-hour period, a year, an era, or an indefinite span — identical to its English cognate in expressions like "in the day of the judges." The early church father Origen of Alexandria (c. 184–253 CE) argued against a literalist reading explicitly: "What intelligent person would believe that the first, second, and third day had an evening and morning when there was as yet no sun?"1Origen, De Principiis IV.1.16, trans. G.W. Butterworth (1936). Augustine similarly argued in De Genesi ad Litteram (c. 401–415 CE) that the "days" of Genesis refer to divine apprehension of order, not chronological time. Augustine of Hippo echoed this, warning that Christians who insist on anti-scientific readings bring the faith into disrepute.

The Documentary Hypothesis — developed by scholars including Jean Astruc (1753) and Julius Wellhausen (1878) — identified distinct literary sources within the Pentateuch, labeled J (the Yahwist), E (the Elohist), D (the Deuteronomist), and P (the Priestly source). The two creation stories reflect P's orderly, liturgical style and J's intimate, anthropomorphic one. This scholarly consensus, refined over 150 years, is today the standard lens through which professional biblical scholars read Genesis.

Interpretation Timeline: Genesis Creation

c. 410 CE Augustine warns against anti-scientific literalism in De Genesi ad Litteram.

1859 Darwin's On the Origin of Species triggers crisis in literalist interpretation.

1987 Edwards v. Aguillard: US Supreme Court strikes down Louisiana's Creationism Act as unconstitutional.

What is striking about the Young Earth Creationist movement (formalized in Henry Morris and John Whitcomb's The Genesis Flood, 1961) is how historically novel it is. Its insistence on a literal six-day creation and a universe under 10,000 years old would have puzzled Augustine, confounded medieval scholars like Thomas Aquinas (who read creation days as logical rather than temporal categories), and alarmed the Calvinist theologians it often claims as ancestors. John Calvin himself wrote: "Moses makes mention of the visible world … it would have been absurd to speak of the creation of the world in terms beyond ordinary understanding."2John Calvin, Commentary on Genesis 1:6, trans. John King (1847). Calvin regularly deferred to scientific expertise in cosmological matters, a position that later Calvinist traditions often ignored.

The politicization of Genesis creation texts has less to do with ancient cosmology than with modern anxieties: about evolution, the authority of science, the stability of cultural identity, and the meaning of human dignity. The text became a battlefield only when those battles needed a battlefield.

Passage II • Matthew 19:24

The Eye of the Needle: Wealth and Salvation

On manufactured loopholes and the astonishing persistence of a verse that means exactly what it says

Again I tell you, it is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle than for someone who is rich to enter the kingdom of God. Matthew 19:24 (NIV); parallel Mark 10:25, Luke 18:25

This is, by most reckonings, the most uncomfortable verse in the Gospels. It has generated more exegetical creativity — most of it strained — than almost any other saying of Jesus. The discomfort is not a modern phenomenon; it was immediate and visceral when Jesus spoke it, and his disciples responded with the same shock their interpreters have managed ever since: "Who then can be saved?" (Matthew 19:25).

The most persistent escape route is the "Eye of the Needle Gate" — a purported small gate in Jerusalem's walls through which camels could squeeze if made to kneel and unloaded. This gate, cited in sermons and Sunday school lessons across denominational lines, is entirely fictitious. There is no archaeological, historical, or textual evidence for such a gate. No ancient source mentions it. The legend appears to originate with an eleventh-century commentary attributed (almost certainly falsely) to the Byzantine scholar Theophylact.3The fictitious gate first appears in discussions attributed to Theophylact of Ohrid (c. 1050–1108 CE), though the earliest certain references in modern Protestant literature date to the nineteenth century. See Garrett, Commentary on Luke (1874), which was likely the vector of popular transmission. It was invented to make the verse manageable.

Origen, Jerome, John Chrysostom, Thomas Aquinas, Martin Luther, and John Calvin all took the verse at face value: it is a hyperbolic statement about impossibility, a common rhetorical form in first-century Jewish discourse. (The Talmud uses an identical construction: "An elephant can pass through a needle's eye" — b. Berakhot 55b.) The camel is large; the needle is small; the point is that something impossible becomes possible only through divine action. Luther wrote bluntly: "I cannot make the rich man's salvation any easier than Christ does."

"The text is quite plain: it says camel, and it means camel. Every attempt to circumvent this is the voice not of Scripture but of comfort." — John Calvin, Commentary on Matthew 19:24 (paraphrase)

The modern prosperity gospel — the teaching that financial wealth is a sign of divine favor — represents the most thoroughgoing contemporary reversal of this verse's intent. Associated with figures including Kenneth Copeland, Joel Osteen, and Creflo Dollar, prosperity theology reframes Jesus's warning as a blessing: God wants you to be rich, and your faith is measured in your net worth. What the verse says is an obstacle becomes, in this reading, an aspiration. This reversal is not merely a misreading; it is the inversion of what the text's context makes unmistakably clear — the passage begins with the Rich Young Ruler being told to sell everything he has and give it to the poor.

Passage III • Matthew 22:21

Render Unto Caesar: The Subversive Coin

On a trick question, a graven image, and two thousand years of arguing about church and state

Render therefore unto Caesar the things which are Caesar's; and unto God the things that are God's. Matthew 22:21 (KJV); parallel Mark 12:17, Luke 20:25

The context is a trap. The Pharisees and Herodians, unusual allies, approach Jesus with a question designed to impale him on either horn of a dilemma: if he says pay the tax, he is a Roman collaborator; if he says don't, he is a revolutionary. Jesus's request for a denarius — a coin he does not himself possess, suggesting deliberately calculated poverty — and his question about whose image it bears shifts the entire frame of the debate.

The Greek verb matters enormously here. The standard English rendering is "render" or "give," but the Greek word is ἀπόδοτε (apodote) — "give back." It carries the connotation of returning something that already belongs to its owner. Caesar's image (εἰκών, eikon) is on the coin; Caesar can have his coin back. But the clear implied question is: Whose image is on human beings? Genesis 1:26 answers: the image of God (בְּצַלְמֵנוּ, betzalmenu). The saying is not a command to obey the state; it may be a near-subversive reminder that ultimate allegiance belongs elsewhere.4See N.T. Wright, Jesus and the Victory of God (1996), pp. 502–507; also Richard Horsley, Jesus and Empire (2003), who reads the saying as a studied refusal to validate Roman taxation authority.

Constantine's conversion (312 CE) made this verse politically indispensable. The newly Christianized Empire needed a theology of state obedience, and "Render unto Caesar" provided it — reread now not as a wry deferral but as a positive command to civic compliance. Augustine's Two Cities framework, and later Luther's Two Kingdoms doctrine, both tried to structure the tension between divine and earthly authority using this text. Luther, during the Peasants' War of 1525, famously used it to urge submission to violent state suppression — one of the most consequential and controversial applications in Christian history.

The American experiment gave this verse a new context. Roger Williams, founder of Rhode Island and an unlikely architect of religious liberty, argued that the mixing of civil and ecclesiastical power corrupted both — a reading that influenced the establishment clause of the First Amendment. Yet the same verse was cited by loyalists during the American Revolution to argue for continued obedience to the British Crown. The text accommodates both with apparent ease, which tells us something important about how it functions: less as instruction and more as a mirror.

Passage IV • 1 Timothy 2:12 & 1 Corinthians 14:34–35

"I Do Not Permit a Woman": The Silencing Texts

On a word that appears once in the New Testament, a probable interpolation, and a female apostle whose name was changed

I do not permit a woman to teach or to exercise authority over a man; she is to remain quiet. 1 Timothy 2:12 (ESV)

Two passages in the Pauline corpus have done more institutional damage to women's religious leadership than perhaps any others in Western history: 1 Timothy 2:12 and 1 Corinthians 14:34–35. These verses have been used to bar women from ordination, exclude them from preaching, and restrict their roles in worship across centuries and denominations. The historical-critical scholarship on both passages, however, is considerably more unsettled than their confident deployment suggests.

The Greek word translated "authority" in 1 Timothy 2:12 is αὐθεντεῖν (authentein). This is a hapax legomenon — a word that appears exactly once in the entire New Testament. Because it is so rare, its meaning must be reconstructed from outside the NT, where it appears in contexts ranging from "to dominate" to "to murder" to "to be the originator of."5See Leland Wilshire's study of 314 non-biblical uses of authentein in Bulletin of Biblical Research 1 (1991): 25–44. The word carries strongly negative connotations in pre-Christian usage, suggesting the verse may prohibit abusive domination rather than all forms of authority. The major English translations — KJV, NIV, ESV, NRSV — all render it as "authority," but that choice is contested, not settled.

The case of 1 Corinthians 14:34–35 is even more textually complicated. Many New Testament scholars — including Gordon Fee, perhaps the foremost Pauline scholar of the twentieth century — argue that these verses are a later interpolation, not written by Paul at all.6Gordon Fee, The First Epistle to the Corinthians (NICNT, 1987), pp. 699–708. Fee argues the verses contradict Paul's endorsement of women praying and prophesying in 1 Cor. 11:5, and their placement varies between manuscripts — appearing after v. 33 in some and after v. 40 in others, a classic sign of marginal notation later incorporated into the text. In the oldest manuscripts, these verses appear in different locations — a hallmark of text that originated as a marginal gloss and was later inserted into the body of the letter at different points by different scribes.

The counterevidence within the Pauline corpus is striking. Romans 16:7 names Ἰουνίαν (Iounian) — a woman — as "outstanding among the apostles." For over a millennium, commentators from Chrysostom to Origen acknowledged Junia as a female apostle. Then, beginning with the thirteenth-century scholar Giles of Rome, the name was masculinized to "Junias" — a name that does not otherwise appear anywhere in ancient literature — and maintained in that form in most English translations from the Reformation through the mid-twentieth century. The NRSV (1989) restored "Junia." The suppression of a female apostle's name across seven centuries of translation is not an accident; it is a policy.

Passage V • Genesis 9:20–27, Ephesians 6:5, Philemon

Scripture and Slavery: The Texts That Justified the Unjustifiable

On the Curse of Ham, the complicity of translators, and a short letter that has been read every possible way

Slaves, obey your earthly masters with fear and trembling, with a sincere heart, as you would Christ. Ephesians 6:5 (ESV)

No episode in American religious history exposes the mechanics of motivated interpretation more nakedly than the antebellum debate over slavery. Both sides claimed the same Bible. Both produced systematic theologies. One side, it turned out, was reading the text through the lens of economic interest; the other was reading it through the lens of human dignity. Understanding how the pro-slavery reading worked — and why it was ultimately rejected — is among the most important lessons biblical interpretation offers.

The cornerstone of pro-slavery theology was the "Curse of Ham" in Genesis 9:20–27. After Noah's drunken episode, he curses his son Canaan — not Ham, and not all of Ham's descendants. The text is unmistakably specific: Canaan, one of Ham's four sons, is cursed to be "a slave of slaves" to his brothers. There is no mention of skin color, no mention of Africa, and no logical pathway from "Canaan" to "all Africans." Nevertheless, Thornton Stringfellow's enormously influential Scriptural and Statistical Views in Favor of Slavery (1856) treated this passage as divine sanction for race-based chattel slavery — a reading Frederick Douglass correctly identified as "a gross fraud."7Frederick Douglass, My Bondage and My Freedom (1855), Chapter 23: "The slaveholders' Bible is not the Christians' Bible." Douglass spent considerable energy demonstrating that pro-slavery readings required willful misreading of the text itself.

The Pauline household codes — Ephesians 6:5–8, Colossians 3:22–25, Titus 2:9–10 — commanded enslaved persons to obey their masters. Slaveholders' theologians read these as divine authorization of the institution itself. Abolitionists replied that Paul was describing, not prescribing — offering pastoral counsel to those already in bondage within an existing social system, while Galatians 3:28 ("there is neither slave nor free") announced the eschatological abolition of all such categories. The same letter to Philemon — Paul's appeal to a slaveholder to receive the formerly enslaved Onesimus "no longer as a slave, but better than a slave, as a dear brother" — was read by pro-slavery interpreters as Paul returning a runaway slave to his legal owner, and by abolitionists as a coded appeal for manumission.8The scholarly literature on Philemon is vast. See N.T. Wright, Colossians and Philemon (TNTC, 1986); also Allen Callahan, Embassy of Onesimus: The Letter of Paul to Philemon (1997), who argues Onesimus was not a runaway slave but an estranged brother.

"Between the Christianity of this land and the Christianity of Christ, I recognize the widest possible difference — so wide that to receive the one as good, pure, and holy, is of necessity to reject the other as bad, corrupt, and wicked." — Frederick Douglass, Narrative of the Life of Frederick Douglass (1845)

What the antebellum debate reveals is that the question was never really about the Bible. It was about what kind of country, what kind of economy, and what kind of humanity the interpreters were committed to defending. The text became the battlefield; the battle was over something else entirely. Charles Finney, Harriet Beecher Stowe, and the abolitionist ministers who read the same Bible arrived at the opposite conclusion — not because their reading was more sophisticated, but because their commitments were different. The lesson endures: the most important question to ask of any biblical interpreter is not "What does the text say?" but "What do they stand to gain?"

Passage VI • Genesis 19, Leviticus 18:22, Romans 1:26–27

Sodom's Real Sin: The Proof Texts Examined

On hospitality violation, the word "abomination" and its shellfish problem, and a Greek neologism whose meaning no one can agree on

You shall not lie with a male as with a woman; it is an abomination. Leviticus 18:22 (ESV)

The story of Sodom (Genesis 19) has lent its name to a category of sexual behavior for so long that it is easy to forget what the text actually says about the city's offense. The men of Sodom demand that Lot's angelic visitors be brought out so that they may "know" (יָדַע, yada) them — a word that can mean sexual intercourse but also simply "get acquainted with." What is unambiguous is the threat: a violent mob demanding access to strangers under a host's protection. The violation of ancient hospitality codes — among the gravest sins in Near Eastern culture — is at the structural center of the narrative.

The prophetic tradition reads the story this way explicitly. Ezekiel 16:49 identifies Sodom's sin without ambiguity: "Now this was the sin of your sister Sodom: She and her daughters were arrogant, overfed and unconcerned; they did not help the poor and needy." Isaiah 1:10–17 and Jeremiah 23:14 cite Sodom as a symbol of injustice and falsehood. Jesus himself uses Sodom as a symbol of inhospitality to messengers (Matthew 10:14–15, Luke 10:12), not sexual sin. The sexual interpretation is not primary; it is a later overlay.9See John Boswell, Christianity, Social Tolerance, and Homosexuality (1980), Chapter 4. While Boswell's broader argument is contested, his textual analysis of the Sodom narrative's original semantic frame is widely accepted in scholarly literature.

Leviticus 18:22 is more explicit, but its context complicates simple application. The Hebrew word translated "abomination" is תּוֹעֵבָה (to'evah), which in Leviticus designates not absolute moral evil but a category of ritual impurity — things that make one unclean within the specific covenant context of ancient Israel. The same word applies to eating shellfish (Deuteronomy 14:3), wearing mixed fabrics (Deuteronomy 22:11), and remarrying a divorced wife (Deuteronomy 24:4). The Holiness Code of Leviticus (chapters 17–26) is a comprehensive purity system for an ancient Near Eastern community, not a portable moral law for all cultures and times — a point consistently made by scholars across theological positions.

The word ἀρσενοκοῖται (arsenokoitai), which appears in 1 Corinthians 6:9 and 1 Timothy 1:10, is a compound Paul appears to have coined himself — possibly derived from the Septuagint (Greek OT) rendering of Leviticus 18:22. Because it is so rare, its precise meaning is genuinely uncertain. Dale Martin of Yale's analysis of the word's subsequent usage in Greek literature finds that it appears in contexts concerning economic exploitation — "the arsenokoitai will oppress the widow and orphan" — rather than consensual same-sex relations.10Dale B. Martin, Sex and the Single Savior: Gender and Sexuality in Biblical Interpretation (2006), pp. 37–50. Martin's argument is that the Greek context of arsenokoitai in non-biblical literature consistently involves economic exploitation rather than consensual same-sex activity. Robert Gagnon, the leading scholarly opponent of gay-affirming interpretations, disputes this reading vigorously. The debate is ongoing, technically sophisticated, and has not produced scholarly consensus.

The modern translation of the word as "homosexuals" — first appearing in the RSV (1946) and then the NIV (1984) — introduced an anachronism. "Homosexuality" as a concept (a stable orientation, not merely an act) was coined by the Hungarian journalist Karl-Maria Kertbeny in 1869. Whatever Paul meant by arsenokoitai, he did not mean what modern readers understand by "homosexual." The translation choice embeds a nineteenth-century psychological category into a first-century text — a form of context collapse with enormous consequences for millions of people.

Passage VII • Deuteronomy 20:16–17, Joshua 1–12

The Conquest of Canaan: Sacred Violence and Its Progeny

On herem, what archaeology says, and how holy war became a portable justification for colonial genocide

But in the cities of these peoples that the Lord your God is giving you for an inheritance, you shall save alive nothing that breathes, but you shall devote them to complete destruction. Deuteronomy 20:16–17 (ESV)

The conquest narratives of Joshua present what may be the most morally troubling material in the Hebrew Bible: divine commands to exterminate entire populations. The Hebrew concept at the center of these texts is חֵרֶם (herem) — devotion to destruction, the total consecration of conquered peoples and their property to God by annihilation. Joshua 6:17 applies it to Jericho: "the city and all that is in it are devoted to the Lord for destruction." These passages have had a long and bloody afterlife.

The archaeological record complicates the historical picture significantly. Jericho, where the Israelites were commanded to destroy every living thing, shows no evidence of destruction or occupation during the Late Bronze Age period when the conquest narratives are traditionally dated. Kathleen Kenyon's exhaustive excavations (1952–1958) found that Jericho was essentially unoccupied during the relevant period. The same applies to Ai (Joshua 8), which appears to have been abandoned for centuries before any Israelite presence.11Kathleen Kenyon, Digging Up Jericho (1957); Israel Finkelstein and Neil Asher Silberman, The Bible Unearthed (2001), pp. 72–96, provide the most accessible scholarly synthesis of archaeological findings related to the conquest narrative. This does not resolve the theological problem — the text commands genocide whether or not it happened — but it changes the historical framing.

The conquest narrative became a portable justification for colonial violence in ways that would have appalled many of its medieval interpreters. The Crusaders who massacred Jerusalem's inhabitants in 1099 — "the streets were running with blood," wrote Raymond of Aguilers — understood themselves as following a Joshua typology. Spanish Conquistadors justified New World genocides with similar language. English settlers in America, particularly in New England, read the indigenous peoples of the continent as the new Canaanites — to be dispossessed or destroyed as God commanded. The Puritan divine Cotton Mather described King Philip's War (1675–76) as a providential fulfillment of the conquest narrative. This is not theological appropriation; it is precise application of the text's logic to new circumstances.

"The day the text is read as authorizing contemporary violence is the day interpretation has become indistinguishable from propaganda." — Walter Brueggemann, Theology of the Old Testament (1997)

Modern scholarship has offered several frameworks for addressing herem without either celebrating it or dismissing the texts entirely. René Girard's mimetic theory suggests the conquest narrative encodes an archaic scapegoating mechanism that the prophetic tradition gradually dismantles. Walter Wink's reading of divine violence as reflective of Israel's cultural and psychological limitations rather than God's actual commands offers another route. What is not available, without extraordinary interpretive gymnastics, is a reading that treats these texts as straightforwardly prescriptive for contemporary communities.

Passage VIII • Matthew 7:1

"Judge Not": Scripture's Most Misquoted Verse

On logs and specks, the full context, and how a call for self-examination became a prohibition on discernment

Judge not, that you be not judged. Matthew 7:1 (ESV)

"Judge not" is almost certainly the most frequently quoted verse in contemporary American popular culture — and almost certainly the most consistently misquoted. The truncation of Matthew 7:1 from its context in the Sermon on the Mount has produced a sentiment that Jesus would likely not recognize: a blanket prohibition on moral discernment. What the passage actually says, when read in full, is considerably more demanding than that.

The very next verse provides the qualification: "For with the judgment you pronounce you will be judged, and with the measure you use it will be measured to you" (7:2). Verses 3–5 offer the famous log-and-speck image: "Why do you see the speck that is in your brother's eye, but do not notice the log that is in your own eye? … You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your brother's eye." The word "then" is critical: it is not a prohibition on removing specks. It is an insistence on doing so with moral integrity — first examine yourself.

The broader New Testament context is equally clear that judgment within a community of faith is expected and necessary. John 7:24 commands: "Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment." First Corinthians 5 has Paul demanding the expulsion of a community member engaged in sexual immorality, and explicitly criticizing the Corinthian church for not judging: "Is it not those inside the church whom you are to judge?" (5:12). Galatians 6:1 counsels correction of erring members. The New Testament church is not an unjudging community; it is a community meant to exercise judgment with humility, self-awareness, and accountability.

The Weaponization Pattern

Matthew 7:1 in its truncated form functions primarily as a conversation-stopper — deployed most effectively by those who wish to deflect accountability for their own conduct. The irony is that the verse, properly read, is an intensification of the standard of moral seriousness it is used to evade. It does not say "mind your own business"; it says "examine yourself first, then act with clear eyes."

The popularity of the truncated version tracks the rise of individualism and the cultural suspicion of moral authority. "Judge not" has become a secularized principle — a kind of moral non-interference treaty — that the text itself does not support. This represents a genuine irony: the verse is most often cited by people who have no particular interest in the rest of the Sermon on the Mount, using the language of scripture to argue for a position (radical non-judgment) that scripture does not endorse.

Passage IX • Revelation 13:18, the Book of Revelation

The Apocalypse of John: Politics in Prophetic Dress

On Nero's number, the genre of apocalypse, and how a critique of Roman imperialism became a roadmap for the end of the world

This calls for wisdom: let the one who has understanding calculate the number of the beast, for it is the number of a man, and his number is 666. Revelation 13:18 (ESV)

Revelation is the most misread book in the New Testament — which is remarkable, given that it announces its own genre in its opening word: Ἀποκάλυψις (apokalypsis), "unveiling" or "disclosure." Jewish apocalyptic literature, of which Revelation is one example among many (see also Daniel, 1 Enoch, 4 Ezra, 2 Baruch), was a crisis genre — produced by communities under intense political persecution to encode resistance and hope in symbolic language that Roman authorities would not immediately comprehend. Reading Revelation as a blueprint for twenty-first-century events misses this foundational interpretive context entirely.

The "number of the beast," 666, is a prime example of the genre's mechanics. The ancient practice of gematria — assigning numerical values to letters — was widespread in both Jewish and Greek cultures. "Nero Caesar" transliterated into Hebrew letters (נְרוֹן קֵסַר, neron qesar) yields the numerical value 666. This calculation is well-established in scholarship and explains why some manuscripts give the number as 616 — the spelling of "Nero Caesar" in Latin rather than Hebrew produces that variant.12The Nero identification was proposed by various nineteenth-century scholars and is now standard in biblical scholarship. See David Aune, Revelation 6–16 (WBC 52B, 1998), pp. 769–773 for a thorough treatment. The 616 variant appears in Papyrus 115 and is cited by Irenaeus as a known reading. The beast is Rome. The whore of Babylon (Revelation 17) is Rome, sitting on seven hills. The seven churches are actual first-century churches in Asia Minor. The book is an encoded political document about the Neronian and/or Domitianic persecutions, not a prediction about microchips or the United Nations.

1970 Hal Lindsey, The Late Great Planet Earth: applies Revelation to Cold War geopolitics. Sells 35 million copies.
1995–2007 Left Behind series by LaHaye and Jenkins sells 65 million copies; enshrines dispensationalist futurism in evangelical popular culture.

The preterist reading — which holds that most of Revelation's prophecies were fulfilled in the first century, with the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 CE — is supported by scholars including N.T. Wright and is arguably the most historically grounded position. The futurist reading, which projects Revelation's images onto our own contemporary moment, is the most recent of the major interpretive traditions and has no parallel in Christian interpretation before the nineteenth century. Darby's dispensationalism — the rapture, the tribulation, the literal thousand-year reign of Christ — was, in historical terms, an innovation, not a recovery of ancient truth.

Passage X • Matthew 26:11, Mark 14:7, John 12:8

"The Poor You Will Always Have": Economic Theology

On expensive perfume, a Deuteronomy allusion, and the most lazily deployed verse in policy debates

For you always have the poor with you, but you will not always have me. Matthew 26:11 (ESV)

Few verses have been more reliably deployed to halt discussions of poverty policy than this single sentence. In its truncated form — "the poor you will always have with you" — it has been cited against welfare programs, foreign aid proposals, redistributive tax policy, and social safety nets with a rhetorical authority that the full passage simply does not support. The context, and the deliberate allusion it encodes, inverts the usual deployment entirely.

The scene is Bethany, days before the crucifixion. An unnamed woman (identified as Mary of Bethany in John's version) anoints Jesus with nard — a Himalayan plant extract worth approximately 300 denarii, nearly a year's wages for a laborer. The disciples, with Judas identified as the objector in John's account, protest the waste: the money should have been given to the poor. Jesus's response — "you always have the poor with you" — is not economic fatalism. It is a deliberate quotation of Deuteronomy 15:11.

Deuteronomy 15:11 reads, in full: "For there will never cease to be poor in the land. Therefore I command you, 'You shall open wide your hand to your brother, to the needy and to the poor, in your land.'" The verse Jesus cites is immediately followed in its original context by a commandment for generous, radical economic redistribution. Any first-century Jewish listener who recognized the allusion — and many would — would hear not permission to ignore the poor but a reminder of an ongoing, urgent obligation toward them.13Craig Blomberg, Neither Poverty nor Riches: A Biblical Theology of Material Possessions (1999), pp. 132–138, provides a thorough treatment of the Deuteronomy allusion. The same point is made by Luise Schottroff, The Parables of Jesus (2006).

The Truncation Problem

The verse as quoted in policy debates consists of seven words extracted from a sentence that is itself an allusion to a larger passage commanding economic generosity. The quotation not only misrepresents Matthew 26:11; it inverts Deuteronomy 15:11. This is not interpretation — it is the weaponization of a half-sentence against the full meaning of both texts it draws upon.

The liberation theology tradition, developed in Latin America in the 1960s–70s by theologians including Gustavo Gutiérrez, Ignacio Ellacuría, and Leonardo Boff, reads the entirety of Jesus's economic teaching as constituting a "preferential option for the poor" — not as a secondary social concern but as a central hermeneutical principle. This is contested by other theological traditions, but it represents a reading grounded in textual seriousness rather than apologetics for existing distributions of wealth.

Meta-Analysis

Patterns of Contention

Ten passages, twenty centuries of argument, and a remarkably consistent set of patterns. Understanding what makes a biblical passage controversial — and what that controversy reveals about the interpreters rather than the text — is more practically valuable than any individual reading.

⚖️
Power Serves Itself
In nearly every case examined here, the interpretation that benefits the powerful has been the dominant one — until the powerless organized to challenge it. This is not coincidence.
✂️
The Truncation Problem
Verses removed from their immediate context — "judge not," "the poor you always have," "render unto Caesar" — reliably reverse or contradict the meaning of the full passage.
🔤
Translation as Argument
Translating authentein as "authority" rather than "domineer"; translating arsenokoitai as "homosexuals" rather than "sexual exploiters": every choice embeds a theology.
📅
Novelty Disguised as Tradition
Young Earth Creationism, the pre-tribulation rapture, and the prosperity gospel are all modern innovations presented as recoveries of ancient truth. Their antiquity is invented.
🌍
Context Collapse
Applying Levitical purity codes to contemporary ethics, reading Revelation as a map of 2024 geopolitics, treating household codes as timeless gender policy — all involve catastrophic context collapse.
🪞
The Mirror Effect
Every generation finds in the Bible's contested passages a reflection of its own anxieties. The text does not create the battle; it becomes its chosen ground.

Translation Politics: Language as Power

The history of Bible translation is, in part, a history of political control. Jerome's Latin Vulgate (completed c. 405 CE) gave Rome a monopoly on the authoritative text that lasted a thousand years. When John Wycliffe translated the Bible into English in the 1380s, the institutional church condemned it and posthumously burned his bones. William Tyndale was strangled and burned for his 1526 English New Testament. Translation was understood — correctly — as a democratizing act that threatened hierarchical control of meaning.

The King James Version (1611), commissioned by James I, was explicitly a conservative project: it retained episcopalian ecclesiastical vocabulary ("bishop" rather than "overseer," "church" rather than "congregation") against the preferences of Puritan scholars. The revisers were instructed to preserve "the old Ecclesiastical Words" — a political decision embedded in what became the most influential English text in history.14Alister McGrath, In the Beginning: The Story of the King James Bible (2001), pp. 170–184. The Fifteen Rules issued to the translators included explicit instructions about ecclesiastical vocabulary that had direct political implications for church governance debates of the period.

Contemporary translation committees are not neutral either. The NIV (New International Version), produced under evangelical sponsorship, reflects conservative theological commitments in its handling of gender language, the arsenokoitai problem, and numerous other contested terms. The NRSV (New Revised Standard Version), produced under mainline Protestant and Catholic auspices, reflects different commitments. The translation you read shapes the theology you encounter — and most readers never know this is happening.

Certainty as Danger

The most consistently destructive force in the history of biblical interpretation is not ignorance but certainty. The slaveholders knew what the Bible said about slavery. The inquisitors knew what it said about heresy. The architects of apartheid's theological justification — the Dutch Reformed Church's report Ras, Volk en Nasie (Race, People and Nation, 1974) — knew what it said about nations. In every case, they were wrong, and their wrongness was invisible to them precisely because they were certain.

The scholarly apparatus of biblical criticism — manuscript tradition, textual criticism, archaeology, ancient languages, comparative literature — exists not to undermine faith but to introduce productive uncertainty where false confidence has done damage. Knowing that authentein is a hapax legomenon does not tell you what to believe about women's ordination; it tells you that the argument is more complicated than confident certainty suggests. That is not nothing. That is, in fact, everything.

The Takeaway

Reading Without Weaponizing

Every bias is invisible to its host. This is not a moral failing unique to slaveholders or inquisitors; it is the ordinary condition of human cognition. We begin with what we need the text to say and work backward to justify it — and the process feels, from the inside, exactly like honest interpretation. The question is not whether we do this, but how to do it less, and how to notice when we're doing it most.

The first discipline is reading against yourself. Find the interpretations of contested passages that most challenge your existing commitments and engage them seriously before dismissing them. If you are a theological conservative, read Elaine Pagels on early Christian diversity. If you are a theological progressive, read Robert Gagnon on the sexuality texts. Not to change your mind necessarily, but to discover whether your confidence can survive an honest encounter with its best-informed opposition.

The second discipline is intellectual humility about what you cannot know. We do not know what Paul meant by arsenokoitai. We do not know whether the authentein passage in 1 Timothy was written by Paul or inserted later. We do not know whether the Genesis creation accounts were meant as cosmology or as counter-narrative to Babylonian creation myths. Honest scholarship says "we do not know" in these places, and the weight of two thousand years of confident misreadings should make us grateful for that honesty, not suspicious of it.

"The mark of intellectual maturity is the ability to hold a question open. The mark of manipulation is the insistence that the question is already closed." — After Paul Ricoeur, Interpretation Theory (1976)

The third discipline is attending to who benefits. Before accepting any interpretation of a contested passage, ask: Who does this reading serve? Who does it harm? Who is proposing it, and what do they stand to gain from its acceptance? This is not a hermeneutic of suspicion for its own sake; it is the application to texts of the ordinary wisdom we apply in every other domain of life where interests are at stake.

The fourth, and perhaps hardest, discipline is distinguishing the text from its use. The Bible did not justify American chattel slavery; people did, using the Bible as their instrument. The Bible did not prohibit women's ordination; institutions did, deploying selected texts as their warrant. The difference matters — not to rescue the text from its history, but to understand how powerful institutions use texts as laundering operations, giving the sheen of divine authority to what is in fact a human arrangement of power. Recognizing the laundry cycle is the beginning of reading honestly.

These ten passages have been contested precisely because they touch the pressure points of every age: who holds power, who is included, what obligations we owe the poor, how we relate to the state, what we make of violence and difference and the end of things. The arguments will continue. New translations will embed new commitments. New political crises will generate new readings. The cycle does not stop.

What can change is the quality of our participation in it. The scholar who knows that yom has multiple meanings, that Junia was a female apostle, that the "eye of the needle gate" was invented — this scholar brings a harder question to the argument, and harder questions are the beginning of better answers.

The Bible's contested passages are not a problem to be solved. They are an ongoing seminar in the difficulty of reading honestly — a seminar whose stakes, across twenty centuries, have been extraordinarily high. The lesson they offer, collectively, is not a theological conclusion but a posture: approach the text with rigor, approach your own certainties with suspicion, and hold with genuine openness the possibility that the tradition you've inherited has, at some point, been beautifully and consequentially wrong.

That is not the end of faith. It may be where faith worth having begins.
