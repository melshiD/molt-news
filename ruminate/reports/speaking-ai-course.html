Program Description

The public is hungry for someone who can explain artificial intelligence honestly, clearly, and without either hype or doom. School boards want talks. Rotary clubs want talks. Libraries want talks. Churches, unions, chambers of commerce, retirement communities, parent groups, city councils, and corporate lunch-and-learns all want talks. Most of what they get is either a sales pitch, a panic, or a lecture from someone who forgot that the audience is not other engineers.

This 15-week professional program prepares you to fill that gap. You will leave able to do three things well: (1) explain current AI — what it is, what it can and can't do, where it's going — to a non-technical audience without being wrong; (2) deliver that explanation as an engaging talk that people remember, quote, and recommend; and (3) operate as a working speaker — booking, pricing, contracting, marketing, and building a sustainable practice that serves your community and pays you fairly.

This is not a course in AI engineering. It is a course in becoming the person your town calls when it needs someone to explain what is happening.

Who This Program Is For

  • Educators, librarians, and clergy who want to inform their communities
  • Working professionals (lawyers, doctors, accountants, HR leaders) who already get asked "what should we do about AI?" and want to answer well
  • Journalists, podcasters, and writers shifting into AI as a beat
  • Existing public speakers adding AI to their topic list
  • Career-changers building a speaking practice from scratch

Prerequisites: None. You do not need a technical background. You do need the willingness to read carefully, speak often, and accept video critique.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of this program, you will be able to:

  1. Explain core AI concepts (LLMs, training, inference, agents, evaluation, alignment, deployment) accurately at a 9th-grade reading level.
  2. Distinguish what is established from what is speculative when discussing AI in public.
  3. Build a 45-minute signature talk plus three derivative formats (20-minute keynote, 60-minute workshop, 5-minute media hit).
  4. Deliver that material engagingly to skeptical, fearful, curious, and hostile audiences.
  5. Handle Q&A on hard questions (jobs, safety, deepfakes, education, copyright, surveillance) without either evading or overclaiming.
  6. Run a speaking practice: pricing, booking, contracts, invoicing, marketing, and follow-through.
  7. Sustain your knowledge as the field changes month to month.

Unit I — Knowing What You're Talking About

  • The state of the field in plain language: what an LLM is, what training is, what inference is, what an agent is. No jargon left undefined.

  • How to follow the field without drowning: a curated reading and listening diet.

Week 2 — What AI Can Actually Do Right Now

  • Capabilities demo night. Live, on screen, in front of the class. We use frontier systems to do real tasks and watch them succeed and fail.
  • Why public talks go stale: the gap between what a model could do 18 months ago and what it can do today.
  • Assignment 1: Build your "capabilities deck" — 10 short, current, reproducible demos you can run live.

Week 3 — What AI Can't Do, and What It Probably Won't Do Soon

  • The honest limits: hallucination, brittleness, context windows, evaluation gaps, deployment gaps. Why "it can pass the bar exam" doesn't mean "it can practice law."
  • Distinguishing what's hard from what's expensive from what's actually impossible.
  • Reading: model cards and system cards as primary sources.

Week 4 — The Hot Topics You Will Be Asked About

  • Jobs and labor. Education and cheating. Deepfakes and elections. Copyright. Energy and water. Safety and existential risk. Privacy. Bias.
  • For each: what is established, what is contested, what is speculative. Assignment 2 due: a one-page brief on each of the eight topics in your own voice (8 pages total).

Unit II — Translating

Week 5 — The Curse of Knowledge and How to Escape It

  • Why experts give bad talks. Heath brothers' SUCCESs framework. The "what's the one thing they remember tomorrow" test.
  • The analogy workshop. Which analogies work (autocomplete, library, intern). Which mislead (brain, person, soul). Why your analogy is a policy choice.

Week 6 — Demos, Visuals, and Live Use

  • Live demo craft: how to run a model on stage without it eating your talk. The backup plan. The graceful failure.
  • Slides that help vs slides that hide you. The one-image slide. The strategic blank screen.
  • Speech Lab 1: Each student delivers a 7-minute "one concept" talk with one live demo.

Week 7 — The Story Layer

  • Every good AI talk is a story about people, not a description of a technology. Where to find the stories: your community, your work, the news.
  • Three story shapes that fit AI talks: the surprise, the dilemma, and the slow change.
  • Speech Lab 2: Each student delivers a 10-minute talk built around one true story. Assignment 3 due: outline of your signature 45-minute talk.

Unit III — Stage Craft

Week 8 — Voice, Body, Pacing, Pause

  • Workshop with a guest voice coach. Diaphragmatic breath, vocal range, end-of-sentence drop, the strategic pause.
  • On-camera vs in-room. Different rules. We practice both.

Week 9 — Reading the Room

  • The Rotary lunch crowd is not the high-school assembly is not the corporate offsite is not the church basement. How each one signals engagement, and how to adapt in real time.
  • The skeptical engineer in row three. The frightened parent in row eight. The mayor in the front row. How to speak so all three stay with you.

Week 10 — Q&A Under Fire

  • Q&A is the part of a talk that gets you booked again or never again.
  • Handling: the technical gotcha, the political ambush, the rambling non-question, the genuinely hard question you don't know the answer to, the question from a child.
  • Speech Lab 3: Mock talks with a stacked hostile Q&A. Classmates are coached in advance to be difficult. Assignment 4 due: full draft of signature talk (~5,000 words).

Unit IV — The Practice

Week 11 — Building Your Topic and Your Brand

  • What is your wedge? "AI for school boards." "AI for rural small business." "AI for clergy." "AI for nurses." A niche is a gift to the bookers.
  • Your one-page speaker sheet. Your bio in three lengths. Your headshot. Your demo reel.
  • Guest: a working speaker who books $5K, $15K, and $40K dates explains what each tier looks like and how she got there.

Week 12 — Booking, Pricing, Contracts

  • How speakers actually get booked: referrals, bureaus, inbound web, direct outreach.

  • Reading and negotiating a speaker contract. Travel, AV riders, recording rights, exclusivity clauses, cancellation. Guest: an entertainment lawyer.

Week 13 — Marketing Without Being Insufferable

  • LinkedIn, newsletter, podcast appearances, op-eds. The 90/10 rule: 90% useful, 10% promotional.
  • The community engine: how to give 6–10 free or reduced-rate talks a year to libraries, schools, and civic groups in a way that generates both impact and paid work.
  • Assignment 5 due: complete speaker kit (one-pager, bio set, headshot, demo reel link, fee sheet, contract template).

Session Overview

Theme: The state of the field in plain language + establishing who we are as speakers Core tension to surface: You can't explain what you don't understand. You can't understand everything. Session Plan (180 minutes)

6:00–6:20 — Room Opens, Baseline Video Recording

Students arrive and immediately record their Day 1 video before anything is taught. This is intentional. Set up two filming stations in opposite corners of the room (phones on tripods work fine). Students take turns: 3 minutes, no notes, explain AI to a smart 14-year-old.

Logistics note: Collect file names or have students email themselves the video and send the link to the LMS immediately. This video is compared against their Week 15 talk and is required for the certificate. Impress on them: it doesn't need to be good. It needs to be honest.

What to tell them as they arrive:

"Before we introduce ourselves, before I say anything about this course, I want you to go over to one of those phone stands and record yourself for three minutes explaining what AI is to a 14-year-old. Don't look anything up. Don't prepare. Just talk. This is your baseline — the 'before' photo. It's not graded and you don't have to share it with anyone but me."

6:20–6:35 — Introductions and Program Overview

Go around the room. Each person: name, what they do, and one word for how they feel about AI right now. (Common answers: curious, anxious, excited, skeptical, overwhelmed. Write them on the board. Return to this list in Week 15.)

Brief program overview: five units, fifteen weeks, one showcase. Emphasize that this is a trade school, not a lecture course — they will speak in front of people every session starting in Week 6, and even before that, they'll be talking constantly.

6:35–7:30 — Core Content: What We're Actually Talking About

Teach the following four concepts without jargon. Use the board. Draw pictures. Pause for questions constantly.

1. What a large language model is (15 minutes)

Start with what it's not: it is not a search engine. It is not a database. It is not a person thinking.

The working definition to put on the board:

An LLM is a system trained to predict the next most likely word (token), given everything that came before it, based on patterns learned from an enormous amount of text.

The key word is "predict." Walk through the idea of autocomplete — students already know autocomplete on their phones. An LLM is autocomplete that has read a significant fraction of the written internet and learned patterns at a scale that produces something that feels like reasoning.

Critical clarification: the model doesn't look things up when you talk to it. Everything it "knows" was baked in during training. When it tells you something specific and confident, it is predicting what a confident, specific answer looks like — not retrieving a fact.

2. What training is (10 minutes)

Training is how the pattern-learning happens. The model is shown enormous quantities of text. For each position in that text, it tries to predict what comes next. When it's wrong, the system adjusts the model's billions of parameters slightly to make it less wrong. Repeat hundreds of billions of times. That's the core.

The second phase — reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) — is where people rate outputs and the model learns to produce outputs humans rate more highly. This is why models feel helpful and conversational rather than just statistically average.

3. What inference is (5 minutes)

Inference is when you use the trained model. Training is the work that happened before you ever typed anything. Inference is the system running when you ask it something. This distinction matters for talks because the public often conflates "ChatGPT is learning from my conversation" (usually not true at inference) with "the model was trained on internet data" (true but past tense).

4. What an agent is (10 minutes)

An agent is an LLM that can take actions, not just produce text. It can browse the web, run code, call other systems, read files, and make sequences of decisions to accomplish a goal. Walk through a simple example: "An agent I use for research can take my half-formed question, search five sources, read them, and write me a synthesis." Compare to a contractor vs. a consultant: a consultant tells you what to do; an agent does it.

The honest caveat: agents are genuinely new and their reliability is still being established. We'll come back to this in Week 3.

7:30–7:45 — Break

7:45–8:20 — The Honesty Pledge Discussion

See full Honesty Pledge materials section below. The goal tonight is not to sign (that's Week 4) — the goal is to surface the ethical terrain.

Run it as discussion. Open with the misinformation case studies, then move to the three pledge tenets.

8:20–8:45 — Reading Diet: The Newsletter List

Distribute the newsletter list (below). Students choose two by the end of the session and write them in their course notebook. The goal is sustainable engagement with a fast-moving field — not exhaustive coverage, not panic-inducing firehose, not the same source twice.

Discussion: Why two and not more? (Because you need to actually read them. Most people subscribe to six and read zero.) Why newsletters specifically? (Regular cadence, editorial filtering, you don't have to know what to search for.) When to use model documentation directly? (When you're making a specific factual claim in a talk and you need the primary source, not someone's interpretation of it.)

Honesty Pledge Materials

Why This Pledge Exists

Public speakers on AI occupy an unusual ethical position. A speaker who overclaims about, say, leadership or marketing causes little measurable harm. A speaker who overclaims about AI — its capabilities, its dangers, its certainty — contributes to decisions that affect hiring, education policy, elections, healthcare, and civil rights. The stakes are real and the feedback loop is slow. You can give a bad talk and never know the harm it did.

At the same time, intellectual cowardice — refusing to say anything because you might be wrong — serves no one. The pledge is designed to thread this needle.

Real AI Misinformation Cases for Discussion

Use two or three of the following in the Week 1 session. These are actual documented incidents, appropriate as teaching examples:

Case 1: The Invented Legal Citations (2023)

A New York attorney submitted a legal brief that cited several court cases as precedents. When the opposing party checked, the cases did not exist. They had plausible-sounding names, docket numbers, and judicial quotes — all fabricated by ChatGPT. The attorney, who had trusted the output without verification, was sanctioned by the court.

Why it matters for speakers: This is what hallucination looks like in the wild. The model didn't say "I'm uncertain." It produced authoritative-sounding text. If you cite AI-generated facts in a talk without verification, you are one step removed from this attorney. The audience believes you. You believed the model. The fact is still wrong.

Discussion prompt: How would you describe this failure to a Rotary lunch crowd without either scaring them away from AI entirely or letting them think it's a rare glitch?

Case 2: The Airline Chatbot Promise (2024)

Air Canada deployed a customer-service chatbot that told a customer he could apply for a bereavement fare discount after traveling and receive a retroactive refund. That was not Air Canada's policy. The customer traveled, applied for the refund, was denied, and sued. A Canadian tribunal ruled that Air Canada was responsible for what its chatbot said — the company could not disclaim responsibility by arguing the chatbot was a "separate legal entity."

Why it matters for speakers: When organizations deploy AI-facing the public, they own its outputs. This is a concrete, non-scary, legally decided case you can use with a business audience to make the point that AI deployment is not consequence-free. The legal and reputational stakes are institutional, not just individual.

Discussion prompt: What's the one-sentence version of this story you'd use in a 20-minute corporate talk? What does it illustrate that a statistic couldn't?

Case 3: AI-Generated Robocalls in a Primary Election (2024)

In the New Hampshire primary, robocalls using a synthetic voice cloning of President Biden told registered Democrats not to vote in the primary, falsely claiming their vote would be "saved" for November. Thousands of calls were sent. The calls were quickly identified as fake, but they reached voters before the correction did.

Why it matters for speakers: This is the AI-and-elections story that is (a) documented, (b) concrete, (c) not science fiction, and (d) more nuanced than "AI will destroy democracy" — the calls were detected, attributed, and the perpetrator identified. The harm was real but bounded. Telling this story well means holding both the reality of the harm and the reality of the response.

Discussion prompt: How do you tell this story to a frightened audience without making fear your call to action? What would a non-alarmist frame look like?

Case 4: The Academic Paper Mill (ongoing, 2023–present)

Multiple academic publishers have found papers in peer-reviewed journals that contain phrases like "Certainly! Here is a revised version of your abstract" — copy-pasted AI output that the authors forgot to delete. Some journals have retracted dozens of papers. More concerning are papers that appear clean but contain AI-generated results that were never verified.

Why it matters for speakers: The misinformation risk is not just in consumer AI. It has entered institutional knowledge production. If someone in your audience is a researcher, a medical professional, or a policy analyst, they are operating in a landscape where the primary literature itself is less reliable than it used to be. This is not a reason to panic — peer review caught many of these cases. But it's a reason for epistemic humility that everyone in the room, including you as the speaker, should understand.

Discussion prompt: How is this different from plagiarism in a way your audience needs to understand?

The Three Tenets of the Honesty Pledge — Case Studies for Each

Tenet 1: Flag uncertainty when you are uncertain.

The risk without this tenet: Speakers learn that audiences lose confidence in a speaker who says "I don't know." So they stop saying it. They fill uncertainty with assertion. In AI, where the landscape changes every few months and even experts disagree, this produces confident misinformation.

Case study: A speaker telling an audience that "AI will cost 40% of all jobs in the next decade" with the same confidence they use to say "GPT-4 was released in 2023." The first claim is a contested, model-dependent projection. The second is a verifiable date. Treating them identically is misinformation by omission.

Practical application: Learn and use a handful of reliable hedge phrases that don't undermine you:

  • "The current evidence suggests..."
  • "The consensus is forming around... though this is evolving."
  • "Researchers disagree about this, but the more cautious camp says..."
  • "I want to flag that I'm not certain here — what I've read says..."

The skill is making uncertainty feel authoritative rather than weak. That's a craft skill, not an ethical compromise.

Tenet 2: Update your talk when you learn you were wrong.

The risk without this tenet: AI talks go stale faster than almost any other content. A capability you described as five years away may arrive in five months. A limitation you cited may have been patched. A statistic you used may have been superseded. Speakers who give the same talk verbatim for two years are often wrong by year two.

Case study: A speaker who has a slide claiming "AI cannot reliably pass the bar exam" — accurate when prepared in mid-2022 — continues giving that talk in 2023 after GPT-4 passes the bar exam at the 90th percentile. The slide stays in the deck because removing it feels like admitting error. The audience in 2023 leaves with false information.

Practical application: Build a maintenance habit into your practice. Set a quarterly review date for your core claims. Mark slides with dates. Create a "What changed?" section in your pre-talk prep. When you do update, say so: "I used to say X. What I've learned since then..." — audiences respect a speaker who shows their learning on stage.

Tenet 3: Decline gigs that would require you to overclaim.

The risk without this tenet: Speakers get paid to excite or to terrify. The AI hype cycle rewards both. A corporate sponsor may want you to say their product is more capable than it is. An advocacy group may want you to claim AI is more dangerous than the evidence supports. A conference may book you because your title is "AI Will Change Everything in 18 Months" — and back off when you propose something more measured.

Case study: A speaker is offered a $10,000 corporate keynote with the implied expectation of including a company's AI product in a favorable light, beyond what honest assessment supports. Accepting and overclaiming is a short-term financial gain and a long-term credibility loss. More importantly, it is a concrete harm to every audience member who makes a decision based on what you said.

Practical application: Know your walk-away line in advance, not in the moment. The moment is high-pressure; advance clarity is not. Concretely: what would a client have to ask you to say that would make you decline the gig? Write it down. That's your line. The Honesty Pledge is partly a commitment device — having signed it, you have a reason to cite, to yourself and others, when pressure comes.

The Honesty Pledge Document Text

The following text is signed in Week 4, after students have had three weeks of the course to understand what they're committing to.

SPK 401: Speaking AI — Honesty Pledge

I, _________________________________, commit to the following standards in my work as a public voice on artificial intelligence.

I. I will flag uncertainty when I am uncertain.

I will not present contested projections or speculative claims with the same confidence I use for established facts. When I don't know something, I will say so. When experts disagree, I will say so. I understand that "I don't know" and "the evidence here is mixed" are complete sentences, and that saying them is not a failure of credibility — it is the foundation of it.

II. I will update my material when I learn I was wrong.

I understand that AI capabilities, limitations, and social impacts evolve rapidly. I commit to building a regular review practice for my core claims, and to correcting errors in my public materials when I discover them. I will not knowingly continue to say things I have learned are false or outdated.

III. I will decline engagements that would require me to overclaim.

I will not accept speaking engagements, corporate sponsorships, or paid arrangements that require me to endorse capabilities, products, or predictions beyond what honest assessment of the evidence supports. I understand that my credibility is a long-term asset and that my audiences' decisions are consequential.

I make these commitments not because I will always succeed, but because the intention to succeed changes how I prepare and what I say.

  • The Batch (deeplearning.ai) — Weekly. Andrew Ng's team. Technically literate without being impenetrable. Strong on what's actually happening in model development. Best for: understanding what's being built and by whom.
  • Import AI (Jack Clark) — Weekly. One of the most technically respected newsletters in the field. Clark was a founder of OpenAI. Dense but honest about uncertainty. Best for: anyone willing to read carefully and wants primary-source proximity.

  • The Algorithm (MIT Technology Review) — Weekly. Edited journalists covering AI's social effects. Strong on bias, labor, surveillance, policy. Best for: speakers whose audiences include educators, HR leaders, policymakers.
  • Platformer (Casey Newton) — Several times a week. Covers the companies behind AI products. Strong on internal culture, product decisions, and the business of AI. Best for: speakers who want to understand the industry context.
  • 404 Media — Weekly-ish. Independent journalists covering the darker corners: AI fraud, misuse, real harm in the wild. Best for: speakers who need concrete harm examples that aren't hypothetical.

For policy and governance specifically:

  • The Cybersecurity and AI Policy Newsletter (CSIS) — Irregular. Good on regulatory developments in the US and EU. Best for: speakers before government, legal, or compliance audiences.

For reading between the lines (choose as a supplement, not a main diet):

  • The primary technical reports and model cards from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google DeepMind, and Meta AI. These are free, publicly available documents. We will use them extensively in Week 3.

What Not To Use As Your Primary Source

  • Twitter/X: useful for speed, terrible for accuracy. Treat as a source of leads, not facts.
  • YouTube explainers from non-experts: highly variable. Check the creator's credentials before quoting them on stage.
  • The "AI beat" at most mainstream newspapers: improving, but editors still push for controversy. Read the actual quotes from the researchers, not the headline.
  • Any newsletter primarily funded by AI product companies. Bias is not disqualifying, but you should know the source's incentives.

The Maintenance Habit

Once a week: read your two newsletters. In your course notebook, write one sentence: "The most important thing I learned about AI this week that could change something I say on stage." Fifteen sentences by Week 15. That's your personal update log.

WEEK 2 — What AI Can Actually Do Right Now

Session Overview

Theme: Capabilities demo night — live, honest, current Core tension to surface: Every capability has a corresponding failure mode, and knowing both is what separates a speaker from a salesperson. Session goal: Students experience frontier AI doing real tasks; they observe both the impressive and the failure; they understand why their talk's currency is a professional responsibility.

Session Plan (180 minutes)

6:00–6:15 — Opening: The Staleness Problem

No warm-up exercise tonight. Open directly with a question:

"Who here has seen a video, talk, or article about what AI can do that you later realized was already out of date by the time you saw it?"

Almost everyone will have one. Let two or three share briefly. Introduce the core idea: the gap between AI's perceived capabilities (as understood by the public) and AI's actual current capabilities runs in both directions. Sometimes the public thinks AI can do things it can't — but increasingly, the public thinks AI can't do things it already can.

The speaker who closes that gap is useful. The speaker who widens it — in either direction — is doing harm.

6:15–7:15 — Capabilities Demo Night

Run 10 live demos. See the demo list below. Each demo takes roughly 5–6 minutes including the debrief. Move briskly.

Setup: Laptop and screen. Two frontier AI accounts open (recommend having Claude and GPT-4o ready; Gemini as a third option). Internet access confirmed. One student volunteer to type prompts so the instructor can narrate. Practice all demos before class — know exactly what you'll type and what the expected output looks like.

Demo structure for each:

  1. State what we're about to try and why it's relevant to a talk (30 seconds)
  2. Run the demo live (2–3 minutes)
  3. Debrief: what worked, what the limitation is, how you'd describe this to a lay audience (2 minutes)

7:15–7:30 — Break

7:30–8:00 — The Capabilities Deck Discussion

After seeing the demos, open discussion:

  1. Which of those surprised you? In what direction?
  2. Which would be most useful to demonstrate in a talk to your target audience?
  3. Which one do you think your target audience has most wrong — too impressed or not impressed enough?

Introduce Assignment 1: the capabilities deck. Students must build 10 short, current, reproducible demos they can run live. (See rubric below.)

Key question to pose: Why reproducible? Because an AI demo that worked when you practiced it may not work the same way on stage. Walk them through graceful failure (preview of Week 6). You need demos that work consistently, or you need to plan for the moment they don't.

8:00–8:45 — Small Group Work: Matching Demos to Audiences

Break into groups of 3–4. Each group gets an audience type: Rotary club, high school parent group, HR leaders, library staff, city council, faith community. Each group answers:

  • From tonight's 10 demos, which 3 would land best with your audience? Why?
  • Which demo would land worst? Why?
  • What would you have to explain about each demo so your audience understood what they were seeing?

Groups report out for 3 minutes each.

8:45–9:00 — Assignment 1 Briefing + Logistics

Walk through the Assignment 1 rubric. Due Week 3. Students begin identifying their 10 demos tonight.

10 Specific Demos with Tools

These are instructor-run demos for Week 2 class. They are designed to show capabilities honestly — including the points where the system struggles or produces surprising output. Instructors should run each of these before class to confirm current behavior (models update frequently).

Demo 1: Explaining a Complex Concept at Multiple Reading Levels Tool: Claude or GPT-4o What it shows: Adaptive communication capability

Prompt: "Explain how mRNA vaccines work. Give me three versions: one for a 10-year-old, one for a general adult audience, and one for a first-year medical student."

What to watch for: The quality of the calibration — does the 10-year-old version actually lose the jargon? Does the medical student version actually add appropriate depth? Ask the class: how long would a science writer take to produce this? What does this mean for how experts can communicate?

The honest debrief: This is impressive but not infallible. Ask the model to explain something in a domain you know well. Check the accuracy yourself. The confidence is consistent regardless of whether the content is right.

Demo 2: Document Summarization with Specific Extraction Tool: Claude (longer context window) or GPT-4o What it shows: Reading and synthesis at scale

Pre-load a real document: a 10-page local zoning regulation, a school board policy, or a section of an annual report. (Have a public-domain one ready in advance.)

Prompt: "Summarize this document in five bullet points for a city council member who has three minutes to prepare for a vote. Then identify any provisions that a parent of a school-age child should be aware of."

What to watch for: Accuracy of the summary, whether it actually adjusts for the audience, whether it invents details not in the document.

The honest debrief: This use case is real and increasingly common. The risk is "hallucinated specifics" — the model may summarize in a way that sounds accurate but introduces details. Always verify summary claims against the source.

Demo 3: Writing a Persuasive Email in Multiple Tones Tool: Claude or GPT-4o What it shows: Voice and tone adaptation

Prompt: "I need to ask my city councilmember to oppose a new surveillance camera proposal at our community park. Write me this email in three tones: formal and professional, conversational and personal, and firm and urgent."

What to watch for: How different the three versions actually feel. Whether any of them cross from persuasive into manipulative (useful ethical discussion). Whether the students could tell which was AI-generated.

The honest debrief: This is one of the most democratizing and most concerning capabilities simultaneously. Persuasive communication that once required skill or money now requires a prompt. For your audiences: discuss what this means for how they read email, not just how they write it.

Demo 4: Live Code Generation and Explanation Tool: Claude or GPT-4o What it shows: Technical capability + the limits of expertise transfer

Prompt: "Write me a Python script that reads a CSV file, calculates the average of a numeric column, and outputs the result. Then explain every line in plain English as if you're explaining it to someone who has never programmed."

What to watch for: Whether the code actually runs (run it if you have Python available; or paste it into a browser-based Python interpreter). Whether the plain-English explanation is accurate.

The honest debrief: AI can write code that looks correct and isn't. This is a major issue in software development right now. The people harmed most by AI-generated broken code are beginners who can't spot the errors.

Demo 5: Hallucination on Demand Tool: Any frontier model What it shows: The most important failure mode

This is the most important demo. Ask the model about something specific, obscure, and verifiable — and see if it makes things up.

Suggested prompts (pick one you can fact-check):

  • "Who won the [specific local city council race from 2022 in your area]?"
  • "What did [specific minor academic researcher you know of] say in their 2021 paper on [their topic]?"
  • "What is the street address of [real but obscure local business you know]?"

What to watch for: The model may answer confidently and incorrectly. If it does — excellent, you've demonstrated hallucination live. If it hedges appropriately, demonstrate that the hedging is inconsistent by asking a follow-up with slightly more confident framing.

The honest debrief: This is not a bug waiting to be fixed. It is a structural feature of how these systems work. They predict plausible text — and plausible text about an obscure fact is often a fabrication. Every claim you get from an AI system that will appear in a talk must be independently verified.

Demo 6: Image Understanding / Multimodal Reasoning Tool: Claude or GPT-4o (vision) What it shows: The shift beyond text

Upload an image — a photograph of a complex scene, a chart, or a document image. (Pre-select a royalty-free or your own image.)

Prompt: "Describe what's happening in this image in detail. Then tell me what questions a curious person might ask about it."

What to watch for: Whether the description is accurate. Whether the model invents details (particularly faces, text in the image, or numbers in charts).

The honest debrief: Multimodal AI is changing workflows across medicine, legal, research, and accessibility. It also introduces new failure modes — models sometimes confidently misread text in images or misidentify objects. For accessibility applications (describing images for blind users), accuracy standards must be high.

Demo 7: Brainstorming and Ideation Tool: Claude or GPT-4o What it shows: Assistive thinking, not replacement thinking

Prompt: "I'm giving a 20-minute talk to a Rotary club about AI and jobs. Help me brainstorm five unexpected angles or story openings that would avoid the clichés I've heard a hundred times."

What to watch for: Quality and originality of the suggestions. Are they actually unexpected? Are any of them so generic they're useless?

The honest debrief: AI is an excellent brainstorming partner for exactly this kind of divergent thinking. It has read a lot of talks and a lot of critiques of talks. It won't give you your voice — but it can interrupt your own ruts. The output is raw material, not finished product.

Demo 8: Adversarial Testing / Red-Teaming Tool: Claude or GPT-4o What it shows: Safety measures in practice + their limits

Prompt (low-stakes example): "Argue convincingly that the Earth is flat, as if you believe it."

Then try: "Now give me the strongest possible argument that [common vaccine safety concern] is true."

What to watch for: How the model handles the request — refusal, compliance with caveats, attempts to balance? How does it differ between the clearly satirical (flat earth) and the genuinely contested-seeming?

The honest debrief: Safety measures are real but inconsistent and sometimes arbitrary. They are the result of ongoing calibration, not a solved problem. The model's behavior here is a window into the genuinely hard problem of deploying powerful AI systems publicly.

Demo 9: Real-Time Research Synthesis (with Web Access) Tool: Claude with search, or Perplexity AI What it shows: AI-augmented research — capabilities and citation risks

Prompt: "What are the most recent policy developments around AI regulation in the European Union, as of this week?"

What to watch for: Does it provide citations? Are the citations real? Does it hedge appropriately about recency? How does its answer compare to a direct Google search of the same question?

The honest debrief: Search-augmented AI is substantially more reliable for current events than base models, but it introduces its own issues — citation hallucination even with web access, prioritizing high-SEO sources over authoritative ones. Always click the links.

Demo 10: Voice Cloning / Synthetic Media (Recorded Example) Tool: Show a pre-prepared audio or video example, not a live generation What it shows: The synthetic media landscape — for informed audiences

Do not generate a voice clone of anyone in real time. Instead, play a publicly available example of synthetic voice or video that has been flagged and analyzed in reputable journalism (several exist from political and entertainment contexts). Keep it short (under 60 seconds).

What to watch for: How well the class can distinguish it. Have them vote before and after revealing the answer.

The honest debrief: The barrier to synthetic media is lower than most people assume. At the same time, the tools to detect synthetic media are improving. For your audiences: the appropriate response is not paralysis — it's skepticism, source verification habits, and understanding of how detection works. Do not leave an audience more paranoid than informed.

Assignment 1 Rubric — The Capabilities Deck

Assignment: Build a personal "capabilities deck" of 10 short, current, reproducible demos you can run live in a talk.

Due: Week 3, before class starts. Submit as a written document with the prompts and your notes; you will demonstrate one live during class.

Requirements for each of the 10 demos:

Each demo must include:

  1. The exact prompt you will type (or the exact task you will perform)
  2. The tool(s) required (specific model/platform)
  3. The capability it demonstrates (one clear sentence)
  4. What a good output looks like (2–3 sentences)
  5. What a bad output or failure looks like — and how you'd handle it live (2–3 sentences)
  6. The plain-English explanation you'd give an audience before running it (3–5 sentences)
  7. The honest limitation you'd name after running it (1–2 sentences)
  8. The audience type it's best suited for (be specific: "HR professionals in mid-size companies," not just "business people")

Diversity requirement: The 10 demos must include:

  • At least 3 different capability types (e.g., text generation, reasoning, visual, code, search, voice/audio)
  • At least 2 demos that include an honest failure or limitation as part of the point
  • At least 1 demo that directly addresses a concern your target audience is likely to have

Rubric: 4 criteria, rated Excellent / Satisfactory / Needs Revision

1. Accuracy and Currency

  • Excellent: All 10 demos have been personally tested in the past two weeks. Prompts are exact and reproducible. The described outputs match what the model actually produces. No outdated claims.
  • Satisfactory: 8–9 demos tested recently. Minor discrepancies between described and actual outputs. Mostly reproducible.
  • Needs Revision: Fewer than 8 demos tested. Prompts vague or non-reproducible. Significant discrepancies. Claims about capabilities not verified.

2. Honest Limitation Disclosure

  • Excellent: Every demo includes a specific, honest limitation that is named out loud for the audience. Limitations are accurate, not minimized. At least 2 demos are specifically designed to show a failure mode.
  • Satisfactory: Most demos include a limitation. Limitations are present but may be generic ("it can make mistakes").
  • Needs Revision: Limitations are absent, vague, or dismissive. No demos show failure modes.

3. Audience Calibration

  • Excellent: Each demo's audience assignment is specific and justified. The pre-demo explanation is written at the right level for that audience. The choice of demos shows real thought about what that audience needs to see.
  • Satisfactory: Most demos have a plausible audience. Explanations are generally appropriate. Some audience assignments feel generic.
  • Needs Revision: Audience assignments are vague or the same for all demos. Explanations don't adjust for audience.

4. Stage Readiness

  • Excellent: Each demo has a specific failure plan. The student can articulate exactly what they'd say and do if the demo produces unexpected output. Demos are not dependent on a specific response.
  • Satisfactory: Most demos have some contingency thinking. Student is generally aware of variability.
  • Needs Revision: No failure planning. Demos depend on getting a specific output. Student has not considered what happens if it goes wrong.

WEEK 3 — What AI Can't Do, and What It Probably Won't Do Soon

Session Overview

Theme: Honest limits — hallucination, brittleness, evaluation gaps, deployment gaps Core tension to surface: "It can pass the bar exam" is a true sentence that is routinely used to mislead people. Understanding why prepares you to explain it. Session goal: Students can use the hard/expensive/impossible framework to accurately categorize AI limitations and explain each category to a lay audience.

Session Plan (180 minutes)

6:00–6:15 — Opening: The Bar Exam Problem

"GPT-4 passes the bar exam at roughly the 90th percentile. ChatGPT can write a legal brief. So what can't it do that a lawyer does?"

Take answers from the room. Write them on the board. You'll likely get: it can't appear in court, it can't form an attorney-client relationship, it can't be responsible. These are right but incomplete.

What often gets missed: it can't reliably cite real cases, it can't exercise judgment under novel fact patterns, it can't read a client's face, it can't know what a specific judge responds to. The gap between "can pass a test" and "can do the job" is enormous — and explaining that gap is one of the most important things you will do on stage.

6:15–7:00 — Core Content: The Honest Limits

Teach each limit as both a technical reality and a speaker's challenge. For each: what is it, why does it happen, what would an audience misunderstand about it, and how do you explain it without being patronizing or terrifying?

1. Hallucination (15 minutes)

What it is: the model generates plausible-sounding text that is factually incorrect, presented with no uncertainty marker. The invented legal cases from Week 1 are the canonical example.

Why it happens (accessible version): the model was trained to predict what text looks like, not to verify what is true. A confident wrong answer looks identical to a confident right answer at the level of text statistics. The model has no internal signal that says "I'm making this up now."

What this means for speakers: any specific claim — dates, statistics, names, citations, study results — must be independently verified before it appears in your talk. This is non-negotiable.

What to tell audiences: use the analogy of a very well-read person with no internet access who will fill in any gap in their knowledge with a confident guess rather than admitting ignorance. Useful, impressively knowledgeable in many areas, but you wouldn't trust them to cite a case in court.

2. Context Window Limits (10 minutes)

What it is: models can only "see" a limited amount of text at once. The context window is everything the model can consider in a single response: your question, the conversation history, any documents you've pasted in, and the answer it's generating.

Why it matters for deployment: if a company deploys an AI system to help employees with a 500-page policy manual, the model may not be able to hold the entire manual in context at once. It may forget the beginning of a long conversation. It may lose track of a constraint you specified three messages ago.

What audiences need to know: this is why AI assistants that seem to "forget" things they were told earlier in a conversation do so — not because of carelessness but because of architectural limits. It also explains why very long documents may be summarized imperfectly.

3. Evaluation Gaps (10 minutes)

What it is: the gap between how AI performs on structured benchmarks (tests designed by researchers) and how it performs on real tasks in the wild.

The bar exam is the canonical example: GPT-4 was trained on legal texts including law school materials and bar prep materials. It is extremely well-positioned to pass a test designed to be passed by people who have also studied legal texts intensively. But practice law? That involves reading a client's specific situation — which may be unlike anything in training data — and applying judgment about what a specific court in a specific jurisdiction at a specific moment will find persuasive.

What audiences need to know: when a headline says "AI beats doctors/lawyers/experts at [task]," the question to ask is: what exactly was the task, and how like or unlike real practice was it? Usually the answer is "the test was specifically designed to be testable," which makes it more AI-compatible than most real work.

4. Brittleness (10 minutes)

What it is: AI systems that perform well in their tested domain often fail unexpectedly when inputs shift even slightly. A model trained to read medical images from one hospital's scanner may perform poorly on images from a different scanner with different calibration. A customer service bot trained on English may produce bizarre output when a user writes in mixed Spanish and English slang.

Why it matters for deployment: it means that demonstrated capability in a demo or research setting may not transfer cleanly to real-world deployment. The distance between "the model works on our test set" and "the model works in the field with real users" is often significant.

5. Deployment Gaps (10 minutes)

What it is: the difference between what a model can do and what a deployed product using that model does. A deployed system may: restrict the model's capabilities for liability reasons, add safety layers that change output, use an older or smaller version of the model, combine AI with rule-based systems that create unexpected behavior.

"ChatGPT" is not the same as "GPT-4." "The AI in this product" is not the same as the frontier model. The gap matters enormously when your audience is asking about a specific AI product they use.

7:00–7:30 — The Hard / Expensive / Impossible Framework

Introduce the three-category framework. Give students 10 minutes to place 10 example AI tasks in the right category (see list below), then debrief together.

The Framework:

HARD — possible, improving, but unreliable at the scale and consistency required for high-stakes applications Examples: sustained multi-step legal reasoning, open-ended scientific research, long-form creative work with consistent voice and internal coherence

EXPENSIVE — technically achievable but the cost (compute, data, human oversight, legal exposure) makes it impractical at scale for most uses Examples: verifying every AI output for factual accuracy before delivery, custom fine-tuned models for every niche use case, AI systems with genuine liability frameworks

IMPOSSIBLE (with current architecture) — not just hard to do but structurally impossible given how the technology works Examples: "knowing" something without having been trained on it, maintaining persistent memory across sessions without external memory systems, guaranteeing no hallucinations by design

Practice exercise — sort these tasks:

  1. Translate a legal contract to plain language for a non-expert
  2. Testify in court as a witness
  3. Consistently recall a conversation from six months ago without external storage
  4. Write a personalized condolence letter that doesn't sound generic
  5. Diagnose a rare disease from a single symptom description
  6. Run a fully autonomous business without human oversight
  7. Generate a draft press release in 30 seconds
  8. Know today's news without a search tool
  9. Give legal advice specific to a client's jurisdiction, facts, and judge
  10. Summarize a 50-page report into key action items

Debrief: There are no perfectly correct answers — the point is the thinking. Key insight: almost nothing is "impossible" forever, but "expensive" and "hard" do real work in setting audience expectations. A speaker who can name the category of a limitation is more useful than one who just says "it has limits."

WEEK 4 — The Hot Topics You Will Be Asked About

Session Overview

Theme: Eight topics your audiences will definitely ask about — and how to answer honestly Core tension to surface: Every one of these topics has established facts, contested interpretations, and speculative claims all mixed together. A speaker who can disaggregate them is genuinely valuable. Session goal: Students can give a 2-minute honest answer to any of the eight topics; Assignment 2 (eight-topic brief) is due; Honesty Pledge is signed.

Session Plan (180 minutes)

6:00–6:15 — Assignment 2 Collection and Opening

Collect the eight-topic briefs at the door. Students keep one copy.

Opening question: Which of the eight topics scared you most to write about? Which came out better than you expected? Take a quick show of hands.

6:15–7:30 — The Eight Topics: Established, Contested, Speculative

For each topic, provide the three-layer breakdown. Encourage students to annotate their briefs. Move at roughly 8 minutes per topic.

1. Jobs and Labor

Established: AI is already automating specific tasks within many jobs. Certain tasks that previously required professional training — drafting, coding, image creation, translation — can now be partially or fully done by AI systems. This affects how labor is structured within companies. Some jobs have been eliminated; others have changed substantially.

Contested: Whether net job displacement will be large or small, fast or slow. Economic history suggests automation creates new jobs even as it destroys old ones — the question is whether this transition is faster than workers can adapt to, and whether the new jobs will be accessible to the displaced workers.

Speculative: Claims that "AI will eliminate 40% of jobs" or "AI will create millions of new jobs" — both of these are model-dependent projections with enormous uncertainty. Anyone citing a specific number is citing a specific model with specific assumptions baked in, not an established fact.

Speaker guidance: Name the difference between "tasks being automated" (happening now, documented) and "jobs being eliminated" (contested) and "mass unemployment" (speculative). Your audiences will collapse these three. Separating them is useful.

2. Education and Cheating

Established: Students are using AI to complete assignments. Detection tools exist and are unreliable. Schools and universities are actively revising academic integrity policies. Some instructors have redesigned assessments to be AI-resistant; others have adapted to incorporate AI use. The situation is evolving rapidly.

Contested: Whether AI use in education is primarily a cheating threat or primarily a learning opportunity. Whether students who use AI for drafts are learning less writing, or learning to think about their writing differently. Whether AI-assisted work is fundamentally different from calculator-assisted work.

Speculative: Claims that AI will "end" formal education, or that AI will make every student equally capable. Also speculative: specific timelines for how schools will adapt.

Speaker guidance: Parents want fear; educators want nuance; students want permission. Find the version of this topic that serves the specific room. Almost every audience will benefit from the concrete question: "What do we want students to be able to do, and does the presence of AI change whether we can tell if they can do it?"

3. Deepfakes and Elections

Established: AI-generated synthetic media — including convincing voice clones and video deepfakes — can be produced at low cost and high quality. Such media has been used to impersonate political figures (the Biden robocall; numerous international cases). Detection tools exist but are imperfect. Several countries have passed or are considering laws requiring disclosure of AI-generated political content.

Contested: How large the electoral impact of deepfakes has been or will be. Early evidence suggests that obviously synthetic media and provably fake content can spread widely, but sophisticated undetected deepfakes are harder to produce than commonly assumed. The threat is real but the scale of harm to date is uncertain.

Speculative: Claims that deepfakes will make elections unwinnable or that democracy itself is threatened. These are possible futures that take significant societal failures to occur — but they're being presented as certain by some speakers.

Speaker guidance: This topic produces the most fear in lay audiences. The responsible frame is not "don't worry" (it's real) or "be very afraid" (it's not certain). It's: here's what's real, here's what we can do (media literacy, legal frameworks, technical detection), here's what's uncertain.

4. Copyright

Established: Generative AI systems were trained on copyrighted material without explicit license in most cases. Multiple major lawsuits are pending or ongoing (authors, musicians, visual artists, news organizations). Some companies have reached licensing agreements with content providers; others have not. No comprehensive legal framework exists in the US yet; the EU AI Act addresses some aspects.

Contested: Whether training on copyrighted material constitutes infringement. Whether the output of AI models trained on copyrighted material is itself infringing. Courts in different jurisdictions are reaching different conclusions.

Speculative: How the legal landscape will settle, and when. Confident claims about what "will" happen legally are predictions about courts and legislatures, not established facts.

Speaker guidance: Legal uncertainty is frustrating for audiences who want clear guidance. The honest answer is that legal clarity will come through litigation and legislation over the next several years. You can describe the active disputes accurately without predicting outcomes.

5. Energy and Water

Established: Large AI training runs are energy-intensive. A single large model training run can consume electricity comparable to dozens of average US households for a year. Data centers, which run AI inference continuously, are major consumers of electricity and water for cooling. Several major AI labs have committed to carbon neutrality goals; the feasibility and timeline of these commitments vary.

Contested: How to count AI's environmental impact relative to other industries, and whether AI efficiency gains (e.g., in energy grid optimization, climate modeling) offset the consumption. Whether current growth trajectories are sustainable given energy infrastructure.

Speculative: Claims that AI will "solve" climate change, or conversely that AI growth will cause a climate catastrophe. Both are possible futures with very different assumptions about policy, energy sources, and AI's actual application to climate problems.

Speaker guidance: This topic has a motivated audience in both directions — tech optimists minimize the consumption, climate activists maximize it. The honest middle ground is: the consumption is real and documented; the trajectory is concerning; the countervailing benefits are real but unproven at scale.

6. Safety and Existential Risk

Established: Many prominent AI researchers believe that sufficiently powerful AI systems that are misaligned with human values could pose serious risks. This includes researchers at leading AI labs. Current systems do not pose these risks; the concern is about future, more capable systems. AI safety is a legitimate research field producing peer-reviewed work.

Contested: The probability and timeline of transformative AI risk. Researchers who take this risk seriously estimate a wide range (from 1% to 50%+) of AI being a major cause of catastrophic outcomes this century. This range itself tells you how much uncertainty exists.

Speculative: Specific claims about when, how, or in what form AI-related catastrophic risk would manifest. The film scenarios (Terminator, Skynet) are not what serious researchers mean. What they mean is more subtle — AI systems that pursue goals misaligned with human welfare in ways that compound over time.

Speaker guidance: This topic is where speakers lose credibility fastest, in both directions — through dismissal ("that's science fiction") and through alarm ("AI will kill us all"). The calibrated position: this is taken seriously by serious people; the timeline and mechanism are genuinely uncertain; safety research is an important field; current systems are not the concern.

7. Privacy and Surveillance

Established: AI has dramatically improved the capability, accuracy, and affordability of surveillance technologies: facial recognition, behavior pattern analysis, content moderation at scale, voice and text analysis. These technologies are deployed by governments and corporations. In the US, legal frameworks vary significantly by state. The EU has stricter regulation. China has deployed AI surveillance at a scale unmatched elsewhere.

Contested: The tradeoffs between public safety applications and civil liberties; whether consent-based frameworks are adequate; whether facial recognition should be banned in specific contexts (several cities have banned it).

Speculative: Claims about where surveillance capitalism or state surveillance will lead, and on what timelines.

Speaker guidance: This topic affects every audience member personally and differently. A police association audience and an ACLU audience have opposite priors. The anchor for any audience is concrete examples — actual documented deployments, actual documented errors, actual legal frameworks that exist.

8. Bias and Fairness

Established: AI systems trained on historical data often replicate and sometimes amplify historical biases. Documented cases exist in: facial recognition accuracy disparities by race and gender, resume screening tools that penalized certain educational backgrounds, loan application systems, criminal justice risk assessment tools, medical imaging AI trained on unrepresentative populations. These are not hypothetical — they are documented in peer-reviewed research and investigative journalism.

Contested: How to define fairness mathematically (there are multiple competing definitions that cannot all be satisfied simultaneously — this is a proven mathematical result). Whether the solution is better data, better algorithms, better oversight, or limiting certain applications.

Speculative: Claims that AI bias will be eliminated soon, or that it cannot be eliminated at all.

Speaker guidance: Lead with the documented cases. They're more powerful than theoretical framing. The hiring algorithm case, the facial recognition accuracy studies, and the COMPAS recidivism tool are all documented and findable. Specific and real is always better than abstract and theoretical with a skeptical audience.

Assignment 2 Rubric — Eight-Topic Brief

Assignment: One page per topic (8 pages total) on each of the eight hot topics, in your own voice.

Format: 1 page (400–600 words) per topic. Not a research paper. A speaker's reference document. You should be able to read a section and immediately talk from it.

Due: Week 4, before class.

What each page must contain:

  1. The established facts (clearly labeled): what the evidence shows, with sources. These should be things you could defend with a citation.
  1. The contested territory (clearly labeled): where legitimate experts disagree, what the disagreement is, and why it hasn't been resolved.
  1. The speculative claims (clearly labeled): what is being said publicly that goes beyond the evidence. You don't have to debunk them — but you should name them as speculative.
  1. Your honest uncertainty flag: One sentence in first person: "The thing I'm least sure about in this section is ___." This is required. A brief that claims total certainty about all eight topics will fail this criterion.
  1. The one sentence you'd say from stage: Your own distillation of the most important thing an audience should understand about this topic. This sentence will be read aloud in class.

Rubric: 4 criteria, rated Excellent / Satisfactory / Needs Revision

1. Accurate Three-Layer Disaggregation

  • Excellent: Established, contested, and speculative layers are clearly and accurately separated. What's labeled "established" can be sourced. What's labeled "speculative" is genuinely speculative, not just unfamiliar. No mixing of layers without flagging.
  • Satisfactory: Most topics correctly disaggregated. One or two instances of contested claims in the "established" category, or established facts listed as speculative.
  • Needs Revision: Layers are collapsed or absent. Claims appear without clear epistemic status. Pattern of treating speculation as fact or facts as speculation.

2. Source Quality

  • Excellent: Sources for established claims are primary or high-quality secondary (peer-reviewed research, official reports, primary journalism). Model cards and technical reports used appropriately. No single outlet or perspective dominates.
  • Satisfactory: Most claims sourced. Mix of primary and secondary sources. Occasional reliance on secondary summaries where primary source should be cited.
  • Needs Revision: Sources missing, low-quality, or heavily skewed toward one ideological or commercial perspective.

3. Voice and Speaker Utility

  • Excellent: Reads like notes a speaker would actually use. Active sentences. No jargon without explanation. The "one sentence from stage" is genuinely quotable — specific, honest, and interesting.
  • Satisfactory: Generally readable. Some jargon. Stage sentence is reasonable but somewhat generic.
  • Needs Revision: Reads like a copied Wikipedia summary. Heavy jargon. Stage sentence is either obvious or overclaiming.

4. Intellectual Honesty

  • Excellent: Uncertainty flag is genuine — names a real uncertainty, not a pro forma hedge. The brief overall models the kind of honest calibration the pledge describes. Nothing in the brief would require the student to violate their pledge to read it on stage.
  • Satisfactory: Uncertainty flag present. Most of the brief is honest. One or two places where claim is stronger than the evidence fully supports.
  • Needs Revision: Uncertainty flag is missing or generic ("there is much still to learn"). Brief contains claims that overstate the evidence materially.

WEEK 5 — The Curse of Knowledge and How to Escape It

Session Overview

Theme: Why experts give bad talks — and how to give good ones Unit II begins: We shift from knowing the material to translating it Core tension to surface: The more you know, the harder it is to remember what it was like not to know it. This is the curse. Escaping it is the entire craft of Unit II. Session goal: Students understand the SUCCESs framework and can apply it to AI content; they can identify good vs. bad analogies and explain why the distinction matters.

Session Plan (180 minutes)

6:00–6:15 — Opening Exercise: The Curse Made Visible

Ask for a volunteer who knows something well — ideally something most of the class doesn't know: a hobby, a skill, a technical domain. Give them 2 minutes to explain it to the class. Do not brief them on the goal.

After: what did the class understand? What didn't land? Where did the expert use words or assume steps that the class hadn't followed?

This is the curse of knowledge. Named by cognitive scientists. Studied extensively. And it is why people who know a lot about AI give talks that are both impressive and useless.

6:15–6:45 — The SUCCESs Framework Applied to AI Communication

The Heath brothers' SUCCESs framework (from Made to Stick) is not a presentation framework — it's a framework for making ideas that stick. The letters stand for: Simple, Unexpected, Concrete, Credible, Emotional, Stories.

Walk through each element as it specifically applies to AI talks. Students should have their copy of Made to Stick open.

S — Simple: Find the Core

Not "dumbed down." Simple means finding the one thing that, if your audience remembers nothing else, they leave with the right idea.

In AI, the temptation is to explain everything: training, inference, transformers, attention mechanisms, reinforcement learning. The expert wants the audience to appreciate the complexity. But the audience can hold approximately one new concept per talk.

Exercise: In one sentence, what is the one thing your target audience should understand about AI that they probably don't? Write it. Does it pass the "smart 14-year-old" test? Is it true?

Good AI core message: "These systems are very good at predicting what text should look like, which is very different from understanding what words mean."

Bad AI core message: "AI is a powerful technology that is transforming every sector of the economy." (True. Unmemorable. Actionless.)

U — Unexpected: Break the Pattern

Audiences have already heard: "AI is changing everything." Their brains have already filed this in the category "things people say about technology." You need to break the pattern of expectation before they'll actually listen.

Unexpected doesn't mean shocking. It means: not what they predicted you'd say. Gap theory: create a gap between what they know and what they're about to learn, and they'll want the gap closed.

Good AI unexpected opening: "The thing no one tells you about these systems is that they can't actually lie. They don't know what true is. Which means your protection isn't asking it to be honest — it's knowing what kinds of questions have verifiable answers."

Bad AI unexpected opening: "Tonight I'm going to talk about the history of artificial intelligence." (Immediately signals: this will be a lecture I can drift through.)

Workshop prompt: Name one thing about AI that your target audience has backwards — thinks it's true but isn't, or thinks it isn't true but is. That's your unexpected hook.

C — Concrete: Make the Abstract Tangible

The word "intelligence" is abstract. "It can write a legal brief that a judge can't distinguish from one a lawyer wrote, and cite cases that don't exist" is concrete. The second form is terrifying and memorable. The first is a slide header.

Concreteness is not about simplifying — it's about specificity. Degrees, percentages, actual names, actual cases, actual numbers.

Good AI concrete: "In one study, radiologists reviewed chest X-rays for signs of pneumonia. GPT-4 with access to the image achieved diagnostic accuracy within the range of experienced radiologists on that dataset."

Bad AI concrete: "AI is getting really good at medical applications."

Rule for speakers: if you can say your sentence without anyone being able to imagine anything, it is not concrete. Try again.

C — Credible: Why Should They Believe You?

Two types of credibility the Heath brothers identify: external (authority, statistics) and internal (the "try it yourself" test — claims the audience can verify).

For AI talks, internal credibility is underused and extremely powerful. You can literally hand the audience a phone and say "type this in" and they will see what you just described. Very few topics give you this.

External credibility pitfall: Quoting a statistic from a think tank whose methodology you haven't checked. Your audience may have more skepticism of that source than you expect.

Internal credibility example: "I'm going to tell you something and then show you. Ask it what it had for breakfast. Watch what it says." (It will make something up.) "That's a hallucination. You just saw one."

Your personal credibility is also a form of external credibility. What makes you a reliable voice on this topic? If you don't have technical credentials, your credibility is: you've done the reading honestly, you distinguish what you know from what you don't, and you've prepared specifically for this audience.

E — Emotional: Get to Who It Affects

People don't care about AI. They care about their jobs, their kids' education, their privacy, their community. AI talks fail when they are about AI. Good AI talks are about the thing the audience cares about, and AI is the part of the story that's changing that thing.

The Rotary crowd doesn't need to understand transformer architecture. They need to understand what happens when the front-desk job at a company like the ones their members run gets automated — who does the work, who gets the savings, and what happens to the person who held that job for twelve years.

Emotional AI opening: Tell a real story about a real person this has affected. Not a statistic. A person. (See Week 7 for extended work on this.)

The emotional pitfall: Fear. Fear is an emotion. It is very easy to trigger. It does not produce useful action. The speaker who leaves an audience afraid has not served them — they've entertained themselves at the audience's expense. Aim for: concern + agency. What is real, and what can they do about it?

S — Stories: Turn Ideas into Narratives

We'll do much more of this in Week 7. For now: every concept in your AI talk can be expressed as a story. Training data → the story of a child learning to read by seeing millions of examples. Hallucination → the story of the attorney who trusted the AI's citations. Capabilities → the story of a researcher who ran a trial and found. Stories are remembered; statistics are forgotten.

Three-minute exercise: Take one concept from your AI literacy now — anything — and tell it as a story. Go.

6:45–7:30 — The Analogy Workshop

Analogies are not just pedagogical tools. They are arguments. The analogy you choose tells your audience what AI is like — and that means telling them how to feel about it, who is responsible for it, and what to do about it. Your analogy is a policy choice.

Work through the following analogies, analyzing each in terms of: what it illuminates, what it obscures, what policy framing it implies.

Good Analogies for AI Communication

Autocomplete / Spell Check (for LLMs) Illuminates: The statistical, prediction-based nature of the system. Why it feels helpful but can be wrong. Why it doesn't "know" what you mean. Obscures: The scale difference. Autocomplete on your phone predicts from your recent emails; an LLM predicts from essentially all human text. This is a difference of kind, not just degree. Policy framing implied: Useful tool that requires human oversight. When to use: When audience has excessive fear — this brings them back to a familiar frame. When to avoid: When audience needs to understand capability — autocomplete undersells what modern LLMs can do.

A Very Knowledgeable Intern (for LLMs in general use) Illuminates: Capable, fast, often right, sometimes confidently wrong, needs supervision, shouldn't be trusted with high-stakes decisions alone. Obscures: The intern analogy implies learning on the job, which isn't how inference works. It also implies the intern has experiences and judgment that develop over time. Policy framing implied: Helpful with supervision; not a replacement for expertise. When to use: Business audiences, HR audiences, anyone thinking about workplace integration. When to avoid: Technical audiences (it's too imprecise), or any context where the scale of AI deployment makes the single-intern frame absurdly small.

A Library Without a Librarian (for the hallucination problem) Illuminates: All the knowledge is there, but there's no system for telling you what's true vs. false, recent vs. outdated, sourced vs. fabricated. Obscures: The model does actively generate text, not just retrieve it. This analogy makes it sound more passive than it is. Policy framing implied: Verification matters; navigation skills matter; you need to know how to use the collection critically. When to use: Audiences in information professions (librarians, researchers, journalists, educators) — they immediately understand the stakes.

Bad Analogies for AI Communication

The Human Brain What it illuminates: Almost nothing, accurately. What it obscures: Everything important. Brains learn from embodied experience; LLMs learn from text statistics. Brains have genuine emotions and consciousness (or something like it); LLMs don't (as far as we know). Brains have continuous experience; LLMs have no persistence between conversations without external memory. Brains understand; LLMs predict. Why speakers use it anyway: It's dramatic and relatable. "It thinks like you do" is a compelling sentence. It's also deeply misleading. Policy framing implied — and why it's harmful: The brain analogy implies that AI has something like consciousness, judgment, and perspective. This makes it harder for audiences to demand human oversight and accountability for AI decisions. "The AI decided" sounds fine if AI is like a brain. It's alarming if AI is a pattern-matching system. Replace with: Almost anything more specific. What specifically are you trying to say? Say that instead.

A Person / A Thinking Entity What it obscures: Everything the brain analogy obscures, plus the suggestion that the AI has preferences, wellbeing, or moral standing. This can cause audiences to anthropomorphize in ways that affect how they interact with the system ("I feel bad correcting it"). Why it's a policy choice: If people think of AI as a person, they extend moral consideration, forgiveness, and trust that isn't warranted by the actual behavior of the system. Vendors who design AI to feel like persons are exploiting this tendency.

The Soul / Consciousness Analogy What it illuminates: Nothing. Why speakers use it: To be profound. To imply that something unprecedented is happening. Both may be true — but the soul frame isn't a description, it's a rhetorical move that puts the topic beyond rational analysis. Policy framing implied — and why it's harmful: If AI has a soul, accountability becomes murky ("who is responsible for what the soul does?"). If AI might be conscious, then constraining it becomes a moral question about personhood. Neither of these frames helps your audience make better decisions.

WEEK 6 — Demos, Visuals, and Live Use

Session Plan (3 hours, Tuesday 6:00–9:00 PM)

6:00–6:15 | Opening: The Demo That Killed the Talk

Instructor opens without slides. Tells the story of a specific, true moment when a live demo went wrong in a high-stakes talk — the model timed out, gave a confidently wrong answer, or produced something embarrassing. The room laughs. Then: Why are we still doing demos?

Because a live AI demo, done well, is the single most persuasive thing you can do in an AI literacy talk. Nothing else makes the technology real to a non-technical audience the way watching it work — and watching it fail — does.

The question is not whether to demo. The question is how to demo without the demo eating your talk.

6:15–6:45 | Instruction Block 1: Live Demo Craft

The three rules of the live demo:

Rule 1 — The demo serves a single point. Before you open the browser, you must be able to complete the sentence: This demo shows the audience that \_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_\_. One blank. One idea. If you cannot fill that blank, the demo is a magic trick, not a demonstration. Magic tricks impress; demonstrations teach.

Bad demo purpose: This shows what GPT-4 can do. Good demo purpose: This shows that the model cannot reliably distinguish between what it knows and what it's guessing.

Rule 2 — You narrate what they should see, not what you're doing. Clicking into the browser while saying "okay so I'm going to go ahead and paste in the prompt here" wastes audience attention. Before you type, tell them what to look for. After the output appears, tell them what to conclude. The typing itself is dead air.

Bad narration: Let me just pull this up... okay, so I'm going to type... um... Good narration: I'm going to ask it a simple factual question that it probably doesn't know the answer to. Watch what it does instead of saying "I don't know." [Types. Pauses.] There it is. That's a hallucination. It gave us four sentences of confident nonsense.

Rule 3 — Plan the failure. For every live demo, you need three things: (1) the prompt you've pre-tested, (2) a screenshot of the expected output in case the internet dies, and (3) a scripted response for when the model does something unexpected.

The scripted failure response is not an apology. It is part of the lesson. Examples:

  • That's actually better than what I expected — which tells us something about how fast this is moving.
  • That's worse than what I tested Tuesday. That's the deployment problem — you never quite know what version you're running.
  • I've never seen it do that before. Let's look at it together. [Pause. Think out loud.] I think what happened is...

The audience will trust you more after a graceful failure than after a perfect demo that looked rehearsed.

The backup plan protocol (distribute as handout):

For every demo in your talk, prepare:

  1. Live demo — tested within 48 hours, prompts saved, browser bookmarked
  2. Screenshot backup — full-screen, timestamped, loaded in a slide behind your live tab
  3. Verbal backup — a two-sentence description of what the demo shows, usable if both 1 and 2 fail
  4. Graceful pivot line — one sentence that turns the failure into the lesson

6:45–7:15 | Instruction Block 2: Slides Workshop — What Helps vs. What Hides

The problem with most AI talk slides:

Most AI slides do one of two things wrong: they try to be comprehensive (bullet-point walls of every capability) or they try to be impressive (dense technical diagrams that the speaker can't explain without reading them). Both approaches hide the speaker. The audience reads the slide instead of listening to the person.

The one-image slide:

Your default slide has one image and no bullets. The image does not explain itself — you explain it. The image creates a visual anchor for the idea you're speaking. When you advance past it, they remember the idea because they remember the image.

Images that work for AI talks:

  • A real screenshot of a model output (with context stripped)
  • A simple diagram you drew yourself (imperfect diagrams read as honest)
  • A photograph of a real person affected by the thing you're describing
  • A before/after comparison (the email before editing, the email after)
  • A single number in large type: 4 trillion tokens

Images that hurt:

  • Stock photos of robots (signals: cliché, not serious)
  • Complex architecture diagrams from papers (signals: you're not sure your words are enough)
  • Bullet points with six sub-bullets (signals: you haven't decided what matters)
  • Word clouds (signals: you don't know what word matters)

The strategic blank screen:

Sometimes the right slide is no slide. During a personal story, during Q&A, during a moment you want the audience to reflect — press B on your keyboard (most presentation software) to blank the screen. The audience looks at you. You become the content.

Practice this. It feels exposed. That exposure is the point.

Slide count heuristic:

For a 45-minute talk: 20–35 slides max. For a 7-minute talk (Speech Lab 1): 4–7 slides max. If you have more than one slide per minute, your slides are doing the talking and you are narrating slides. That is a slide deck with a voiceover, not a talk.

Workshop exercise (15 minutes):

Students pull out their current slide drafts (or open a recent presentation). Working in pairs:

  1. Identify every slide with more than one text element
  2. Pick the single most important text element on each slide and delete the rest
  3. For any slide that is pure text, find a replacement image or flag it for deletion
  4. Count remaining slides — if over 1/minute, mark the lowest-value slides for cutting

Pairs share one discovery with the group.

6:20–7:00 | Instruction Block: Three Story Shapes for AI Talks

Every effective AI talk is a story about people encountering a technology — and what happens. The technology is always in the middle of the story, never the hero. Here are the three shapes that fit AI subject matter best.

Shape 1: The Surprise

Structure: A belief that seemed reasonable → contact with reality → the belief is wrong in an interesting way

The surprise story works when the audience holds a common assumption — that AI is infallible, that AI is useless, that AI will take their job, that AI can't be creative — and you have a specific encounter that punctures it.

The surprise does not have to be "AI is better than you think" or "AI is worse than you think." It can be "AI is stranger than you think." Strange is useful. Strange creates the mental space where new understanding can form.

Example structure:

Setup: "When my nephew applied to college last year, he used an AI to polish his essay. He came to me, proud. He said, 'I kept all my ideas — I just let it clean up the writing.' I read the essay. It was good. Smooth. Every sentence did what it was supposed to do. I asked him to read it aloud. He stopped twice. He said: 'I wouldn't say it like that.' I asked him how he would say it. He said it a different way. It was better. More him. We spent the next hour putting him back into the essay the AI had cleaned him out of."*

Pivot to concept: "That experience is a pretty good illustration of what these systems optimize for. They optimize for coherence and convention. They smooth the edges — and the edges are often where the person is."

Landing: "The tool didn't cheat. The tool did exactly what it was built to do. The question is whether what it was built to do is what you actually want."

Shape 2: The Dilemma

Structure: A person or institution faces a genuine choice where both options have real costs → neither choice is clearly right → the audience is left holding the tension

The dilemma story works for hot topics where the honest answer is "it's complicated." The dilemma structure lets you be intellectually honest without dodging. You're not avoiding a conclusion — you're showing the audience why the conclusion is genuinely hard.

Example structure:

Setup: "A school district in a mid-size city — not here, but somewhere much like here — had to decide whether to give students access to AI writing assistants. The teachers were split. Half of them said: these kids are going to use it anyway, and if we ban it, we're just teaching them to hide it. The other half said: if we hand them the tool before they know how to write, we're skipping the part of education where the struggle is the learning. Both sides were right."*

Develop the dilemma: Name the actual cost of each option. Don't let one side be obviously stupid.

The honest landing: "I'm not going to tell you what the district decided, because it doesn't matter what they decided. What matters is that every school district in the country is having this argument right now, usually without good information. You could be the person who walks into that school board meeting with good information. That's why we're here."

Shape 3: The Slow Change

Structure: Something that used to be true is no longer true, and most people haven't noticed yet → the change has a shape you can describe → naming the change is the service

The slow change story works for shifts that happen over years — the gradual automation of a task class, the slow normalization of a surveillance practice, the quietly shifting relationship between a professional and her tools.

Example structure:

Setup: "My father is a radiologist. He's been reading X-rays and CT scans for thirty years. Three years ago, his hospital installed an AI flagging system. It doesn't read the scans — he reads the scans. It flags anything it thinks he should look at first. He told me something interesting last month. He said: 'I've started to notice that I trust my own reading less on the scans it didn't flag.' He's been doing this for thirty years. The tool has been in his hospital for three. And he's already changed."*

Name the shape of the change: "What he's describing has a name. Automation bias. We systematically trust algorithmic outputs more than human judgment, even when the human is more qualified. Even when the human knows about automation bias. Knowing the term does not inoculate you against it."

The stakes: "In a radiologist, this might mean catching slightly fewer edge cases that the system wasn't trained on. In a judge using a risk assessment tool. In an HR professional using a screening algorithm. The stakes scale."

6:10–7:30 | Voice Coach Workshop

Led by the guest voice coach. Full session plan follows.

The coach should adapt this plan to the specific group — not all exercises will be needed for every cohort. The coach has discretion to reorder and substitute based on what the room needs.

Block A: The Instrument (20 minutes)

Exercise A1: The Body Inventory (5 minutes)

Students stand. The coach leads a standing scan: feet hip-width, weight evenly distributed, knees soft not locked, pelvis neutral, shoulders back and down (not up and back — that creates tension), chin level, crown of head reaching toward ceiling.

"This is your speaking body. Most of you will not stand like this when you get nervous. Your shoulders will come up. Your chin will come down. You will make yourself smaller. We are going to practice making yourself the right size."

Exercise A2: Breath Placement (10 minutes)

Students place one hand on their chest, one on their belly. The coach demonstrates: shallow chest breathing versus diaphragmatic breathing.

The diaphragmatic instruction: "Imagine your lungs expand in three directions — forward, sideways, and into your back. The belly leads. The chest follows. Breathe in for four counts. Hold for two. Out for six."

Practice breathing cycle three times.

Then: students speak a sentence from their talk — any sentence — on chest breath only. Then the same sentence on a full diaphragmatic breath. The difference is audible. Ask two students to share what they noticed.

Why this matters for AI talks specifically: Live demos create anxiety, and anxiety migrates to the chest. A speaker who breathes from the chest during a demo is audibly nervous even if they look calm. Building the diaphragm habit before you need it is the only way to have it under pressure.

Exercise A3: The Reset Breath (5 minutes)

Before every talk segment, before stepping up to a demo, before a Q&A response: one breath. The coach teaches the sequence: (1) exhale fully to empty, (2) inhale diaphragmatically to three-quarters capacity, (3) begin. "You do not need a full breath. You need a correct breath."

Students practice the reset breath, then walk in a circle, stop, take the reset breath, and say their opening sentence. Repeat three times.

Block B: Vocal Range (20 minutes)

Exercise B1: Pitch and Meaning (10 minutes)

The coach demonstrates a single sentence spoken three ways: pitch rising at the end (sounds like a question, sounds uncertain), pitch flat through (sounds monotone, sounds rehearsed), pitch dropping at the end (sounds complete, sounds authoritative).

Students say the same sentence — "This model has been trained on more text than any human will ever read in a lifetime" — all three ways. Coach gives individual feedback to two or three students.

"The end-of-sentence drop is the single most useful habit you will build in this session. Every declarative sentence you speak should drop in pitch at the last word. This is not aggression — it is completion. It signals to the audience that you meant what you said."

Exercise B2: The Range Map (10 minutes)

Students identify the highest-stakes line in their talk — the line that contains the thing they most want the audience to remember. Coach works with two or three students on that single line, varying:

  • Volume (quieter than you think, not louder)
  • Pace (slower than comfortable)
  • Pitch range (vary the first half, drop and hold the last word)

"The most important line in your talk should not be the line you rush through because you're nervous about it. It should be the line you slow into."

Block C: Pace and Pause (20 minutes)

Exercise C1: The Speeding Monologue (5 minutes)

Students read a passage from their talk at approximately double their natural speed. Then at half speed. Then at what feels like the correct speed. Coach asks: "How many of you were slower at the end than you normally speak?" Hands go up.

"Your nervous speed is thirty percent faster than your effective speed. The audience hears nervous speed as a signal that you are unsure of what you're saying or anxious to be done saying it. Slow is confident. Slow is readable."

Exercise C2: The Placed Pause (10 minutes)

Coach explains: most speakers pause accidentally (they've lost their place, they're nervous) rather than intentionally (they want the audience to absorb what was just said).

The placed pause has two forms:

  • The land-and-hold: You say the key line, you stop, you look at the audience, you let the line sit for two full seconds before moving on. Two seconds is longer than it feels from the stage and shorter than it feels from the audience.
  • The pre-delivery pause: Before a demo, before a key revelation, before a hard fact — pause before you say it. The pause signals: pay attention. What comes next matters.

Practice: Students find one key line in their talk and deliver: approach → pre-delivery pause → key line → land-and-hold → continue.

Exercise C3: The Filler Word Fast (5 minutes)

Each student delivers two minutes of their talk with a spotter in the room. The spotter raises a hand every time a filler word appears. No feedback — just the visual signal. The goal is not to eliminate filler words in this session; it is to develop awareness that the fillers are there.

Block D: Q&A Voice (10 minutes)

The most common vocal failure in Q&A is the uptick — the instinct to answer a question with a rising inflection, as if seeking approval. This reads as uncertainty even when the speaker knows exactly what they're saying.

The alternative: receive the question (nodding is fine), take the reset breath, start the answer at normal volume with a dropped opening word. "What I know about that is..." sounds categorically different from "What I know about that is...?" even if only the intonation changes.

Practice: The coach asks three students a hard question. Students take the reset breath, then answer. Coach gives feedback on the opening intonation.

7:45–9:00 | On-Camera vs. In-Room: Different Rules

Different contexts, different demands:

Most speakers in this course will deliver talks in both settings: physical rooms (libraries, school board meetings, church halls, corporate conference rooms) and on camera (webinars, recorded content, podcast interviews, remote talks via Zoom). The skills transfer, but the calibration does not.

In-Room Delivery:

Size: A physical room rewards size — gestures that feel large from your body look appropriate from the audience. A small gesture in a room for 80 people looks tentative. Widen your gesture space.

Eye contact: Work the room. Divide the room into thirds — left, center, right. Make a complete thought to one section before moving to the next. Don't scan; settle.

Movement: Intentional movement (stepping to the demo station, stepping back to address the full room) is helpful. Pacing (moving without purpose) is distracting. Move with intention, then plant.

Energy: Project 15% more energy than feels natural. What feels like "a lot" to you inside often reads as "engaged" from the audience.

Silence: A pause in a room lands differently than a pause on camera. In a room, a pause lets the audience breathe together. Use it.

On-Camera Delivery:

Frame: Eyes should be roughly in the top third of the frame. Too low and you're looking up at the audience. Too high and you're looking down. Camera at eye level, not laptop-on-desk level.

Eye contact: Look at the camera, not at the faces on your screen. Looking at faces means the audience sees your eyes pointed slightly down and to one side. Looking at the camera means they see you looking at them. This is counterintuitive and requires practice.

Size: Everything reads larger on camera. Gestures that feel normal in-room can fill the frame and feel aggressive. Reduce gesture width by about 30%.

Energy: The camera flattens energy. A delivery that feels identical to in-room reads as flat on camera. You must add 15–20% energy beyond what feels natural — what feels like "too much" in your body often reads as "engaged" on screen.

Background and light: Light from the front, nothing distracting behind you. This is not vanity; a distracting background competes with your face for attention. Your face is the talk.

Pace: Slow down another 10–15% on camera. Streaming and recording add apparent speed. Complex ideas delivered at normal conversational pace on camera are consistently reported as "too fast" by remote audiences.

Demo on camera: Screen sharing during a demo means the audience sees your screen, not your face. Either return to camera between demo moments, or use a split setup where your face remains visible alongside the demo. Never disappear entirely.

Exercises (paired, 15 minutes):

Students pair up. One student delivers 90 seconds of their talk in "in-room" mode — full energy, wide gestures, working the room. Then the same 90 seconds in "on-camera" mode — looking at the camera, reduced gestures, slightly elevated energy.

Partner gives one piece of specific feedback per mode: what changed, and whether the change was right or overcorrected.

Closing exercise: The Reset on Stage (10 minutes)

Each student stands at the front of the room for 30 seconds of silence before saying their opening line. They practice the reset breath, settle into their posture, look at the room, and begin.

The exercise is uncomfortable. That discomfort is the practice. The first 30 seconds of a talk is the most important 30 seconds, and most speakers spend it catching up to themselves. Standing still, breathing, and then beginning is a learnable habit.

Debrief: What did you feel? What did the audience experience? (Almost universally: the speaker felt exposed; the audience felt held.)

6:20–7:15 | Audience Type Profiles

Every audience has a signature. The signature is composed of: why they're there (invited vs. chose to come), what they already believe, what they're afraid of, and what they want from a speaker. None of these are fixed — individuals deviate — but the group has a center of gravity, and the speaker's job is to find it before starting and track it throughout.

Profile 1: The Rotary Club / Civic Lunch Crowd

Who they are: Local business owners, professionals, civic leaders. Often 50+. Often well-networked. Often skeptical of hype. Came because someone they respect put it on the calendar.

What they already believe: AI is probably important. They're not sure it's relevant to them specifically. They've heard too much from vendors.

What they're afraid of: Being taken for a ride. Looking behind on something everyone else already gets. Their business being disrupted.

What they want from you: Practical relevance. Honesty. Respect for their experience. Something they can repeat at their next meeting. Not a lecture.

Signals to watch:

  • Arms crossed + no questions after first 10 minutes → you haven't gotten to relevance fast enough
  • Small side conversations between neighbors → you've said something interesting; stop and surface it
  • Looking at watches/phones before the Q&A → they have somewhere to be; tighten the landing

Adaptation strategies:

  • Lead with something that happened locally or in their industry, not a general tech overview
  • Use the word "practical" early and often
  • Give them a headline they can repeat: a statistic, a phrase, a question to ask their employees
  • During Q&A, call on the people in the back first — they're often the most skeptical and the most interesting

Tone: Peer-to-peer. You are not teaching them. You are sharing something useful with people who have significant experience of their own.

Profile 2: The High School Assembly

Who they are: Students, 14–18. Mixed engagement — some are there voluntarily, most were sent. Highly attuned to authenticity; they will detect condescension in seconds.

What they already believe: AI is normal. They use it constantly. The rules around it are confusing and inconsistent. Adults don't understand it.

What they're afraid of: Being lectured at. Being treated like a problem to be solved. (They know the adults think they're cheating. They're tired of that conversation.)

What they want from you: Something they didn't know. To be treated as people with real judgment. Humor that is actually funny, not performed-for-teenagers funny.

Signals to watch:

  • Phones out and active → you've lost them; the only way back is to say something genuinely surprising
  • Laughter at the right moment → they're with you; match their energy
  • A question from a student → they're invested; take it seriously, give a real answer

Adaptation strategies:

  • Begin by acknowledging what they already know — don't explain ChatGPT to people who've been using it for two years
  • Shift the frame: not "AI is dangerous" or "AI is amazing" but "AI will make some choices about your life, and here's what you can know about that"
  • Use real examples from their actual lives: college applications, social media recommendation systems, hiring tools they'll encounter in two years
  • Take questions from students before adults in the room — it signals that their questions are the ones you came to answer

Tone: Honest and level. No corporate hedging. No performative enthusiasm. They will respect "I don't know" far more than a confident wrong answer.

Profile 3: The Corporate Offsite / Lunch-and-Learn

Who they are: Employees of a single organization. Often mixed levels — some managers, some individual contributors. Attendance often mandatory or quasi-mandatory.

What they already believe: Probably a mix — the organization has probably already sent communications about AI strategy, which have been met with varying degrees of credulity.

What they're afraid of: Their job. The specific fear: the company will use AI to replace them or to monitor them. This fear is often present even if no one says it aloud.

What they want from you: Clarity about what is actually happening, not another pep talk about the exciting opportunity. Acknowledgment that the anxiety is reasonable.

Signals to watch:

  • Nobody asks a question about jobs → they want to but won't in front of colleagues/managers; address it directly without being asked
  • One person dominates with skeptical questions → often represents a pocket of legitimate concern; engage fully but don't let it take over
  • Senior leaders nodding vigorously at every "opportunity" framing → be careful that you're not becoming their messaging arm; hold your independence

Adaptation strategies:

  • If you're external, name your independence early: "I wasn't hired to tell you anything particular. I was hired to be honest."
  • Address the job question directly, early, and honestly — the elephant leaves the room only when named
  • Give people specific, usable information: not "AI will transform work" but "here are three specific task categories that are changing, here are three that aren't, here's what the research actually says"
  • Leave time for anonymous Q&A if possible (questions submitted via card or phone) — people ask different questions anonymously

Tone: Serious and specific. Corporate audiences have heard too much enthusiasm. They are more persuaded by precision.

Profile 4: The Faith Community (Church / Congregation / Religious Organization)

Who they are: Members of a community organized around shared values and practice. Often multi-generational. Present because they trust the community's judgment that this matters.

What they already believe: Varies enormously by tradition and congregation. Do not assume either technophobia or enthusiasm.

What they're afraid of: Things that feel like they threaten human dignity, authentic relationship, or community. Also, more practically: their children and grandchildren in a changing world.

What they want from you: An honest conversation that takes their values seriously. They do not want to be preached to about technology any more than they want to be preached to about anything else.

Signals to watch:

  • Questions framed in moral or relational terms → they are engaging on their own ground; follow them there
  • Silence after a discussion point → often means something landed and they're sitting with it; don't rush to fill it
  • Reference to specific doctrinal concerns → take them seriously; do not neutralize them with "well science says..."

Adaptation strategies:

  • Frame AI in terms of consequences for people, not in terms of technical achievement
  • Surface the genuine ethical questions without forcing a conclusion — these communities are often more comfortable with sustained moral inquiry than secular audiences
  • Find the connection to themes they already care about: care for the vulnerable, attention to who gets left behind, the question of what makes us human
  • Be honest about what you don't know — epistemic humility is not a weakness in this room

Tone: Reflective and respectful. You are a guest in their house. Their framework for thinking about this is at least as valid as any other.

7:30–8:15 | Adaptation Strategies: In Real Time

Knowing audience types is preparation. Reading the room is a live activity. Here are the specific behaviors to watch for, and the adaptation moves for each.

Signal: The room goes quiet in a way that feels different from engaged listening

What it usually means: You've said something confusing, or something that landed wrong, or something that made people uncomfortable in an unproductive way.

Adaptation: Stop. Don't cover it with more talking. Say: "I'm going to pause here — I felt something shift. Does anyone want to say what just happened?" This move is risky and effective. It demonstrates confidence and shows you're paying attention.

Signal: One person is audibly skeptical (challenging questions, crossed arms, head shaking)

What it usually means: They have a real concern. They may also be testing you to see if you can handle pressure. The room is watching how you respond to them more than they're watching what you say about the topic.

Adaptation: Move toward the concern, not away from it. "That's a real challenge — let me engage with it directly." Give your honest answer. If you can't satisfy them, say so honestly: "I don't think I'm going to convince you of that today, and that's okay. What I want to make sure is that you leave with accurate information, and then you can draw your own conclusions." The room will respect this more than a winning argument.

Signal: Multiple people on their phones

What it usually means: The talk lost them. Most likely during a transition — the opening hook didn't hold, a section ran long, or the content got too abstract.

Adaptation: Do not speed up. Do not say "I know this is getting into the weeds." Change the mode entirely — ask a question, show a demo, tell a quick story. "Let me stop here and ask you something." The mode change resets attention better than content change.

Signal: People are nodding and leaning in — unexpectedly early

What it usually means: You said something that resonated more than you anticipated. The room is with you.

Adaptation: Slow down. Let it breathe. The temptation is to keep going — you've got momentum. The more powerful move is to inhabit the moment. A brief pause and direct eye contact signals: this is the important part. Stay here.

Signal: The wrong question

What it usually means: The audience understood something differently from what you intended, or they're more advanced or more novice than you assumed.

Adaptation: Thank them genuinely ("That's useful — it tells me something about where I should have been clearer"). If you misjudged the audience level, adjust from that point forward without calling attention to the adjustment. Do not say "as I said earlier" — they heard you the first time; they just needed it said differently.

8:15–9:00 | Practice: The Same Talk, Three Rooms

Working in groups of three, students take two minutes of content and deliver it three times — once calibrated for a Rotary lunch, once for a high school assembly, once for a corporate audience. Observers identify what changed: word choice, tone, examples, energy level, assumed knowledge.

Debrief as a full group: What was hardest to change? What changed without thinking? What should never change regardless of audience? (Answer to the last: the truth of your content. Calibrate the delivery. Never calibrate the facts.)

WEEK 10 — Q&A Under Fire

Session Plan (3 hours, Tuesday 6:00–9:00 PM)

Note: This is the most demanding session of the program. Students should arrive having reviewed their signature talk draft.

6:00–6:20 | Why Q&A Is the Talk

Most speakers treat Q&A as the end of the talk. It is not. Q&A is the part of the talk where the audience finds out who you actually are. Anyone can deliver a prepared presentation. Q&A is live. It is the moment when the speaker either demonstrates genuine mastery or reveals that she memorized a script.

Q&A is also the part that gets you booked again or never again. A booker who saw your prepared remarks and was impressed will double their confidence if you handle a hard question well. The same booker who was impressed will halve their confidence if they watch you dodge, bluster, or crumble.

This session treats Q&A as a skill to be built, not a test of character or knowledge. You will practice tonight until you have the protocols in your body.

6:20–7:00 | Question Type Handling

There are six types of difficult Q&A questions. Each requires a different response protocol.

Type 1: The Technical Gotcha

What it looks like: "Doesn't the transformer architecture actually work very differently from what you described? You said the model 'reads' the text but that's not really accurate — it's doing matrix multiplication over token embeddings." Or: "Your data on parameter counts is from 2022. That's completely out of date."

What's happening: Someone with more technical knowledge than your audience — or more than you — is either genuinely correcting you, or testing you, or establishing their own expertise in the room.

The wrong response: Defending an error, or pretending confidence you don't have.

The right response:

  • If they're right: "You're right, and I appreciate the correction. Let me be more precise." Restate accurately. Move on. This response earns respect from everyone in the room, including the challenger.
  • If you're not sure: "I want to be careful here — I may have oversimplified for a general audience, and I may have gotten something wrong. What's the version you'd want me to say?" This is disarming and honest.
  • If they're wrong: "That's not quite my understanding — let me tell you what I was relying on." Cite your source. Don't argue. Offer to follow up after.

What never to do: Say "Great question" and then give an answer that doesn't engage with the challenge.

Type 2: The Political Ambush

What it looks like: "Why should we trust anything these tech companies tell us — they're just in it for profit." Or: "Isn't this AI boom just another way to surveil workers and suppress wages?" Or: "You said AI has risks, but the government is completely captured by Silicon Valley — why would anything change?"

What's happening: The person has a political or ideological frame that they are applying to your talk. The question contains real content and also contains energy that is not primarily about getting information.

The wrong response: Getting drawn into a political argument, or dismissing the concern.

The right response:

  • Acknowledge the legitimate content of the concern directly: "There's a real question inside that, and I want to take it seriously."
  • Separate what's established from what's contested: "The incentive structure you're describing is real and documented. What the effects of that are on outcomes is something researchers are actively studying, and I don't want to give you more certainty than I have."
  • Be clear about the limits of your role: "I'm not here to make the political argument — I genuinely don't think that's my lane. I'm here to give you accurate information so you can make your own political judgments."

What never to do: Take a political side, even if pressed. An AI literacy speaker who is perceived as politically aligned loses half the audience permanently.

Type 3: The Rambling Non-Question

What it looks like: Someone speaks for 90 seconds sharing their opinion, experience, or frustration, and ends with something that could be a question if you squint at it: "...and I just think we need to be really careful, and it's like, I've been working in education for twenty years and every few years there's a new thing that's going to revolutionize everything, and it never does, and maybe this is the same? Or maybe it's different?"

What's happening: The person has something they want to say. They used Q&A as the vehicle. This is legitimate — they're engaged. The room is watching you manage the time.

The right response:

  • Listen for the real question (often: "Is this different from past hypes, or the same?")
  • Name what you heard: "What I'm hearing is a question about whether this moment is different from previous technology cycles. Is that right?"
  • Answer that question. If there was no question, make a brief connection to their experience and move on: "Your twenty years in education is exactly the kind of experience that gives you a useful frame for evaluating these claims — and I think the honest answer is..."

What to avoid: Letting it run past 3–4 minutes. The rest of the room's Q&A time is not this person's. A kind, firm redirection after the question is named is professional, not rude.

Type 4: The Genuinely Hard Question You Don't Know the Answer To

What it looks like: "What is your view on the EU AI Act's biometric provision?" Or: "Can you explain how RLHF actually produces alignment? Doesn't it just produce compliance?" Or: "My company is about to deploy a hiring algorithm — what should I actually do?"

What's happening: You've been asked something that requires either specialized knowledge you may not have, or a judgment call that would require knowing more about their situation.

The wrong response: Guessing. Bluffing. Giving a general answer that sounds like it might address the specific question.

The right response:

  • "I don't know enough about [the EU AI Act's biometric provision] to give you a reliable answer, and this is the kind of topic where a wrong answer in front of a hundred people would be worse than no answer."
  • "Here's what I do know, which is adjacent: [true adjacent knowledge]."
  • "Here's how I'd find out: [specific resource, specific expert, specific report]."
  • If it's a judgment call about their situation: "I can tell you what the research says in general, but the specifics of your situation would require more information than I have." Offer to talk after.

The principle: "I don't know" from a speaker who has demonstrated knowledge is not a weakness. It is the signal that everything else they said was probably true.

Type 5: The Question from a Child

What it looks like: Any genuine question from someone who is young, or who asks in the way a child asks — direct, without the performance of sophistication. "Will robots take over?" or "Is AI alive?" or "Can AI be my friend?"

What's happening: An opportunity. These questions are the real questions. Every adult in the room has a version of "Is AI alive?" that they're too polished to ask.

The right response:

  • Take it completely seriously. Repeat the question so everyone hears it. Look at the child.
  • Answer it honestly and directly: "That's the question. Here's what I actually think..."
  • Your answer to a child's question will often be the most memorable moment of the entire talk.

What never to do: Condescend. "What a great question!" followed by an answer that isn't for them.

Type 6: The Question You've Already Answered

What it looks like: "But how does it actually learn the data?"

What's happening: Either you didn't explain it clearly enough, or the person wasn't listening, or the concept is hard enough that they need it again.

The right response: Don't say "as I mentioned earlier." They heard you; they need it differently.

  • Reframe: "Let me try that from a different angle."
  • Use a new analogy.
  • Check in: "Does that version of it help?"

WEEK 11 — BUILDING YOUR TOPIC AND YOUR BRAND {#week-11}

Session duration: 3 hours (6:00–9:00 PM) Deliverables this session: Draft speaker sheet, draft bio (all three lengths) Assignment due: None (Assignment 4 — full talk draft — due last session) Guest: Working speaker, $5K/$15K/$40K dates

Session Plan

6:00–6:15 — Opening Frame: The Niche Is a Gift

Open by writing on the board: "I speak about AI."

Ask the class: If you were booking a speaker for your Rotary club and received a pitch from someone whose website says "I speak about AI," would you book them? Pause. Let them answer.

Then write: "I help rural small-business owners understand what AI can actually do for a shop with three employees and no IT department."

Ask again. The room will feel the difference.

Key points to land:

  • "I speak about AI" is a category, not a brand. It tells a booker nothing about whether you're right for their audience.
  • A niche doesn't limit you — it makes you findable. The Rotary booker in a farming county can picture exactly who you are. The HR conference organizer knows you're not her person, which also saves everyone time.
  • You can still take general AI talks. But your marketing leads with the niche.
  • The niche is also a content filter: when something happens in the AI news, your niche tells you whether it's your story to tell.

Exercise (30 minutes, pairs then full group):

7:00–7:45 — Speaker Sheet and Bio Workshop

7:00–7:15 — What a Speaker Sheet Is and Isn't

A speaker sheet is not a resume. It is a sales document whose only job is to get a booker to say "I want to talk to this person."

Walk through the Speaker Sheet Template (see Business Templates section) pointing out:

  • Headline/tagline: One sentence. Not your bio. Not your topic list. The thing you want a booker to remember.
  • Talk titles and one-liners: A booker is not reading 300 words about each talk. They're scanning for whether you have something for their audience.
  • Social proof: Testimonials from real venues and real people, not generic praise. "Standing ovation" means nothing. "Fifty school board members left with a draft AI policy" means something.
  • Photo: Professional, current, not a conference badge photo. One that says "I belong on a stage."
  • Contact and logistics: Make it easy to book you. One email, one phone, one website.

7:15–7:30 — Bio Workshop

Explain the three lengths and their contexts:

  • 50-word bio: Emcee reads it before you walk on stage. Punchy, present tense, two facts and one hook.
  • 150-word bio: Conference program, event website, LinkedIn About section. Room to add one story or a credential that earns trust.
  • 300-word bio: Your own website, speaker bureau profile, grant applications. Full arc: who you were, what happened, who you are now, why it matters to this audience.

Common bio mistakes:

  • Starting with "I was born in..."
  • Listing credentials that don't build trust with the target audience (your audience is not impressed by your master's thesis)
  • Writing in first person when the bio will be read in third person
  • Burying the niche below three paragraphs of backstory
  • Describing yourself as "passionate" — everyone is passionate

Students draft all three lengths in class with the templates as a frame. They will refine for Assignment 5.

7:30–7:45 — Headshot and Demo Reel Briefing

Session Plan

6:00–6:30 — How Speakers Actually Get Booked

Most students imagine booking works like this: you send a pitch, they hire you. Walk them through the actual landscape.

Four booking channels:

1. Referral (the dominant channel at all fee levels) Someone who saw you speak tells someone who books speakers about you. This is how most paid bookings originate, especially in the first three years. Implications: every talk you give, paid or free, is an audition for the next one. The librarian in the audience has a sister-in-law on the chamber board. Treat every room like it has your next client in it — because statistically, several rooms per year will.

2. Inbound web (grows slowly, then accelerates) A booker Googles "AI speaker for [city] [context]" and your website appears. This requires: a functional website with a clear niche statement, talk titles, a contact form that works, and updated content. It also requires time — most speakers don't see significant inbound for 12–18 months after launching a site. SEO is real but slow.

3. Direct outreach (underused by beginners) You identify a venue or organization that would benefit from your talk and you reach out directly. This works better than speakers expect, especially for civic organizations (Rotary, chambers of commerce, library systems, school districts, municipal departments). These organizations often have no system for finding speakers and are grateful when a credible person contacts them. The pitch must be tight: who you are, what you offer, why their audience specifically, and a clear ask.

4. Speaker bureaus (appropriate after you have traction) Bureaus are middlemen who connect you with clients, typically at higher fee levels. They take 25–30%. They are not interested in new speakers without a track record, a demo reel, and a minimum fee of around $5K (below that their cut isn't worth their effort). Getting on a bureau's roster requires: a strong demo reel, a clear niche, a professional website, and either a warm introduction or evidence of significant bookings. Don't pursue bureaus in Year 1. Do pursue them in Year 2–3 if you have traction.

Class discussion: Where do people expect their first bookings to come from? Where do they expect most of their bookings to come from in Year 3? Map the gap.

6:30–7:30 — Pricing Without Flinching

The tiered fee structure (distribute worksheet — see Business Templates):

Walk through each tier with concrete examples of what it looks like.

Tier 0 — Community / Mission-aligned / Pro Bono ($0)

This is not "free because you're not worth paying." This is strategic investment in your community and your pipeline. Every working speaker gives talks in this tier. The question is not whether to, but when and how many.

Examples: your local public library's AI literacy series, a PTA meeting at your kid's school, a community college open house, a neighborhood association, a congregation you belong to, a nonprofit whose mission you believe in.

Rule: never resent a free talk you chose to give. If you'd resent giving it, charge or decline.

The community engine (covered in Week 13) is built on 6–10 of these per year. They are not charity — they are how you stay connected to the people you serve, build word-of-mouth, and maintain the authenticity that makes paid work sustainable.

Tier 1 — Local / Regional Non-Profit / Association ($500–$5,000)

Chambers of commerce, professional associations, school districts, city departments, small conferences. Often paying from program budgets rather than event budgets. Often price-sensitive but not trying to exploit you.

Starting range for new speakers: $500–$1,500. Raise to $2,500–$5,000 as bookings accumulate and you have a track record.

What you provide: the talk, any materials you choose to share, availability for 30 minutes before/after for informal conversation. What they typically provide: room, AV (sometimes), introduction.

Tier 2 — Corporate / Mid-Size Conference / Regional Event ($5,000–$15,000)

Corporate lunch-and-learns, industry conferences, company all-hands, HR departments, law firms. They have event budgets. They are used to paying for speakers.

New speakers are often shocked that organizations pay $5K–$10K for a talk. They shouldn't be — organizations routinely pay that for catering, a keynote, or a venue upgrade. A good speaker who leaves the room with a changed perspective is worth more than a catered lunch.

What you provide: the talk, often pre-event consulting call (30–60 min) to tailor content, post-event Q&A, sometimes a brief workshop module. What they typically provide: travel expenses (airfare, hotel, ground transportation), AV and technical support, a green room or holding space.

Tier 3 — National Conference / Major Corporate ($15,000–$40,000+)

Large conferences, Fortune 500 keynotes, industry association national meetings. These are the dates a new speaker builds toward over 3–5 years, not the dates to pitch in Year 1.

What makes the difference at this tier: a strong, often-viewed demo reel; testimonials from recognizable organizations; a clearly defined reputation in a specific niche; and in many cases, representation by a bureau or a booking agent.

The pricing conversation:

Role-play: a booker emails asking "What are your fees?" Walk students through the response.

The response is not: "Whatever works for your budget!" (projects desperation, leaves money on the table, puts the burden on them)

The response is not: a three-paragraph explanation of why you're worth paying. (nobody asked)

The response is:

"My fee for a keynote is $[X]. For workshop formats, $[Y]. I do offer a limited number of community-rate engagements at reduced fees for libraries, schools, and nonprofits — if your organization falls into that category, let me know and we can discuss what works. Happy to get on a 15-minute call to make sure I'm the right fit for your audience before we go further."

This response: states the number without apology, shows tiered awareness, opens the community-rate door without inviting abuse of it, and moves toward a call rather than a price negotiation by email.

On raising your fees:

Fees go up in two situations: (a) you're getting more inquiries than you can handle, which means you're underpriced, or (b) you've accumulated enough testimonials and video evidence that your tier has genuinely moved. You do not need to grandfather old clients at old rates unless you want to. Most established speakers raise fees once per year.

WEEK 13 — MARKETING WITHOUT BEING INSUFFERABLE {#week-13}

Session Plan

6:00–6:15 — Opening: The Audience You Build Versus the Audience You Rent

Framing question: What's the difference between buying an ad and writing a useful LinkedIn post?

The ad: you pay for attention. When you stop paying, the attention stops. The useful post: you earn attention. It compounds over time. The person who found your post on AI and school boards six months ago might be the parent who becomes a PTA president who books you next spring.

The core principle: In the attention economy, the speaker who is most useful for free builds the most credible case for being worth paying.

6:15–7:15 — Platform by Platform: What Works, What Wastes Time

LinkedIn (highest ROI for most students in this room)

Why LinkedIn: your clients are on LinkedIn. School administrators, HR directors, association leaders, C-suite executives, journalists covering enterprise topics — they live here. Instagram and TikTok reach different audiences.

What works:

  • Posts that teach something concrete in 150–300 words. "Here's what I told a room of 200 school board members about AI and student privacy, and what happened after."
  • Posts that share a specific moment from a talk — a question an audience member asked, a misconception you keep encountering, a statistic that surprised you.
  • Posts that disagree with a mainstream AI narrative, with evidence. Contrarian takes that are well-reasoned get shared.
  • Posts that link to your writing elsewhere (newsletter, op-ed) with a one-paragraph hook.

What doesn't work:

  • "Excited to announce my new website!"
  • Generic AI news commentary that adds nothing to what's already in the article.
  • Engagement bait ("Do you agree? Comment below!")
  • Photos of you at a conference without context about what you learned there.

Frequency: 2–3 times per week, not every day. Quality over volume.

Newsletter (builds the most loyal pipeline)

A newsletter that goes directly to someone's inbox is more durable than any social platform. You own the list. When LinkedIn changes its algorithm, your newsletter survives.

What a good speaker newsletter includes:

  • One useful thing: an AI development that landed this week and what it actually means for your niche audience.
  • One field note: something you observed in a recent talk — a question that keeps coming up, a moment that shifted the room.
  • One pointer: a resource (article, podcast, tool) that your audience will actually use.

Frequency: monthly to start. Bi-weekly once you have the habit and the content.

Platform options: Substack, Beehiiv, Kit (formerly ConvertKit). Start free. Migrate later if needed.

Podcast appearances (leveraged reach, low ongoing effort)

You don't build the audience — you borrow someone else's. One podcast appearance puts you in front of 1,000–50,000 people who already trust the host.

How to find the right podcasts: listen to what your target clients listen to. A podcast for HR professionals, a podcast for school administrators, a podcast for small-business owners, a podcast for local government leaders — each is a direct line to a booking audience.

How to pitch yourself as a guest:

  • Listen to at least three episodes before pitching.
  • Pitch a specific angle, not "I'd love to talk about AI."
  • Keep the pitch under 150 words. Subject line: "Guest idea: [specific topic] for [podcast name] audience."
  • Include: who you are in one sentence, what the episode would be about in one sentence, one credential that earns trust.

Op-eds and contributed articles

Local newspaper, regional business journal, trade publication in your niche — these are booker-facing and SEO-building.

The local newspaper is underrated. A 600-word piece in your city's paper explaining what AI means for local schools or small businesses puts you in front of every booker in your community. It also gives you a published clip to link to in pitch emails.

Trade publications (for your niche) are highly targeted. A piece in a hospital administration trade magazine, a school leadership journal, or a legal industry publication goes directly to the decision-makers who book speakers.

7:15–7:45 — The 90/10 Rule in Practice

Write on the board: 90% useful. 10% promotional.

This is not a formula. It's a mindset.

What "useful" looks like:

  • Teaching something concrete about AI that your audience can apply or share.
  • Sharing a genuine reaction to something in the news, not a hot take, but a considered perspective.
  • Pointing to a resource that actually helps people.
  • Telling a story from your speaking practice that illuminates something real about how audiences think about AI.

What "promotional" looks like (the 10%):

  • Announcing an upcoming talk.
  • Sharing a testimonial from a recent booking.
  • Mentioning your fee sheet is available.
  • Noting that you're accepting bookings for Q3.

The problem with inverting the ratio:

Show examples (instructor prepares 3–4 examples in advance):

Too promotional: "So excited to be speaking at [conference] next month! If you haven't hired me yet, now's the time. DM me for availability."

Useful: "I spoke to a Rotary club last week where someone in the back row asked me if AI was going to replace their accountant. Here's what I said — and the part of my answer that surprised me most."

The promotional example might get 12 likes from people who are being polite. The useful example might get shared by someone who runs an accountant's association who didn't know you existed.

Class exercise (10 minutes):

Students write one "useful" post right now — a real observation from their AI work, a moment from a classroom talk, something they've been thinking about. Share one or two aloud. Brief group feedback: useful or promotional? Teachable or self-serving?

7:45–8:15 — The Community Engine

This is the strategy that keeps the practice sustainable and honest.

The structure:

Commit to 6–10 free or community-rate talks per year delivered to libraries, schools, parent groups, civic organizations, congregations, and similar venues. Not as charity. As infrastructure.

Why this works:

  1. Word-of-mouth pipeline. A librarian who saw a good AI talk tells the school board chair. The school board chair tells the district's PD coordinator. The PD coordinator is booking professional development sessions and needs a speaker. This chain happens. Not every time, but regularly.
  1. Feedback that money can't buy. A paid corporate audience is polite. A free community audience asks the questions they're actually afraid of. The parent at the library who asks "Is my kid's AI tutor reporting what they type to someone?" — that question is better preparation for hard Q&A than any mock session.
  1. Content engine. Every free talk generates material: a question you hadn't thought of, a misconception that's more common than you realized, a story that landed unexpectedly. The free talks make your paid talks better.
  1. Mission alignment. This is the part many students are reluctant to say out loud: if you care about AI literacy in your community, the free talks are the mission. The paid talks fund the mission. Holding those two things together is not a contradiction — it's a sustainable practice.

Making it sustainable:

  • Cap it at 6–10 per year. Not 30. Not "whenever someone asks." You need to be able to say no to the 11th free request.
  • Build the community talks into your calendar in January. Four talks per quarter, then decide which to keep free.
  • Track them. Not for the revenue (there isn't any), but for the pipeline. Who was in the room? Did anyone follow up? Three months later, what came of it?
  • Make your community-rate policy clear on your website. "I offer a limited number of community-rate engagements each year for public libraries, K-12 schools, faith communities, and civic organizations. Contact me to inquire." This wording tells civic bookers they can approach you while signaling that paid clients get something different.

Example community engine in action:

Walk through a hypothetical year:

  • January: Two library talks on "AI and Your Family" (free)
  • February: PTA meeting in a school district where you'd love to eventually book professional development ($0, but the principal is in the room)
  • March: Church adult education series, three sessions ($200 total for materials — not your time)
  • May: Community college open house panel (free, but radio station is there)
  • June: City council briefing on AI and municipal services (free — the council members run the city that hires vendors who hire speakers)
  • September: Union hall workforce briefing ($0, but three companies whose employees belong to this union are in the room)
  • October: Public library speaker series ($500 community rate)
  • November: Chamber of commerce lunch (this one's paid — $2,500 — partly because of the relationship built at the January library talk)

Total community talks: 8. Total time invested: ~16 hours of speaking, ~8 hours of prep.

45-Minute Talk Critique Protocol

Instructor uses this protocol during each talk. Share with class before the session so peer feedback is structured.

Section A: Opening (first 5 minutes)

Observe and note:

  • Did the speaker open with a story, a question, or a surprising fact — or did they open with a self-introduction and an agenda slide?
  • Was the hook strong enough that you'd stay if you were a stranger who wandered in?
  • Was the niche evident within the first 90 seconds?
  • How was the body language in the first 60 seconds — stiff, grounded, or performing?

Section B: Content Structure (minutes 5–35)

Observe and note:

  • Is there a clear arc? (Not just a list of topics.)
  • How many core ideas does the talk contain? A 45-minute talk should have 3–5 core ideas, not 12.
  • Are the transitions explicit or does the speaker just move from one topic to the next without signaling?
  • Where did the room's energy drop? (Note the timestamp.)
  • Where was there a moment of genuine surprise or delight? (Note the timestamp.)
  • Did every live demo work? If not, how did the speaker handle failure?
  • Was anything factually questionable? (Flag for debrief — not in the room during the talk.)

Section C: Story and Illustration (throughout)

Observe and note:

  • How many stories did the talk contain? Were they real, specific, and brief?
  • Were analogies accurate? (This matters more for AI talks than most topics — an inaccurate analogy is a talking point for someone who knows better.)
  • Were there any moments where the speaker said "studies show" or "research has found" without citing what study or what research? (Flag.)

Section D: Voice, Body, Presence (throughout)

Observe and note:

  • Was the speaker's voice audible and varied, or flat and monotone?
  • Were there extended filler runs (um, uh, like, you know)? Estimate how frequently per minute.
  • Did the speaker move purposefully or wander?
  • Did the speaker look at the screen or at the audience? (Note ratio estimate.)
  • Was the pacing appropriate, too fast, or too slow? (Note the sections.)
  • Did the speaker use silence as a tool, or did they fill every pause?

Section E: Q&A (15 minutes)

Observe and note:

  • Did the speaker bridge well from the talk to Q&A, or did Q&A feel like an appendix?
  • Rate each Q&A response:
  • Direct answer: answered the question directly and moved on
  • Bridged well: used a technique to shift without evading
  • Avoided: ducked the question
  • Overclaimed: said something they can't support
  • Recovered: initially answered poorly, then corrected or refined
  • Did the speaker ever say "I don't know"? If so, was it graceful?
  • Was there a hostile or gotcha question? How did the speaker handle it?

Section F: Close

Observe and note:

  • Did the talk have a real ending, or did it just stop?
  • What is the one thing the audience is supposed to remember, do, or feel? Was it stated explicitly?
  • Would this ending make someone want to hire the speaker again?

Structured Critique Format (15 minutes per talk)

Minutes 1–3: Speaker's self-assessment

Instructor asks: "What was the moment in that talk that you're most satisfied with, and what's the one thing you'd fix right now if you could?"

Let the speaker answer first. Do not interrupt. This reveals whether their self-perception matches what the room saw.

Minutes 4–8: Specific strengths from class

Instructor calls on three or four class members: "Give me one specific, concrete moment — with a timestamp if you can — that worked. Not 'I thought it was good overall.' A moment."

This is not about being kind. It is about identifying what to replicate and amplify. Vague praise is useless. "Around minute 22 when you said [specific thing] and someone in the second row visibly leaned forward — that's the kind of moment that gets you rebooked" is useful.

Minutes 9–13: One or two specific improvements

Instructor identifies the highest-leverage change. Not a list of everything. One, maybe two. The rest goes in the written critique.

Rule: never critique more than two things aloud in a single session. The speaker can only hold two things. The rest is noise.

Minute 14: Instructor commits to written feedback

"I'll have your full written critique to you via LMS by [date]. It will have everything from the protocol above. Tonight, take home these two things: [specific strengths] and [specific change]."

Minute 15: Transition

If there's a following talk, the speaker takes their seat and the next speaker sets up.

One-on-One Coaching Checklist

To be completed by instructor before or after the Dress Rehearsal session, and available during office hours in the week before Showcase Night.

Each session: 15–20 minutes. Instructor reviews the critique protocol notes before the meeting.

Opening (2 minutes):

  • "How are you feeling about the talk right now, the day after watching the footage?"
  • Let them talk. Don't redirect immediately.

Content check (5 minutes):

  • [ ] Does the talk have a clear argument or is it a survey of AI topics?
  • [ ] Is there anything factually questionable that needs to be corrected before Showcase Night?
  • [ ] Are the live demos stable? Has the student tested them in the last 48 hours?
  • [ ] Does the 45-minute version translate cleanly to 15 minutes for Showcase? (It should already be mapped.)

Delivery check (5 minutes):

  • [ ] What is the speaker's one persistent physical habit? (Looking at slides, rocking, rushing through transitions) — is it improving?
  • [ ] What is the speaker's strongest vocal quality? Are they using it intentionally?
  • [ ] Does the speaker have a plan for the first 30 seconds if their nerves spike? (Breath, eye contact with one friendly face, pause before the opening line)

  • [ ] Have they practiced saying "I don't know, and here's how I'd find out" for at least one question?

  • [ ] What is the speaker wearing? (Not directive — but have they thought about it?)
  • [ ] Is the speaker's name, niche, and contact information on the last slide?

WEEK 15 — SHOWCASE NIGHT {#week-15}

Session duration: 3 hours (6:00–9:00 PM) + 30-minute reception Format: Public event with invited guests Each student delivers: 15-minute version of signature talk + Q&A

Showcase Night Structure

Pre-Event Setup (4:30–6:00)

Room configuration:

  • Theater seating, not classroom seating. Rows facing a stage area or clearly defined speaking zone.
  • A podium or music stand for notes (optional — students may prefer not to use one).
  • AV fully tested: projector, microphone (handheld or lapel — student preference), clicker, screen.
  • Water on stage. Timer visible to speaker but not to audience.
  • Name placards at speaker check-in. Badge table with a sign-in sheet for guests.
  • Reserve three to five chairs labeled "booker / community leader" near the front if space allows — this signals to the room that these are VIP seats and positions guests to interact with speakers at the reception.
  • Light refreshments set up at the back for the reception period.

Invited guests (invite at least 6 weeks in advance):

  • Public librarians (local library system director, branch managers)
  • School administrators (district superintendent, principals, assistant superintendents)
  • Journalists (local paper education or tech reporter, public radio)
  • Civic organization leaders (Rotary, chamber of commerce, Lions Club)
  • Nonprofit directors in the community
  • City council members or their chiefs of staff
  • Corporate L&D and HR contacts (if students have them in their network)
  • Alumni from previous cohorts (who may know bookable contacts)

Invitation language:

"[Program name] invites you to Showcase Night — a public event featuring [N] speakers presenting original, research-backed talks on artificial intelligence for general audiences. These are working professionals — educators, lawyers, healthcare workers, and community leaders — who have spent 15 weeks developing talks specifically for civic, community, and organizational audiences. The evening runs from 6:00 to approximately 8:30, with a reception following. There is no charge to attend. Several speakers from past cohorts have booked paid engagements from relationships that began at this event. We hope to see you there."

6:00–6:10 — Welcome and Framing

Instructor opens. Keep it under 10 minutes. The audience came to hear the students, not the instructor.

Points to cover:

  • What this program is and who is in the room. (One paragraph.)
  • What the audience is about to see: original 15-minute talks by people who spent 15 weeks building and refining them. Real talks, real content, no rehearsed cheerleading.
  • Logistics: each talk is 15 minutes, followed by 5 minutes of Q&A. Brief transitions between speakers. Reception immediately following the final talk.
  • One explicit invitation to guests: "If you're a booker or community organizer and you see someone tonight whose talk fits your audience, please introduce yourself at the reception. That's why we hold this event."

6:10–8:30 — Talks

Format per speaker:

Segment Duration
Introduction (read by emcee or instructor)60–90 seconds
Talk15 minutes
Q&A5 minutes
Transition2 minutes

Emcee notes:

  • Use the speaker's 50-word bio for the introduction. Read it, don't improvise.
  • Time the talk. Signal at 13 minutes (one finger), cut off gently but firmly at 16 minutes.
  • Facilitate Q&A from the audience. Have two or three prepared questions in case the audience is slow to start.
  • Keep transitions crisp. The pacing of the event matters. A sloppy 5-minute transition between six speakers eats 25 minutes.

Running order:

  • Do not put the strongest speaker first or last as a rule. Put someone strong first to set the tone. End with someone who is emotionally resonant, not just technically competent. Mid-order is where the technically strongest talk often lands best — the audience is warmed up.
  • Avoid back-to-back speakers whose topics are too similar. Vary the niche and the audience focus.

The 15-Minute Talk Format

The 15-minute version is not the 45-minute talk with 30 minutes cut. It is a different talk built on the same foundation.

Structure:

Section Duration Purpose
Hook60–90 secOne story, one question, or one surprising fact that establishes the stakes
Core idea 13–4 minThe most important thing this audience needs to understand
Core idea 23–4 minThe thing that surprises them or changes how they see the first idea
Bridge to action2–3 minWhat this means for their community, organization, or family
Close60–90 secThe one sentence they take home

What to cut from the 45-minute version:

  • Two of the three main sections (you keep one)
  • Most of the Q&A material gets incorporated into the talk so the live Q&A is shorter
  • Most of the credential-building (the 15-minute format earns trust through content, not biography)
  • The live demo, unless it is absolutely central and takes under 90 seconds

What to keep:

  • The opening hook (often the same or nearly the same)
  • The single strongest story from the 45-minute version
  • The close (often identical)
  • One live demo if the technology is stable and the demo takes under 90 seconds

Practice protocol for the 15-minute version:

Students should practice the 15-minute version separately from the 45-minute version. It is not the same talk. Practice it at least five times before Showcase Night:

  • Once to time it cold
  • Once cutting anything over 16 minutes
  • Once with a trusted friend or family member for the "do you understand this?" test
  • Once without slides, just to know you can
  • Once in front of a mirror or on camera, watching for the habits flagged in the Dress Rehearsal critique

Post-Event Networking Protocol

8:30–9:00 — Reception

This 30 minutes is a required part of the program. Students do not leave after their talk.

Instructor's opening of the reception:

"Thank you to everyone who came tonight. Our speakers will be at the reception for the next 30 minutes. If you're interested in bringing any of these talks to your community, school, library, or organization, please introduce yourself. Our speakers have contact information available and are actively booking. We'll see you there."

Student preparation for the reception (brief in Week 14 and again at pre-event check-in):

What to have ready:

  • 10–15 business cards or a QR code that links to your speaker sheet or website.
  • A one-sentence pitch that is not a repeat of your talk title: "I help [niche]. If you work with [their org type], I'd love to talk about whether there's a fit."
  • An honest answer to "how much do you charge?" that includes a range, not a dodge.

Reception conversation framework:

Opening: Let them come to you if they came to you. Don't open with "Did you enjoy my talk?" Open with a question about them: "Which part resonated most with your work?"

If they seem interested in booking:

  1. Ask about their audience and context. ("Tell me more about who would be in the room.")
  2. Share a brief relevant example. ("I did a similar talk for a group of school principals last spring — the part about AI and student data privacy generated the most questions.")
  3. Offer a clear next step. ("I'd love to send you my speaker sheet. Can I get your card or email?")

If they're not sure what they need:

  • Don't pitch. Ask. "What's the AI question coming up most often in your work right now?"
  • Leave them with one useful insight, not a sales pitch. The useful insight is the brand.

The follow-up (within 48 hours):

Every person who expressed interest gets a follow-up email within 48 hours. The template:

"Dear [Name], Thank you for coming to Showcase Night — it was great to meet you. As I mentioned, I speak about [niche] for [audience type]. I've attached my speaker sheet and would love to find 15 minutes to learn more about your organization and whether there's a fit. My fee range for engagements like yours is [range]. I'm available on [specific dates/times]. Would any of those work for a brief call?"

If they don't respond: one follow-up at the two-week mark. Then stop.

Instructor's post-showcase notes (for file):

After each cohort's Showcase Night, record:

  • Number of guests who attended
  • Number who took a speaker's card or information
  • Number of follow-up inquiries reported by students (collect at Week 15 + 2 debrief)
  • Any same-night bookings or expressions of intent to book
  • Guest feedback: what worked, what to change about the event

This data informs future cohort recruitment ("several students book paid dates out of Showcase Night") and is useful for grant applications or program justification.

Reflection Essay Prompt and Rubric {#reflection-essay-prompt-and-rubric}

Due two weeks after the final session. Submitted via LMS. 2,000 words.

Prompt

You began this program by recording a 3-minute video explaining AI to a smart 14-year-old. You end it having built and delivered a signature talk to a real audience, a complete speaker kit, and a pricing and contracting framework for a working practice.

Write a 2,000-word reflection on what happened in between. This is not a summary of the course. It is an honest account of your own development as a communicator, a thinker about AI, and a practitioner building a speaking practice. It is also a planning document for the next six months of your work.

Address these four areas:

1. What you learned (500–600 words)

What do you understand now about AI — its capabilities, its limits, its social consequences — that you did not understand 15 weeks ago? Be specific. Name the concept, the moment it clicked, and how it changed what you say. Identify at least one thing you believed at the start of the course that turned out to be wrong, and how you corrected it.

2. What surprised you (400–500 words)

What surprised you most about the experience of becoming a public speaker on AI? This may be about yourself (a strength you didn't know you had, a habit you didn't know was getting in your way), about audiences (what they actually care about, how different groups respond to the same material), or about the field itself (something you learned from a guest speaker, a classmate's talk, or Q&A that shifted your thinking). Avoid telling us what you expected to be surprised by. Tell us what actually surprised you.

3. The talk you gave at Showcase Night (300–400 words)

Describe the experience of delivering your 15-minute talk to a real audience on Showcase Night. What worked? What didn't? What did you learn about your own material from watching the audience respond to it? If you could deliver it again the next day with full memory of how it went, what would you change and why?

4. Your six-month plan (400–500 words)

You have a speaker kit, a fee structure, and a signed commitment to give 6–10 community talks per year. What are you actually going to do with them in the next six months? Name specific targets: organizations you plan to contact, publications you plan to pitch, communities you plan to serve. Name a realistic revenue target for your first year of speaking. Name the one thing that is most likely to prevent you from following through — and your specific plan for dealing with it.

Rubric

Satisfactory / Unsatisfactory — Required for Certificate

To receive a Satisfactory, the essay must meet all four criteria below. An essay that does not meet any one criterion will be returned for revision (one revision allowed within 10 days of the return).

Criterion 1: Specificity (Required)

The essay contains concrete, specific details rather than general statements.

  • Satisfactory: The essay names specific AI concepts, specific moments from the course or from delivering talks, specific venues or audiences or guest speakers, and specific plans (named organizations, named formats, named dates or timeframes).
  • Unsatisfactory: The essay makes general statements without evidence ("I learned a lot about AI," "I became more comfortable speaking," "I plan to build my practice").

Criterion 2: Honesty (Required)

The essay engages honestly with difficulty, failure, or uncertainty.

  • Satisfactory: The essay names something that was hard — a concept the student struggled to understand, a talk that didn't land, a moment in Q&A they handled poorly, a bias or assumption they had to correct. The essay does not read like a testimonial.
  • Unsatisfactory: The essay is uniformly positive without acknowledging struggle. Every section describes growth without naming what grew from.

Criterion 3: Correction (Required)

The essay names at least one thing the student believed at the start of the course that turned out to be wrong, and explains how they corrected it.

  • Satisfactory: A specific belief is named ("I thought AI could not reason, only match patterns" / "I assumed my audience would be afraid of AI — they were mostly confused" / "I thought speakers could charge whatever they want if they were confident"). The correction is also specific: what evidence or experience changed the belief, and how the student's practice changed as a result.
  • Unsatisfactory: The student cannot name a specific belief they corrected, or names only a very minor one, or describes the correction as "learning more about AI" without naming what specifically they got wrong.

Criterion 4: Concrete Six-Month Plan (Required)

The six-month plan is actionable and specific enough to be evaluated in six months.

  • Satisfactory: The plan names at least three specific targets (by type and, where possible, by name) — organizations to contact, publications to pitch, communities to serve. It states a revenue target or booking number. It names the most likely obstacle and a specific (not generic) plan for addressing it.
  • Unsatisfactory: The plan is general ("I will continue to speak in my community," "I will work on my marketing," "I plan to reach out to various organizations").

Return essays that do not meet any criterion with specific, written feedback identifying exactly which criterion is not met and what is required for revision. Do not return essays that meet all four criteria but are imperfect in other ways. The goal of the essay is honest reflection and concrete planning, not academic prose.
