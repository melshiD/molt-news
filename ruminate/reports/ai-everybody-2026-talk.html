Part 1

45-Minute Talk Outline

The talk is structured in four movements: grounding (why this talk, what's actually happening), the landscape (amazing and concerning in balance), navigation (what to do about it), and closing (what happens now). Each segment has a clear emotional goal, not just an information goal.

0:00–2:00
Opening Hook — The one number that changes everything
2 minutes
  • Open with one number: 60 days. ChatGPT went from zero to 100 million users in 60 days. Faster than smartphones. Faster than social media. Faster than the internet itself.
  • Let that land. Then: "That was November 2022. We're now in April 2026. I'm going to tell you what happened in between — and why it matters to you specifically."
  • Brief speaker credibility + empathy: "I know many of you have been getting bits and pieces of this — headlines, dinner table arguments, things your kids or grandkids said. My job tonight is to give you the full picture."
  • Set the frame: "This talk will not predict the future. Nobody can do that honestly. But it will give you a calibrated map of what's real, what's hype, what's genuinely amazing, what's legitimately concerning, and — crucially — what you can actually do."
Tone: warm, grounded, slightly conspiratorial — "let me tell you what's actually going on." Not a lecture. A conversation.
2:00–10:00
The Story — From chatbot to infrastructure in 3 years
8 minutes
  • Walk the timeline as a story, not a list. Each event should feel like a chapter.
  • 2022: Everyone thought it was clever but nothing serious. (The "it's just autocomplete" era)
  • 2023: Images, code, voice — three different industries disrupted at once. People started paying attention.
  • Early 2024: AI scores better than most humans on the bar exam, medical boards, GRE. "At this point, the 'just autocomplete' defense became hard to say with a straight face."
  • May 2024: GPT-4o — real-time voice and vision. The movie Her, but real. "You could have a conversation with it like a person."
  • October 2024: AI that thinks before answering — reliably PhD-level on hard math and science.
  • January 2025: DeepSeek moment — Chinese lab matches GPT with fraction of resources. NVIDIA loses $600 billion in one day. "The assumption that only trillion-dollar companies could do this? Gone."
  • 2025: Agents — AI stops talking and starts doing. Writing code, sending emails, managing workflows. "The conversation changed from 'what can it say?' to 'what can it do?'"
  • April 2026: Three major new AI models in one week. "This is normal now."
  • Key message to land: "We are not in a hype cycle. We are in the early years of something comparable to electrification — a technology that will take decades to play out and is already irreversible."
Pacing: Move through this briskly — you want energy, not depth. The audience will want to linger. Don't let them yet. Say "I'll come back to that" as needed.
10:00–17:00
What AI Actually Does — The honest map
7 minutes
  • Lead with calibration: "Before we talk about impact, let's be honest about what AI can actually do. Both the enthusiasts and the skeptics get this wrong."
  • What it does well: writing, code, translation, explanation, synthesis from documents you provide. Real productivity gains — developers are 55% faster. Real democratization — capabilities that used to cost hundreds per hour, now free.
  • The intern analogy: "Think of the best, most tireless, most encyclopedic intern you can imagine. That's AI. But here's the catch: they sometimes make things up. Confidently. Fluently. And you can't tell when."
  • The hallucination problem — explain simply. "AI generates what sounds like an answer. When it doesn't know, it doesn't say so — it generates anyway. Studies show it's actually 34% more likely to sound certain when it's wrong."
  • The reliability table — walk through a few examples to make it concrete. Medical symptom checking: 4–5 out of 10. Summarizing a document you give it: 8–9 out of 10. The question to always ask: "Is this verifiable? Did I provide the source material?"
  • Close this section: "The rule is simple: trust AI to help you think, not to tell you facts. Verify anything that matters."
This is the section where skeptics in the room will nod. They need this grounding before the amazing section lands credibly.
17:00–24:00
The Amazing — Things worth being genuinely excited about
7 minutes
  • Transition: "Now that we have calibrated expectations — let me tell you about things that genuinely took my breath away."
  • Protein folding: "For 50 years, figuring out one protein's shape could take a PhD student their entire dissertation. There are 200 million known proteins. In 2024, AI solved this problem. The drug discovery pipeline is now fundamentally different."
  • Antibiotics: "No new antibiotic class since 1987. AI screened 36 million molecules and found candidates that eliminate MRSA in mouse models. Within months. Nobody planned for this."
  • The crop disease app: Tell the Kenya story. 1 million users. Free. In Swahili. "One agricultural extension officer can serve 5,000 farmers. This app serves every farmer with a phone."
  • The single mother legal appeal story: "A single mother working two jobs. Special education appeal. No attorney, no money for one. Used free AI to draft a proper legal response with correct citations. Won."
  • The rare disease diagnosis story: "A 12-year-old. Three years. 14 specialists. No answer. AI flagged a pattern across all his records that no individual doctor had seen. The diagnosis was confirmed. Treatment began."
  • Close: "These aren't promotional materials. These are documented outcomes. And they represent something real: access to expertise that was previously rationed by wealth. That matters."
Let the emotion of the stories do work here. Pause after the rare disease story. Let it breathe.
24:00–33:00
The Concerning — Real risks, honestly addressed
9 minutes
  • Transition: "Now I need to tell you the hard parts. And I'm going to be specific, because vague concern is useless."
  • Jobs: "AI is disrupting white-collar cognitive work first — reversing 200 years of automation history. The people most at risk are entry-level knowledge workers. That matters because those jobs have historically been how people built careers." WEF numbers: 92M displaced, 170M created, but not the same people, not the same places.
  • Gender dimension: "79% of employed women are in high-automation-risk jobs versus 58% of men. This isn't a coincidence — it's the legacy of which jobs women were channeled into. Without intervention, AI accelerates existing inequalities."
  • Scams: "In 2025, AI-powered scams increased 1,210%. Voice cloning from 3 seconds of audio. A finance professional at a major firm transferred $25.6 million after a video call where the entire team — including the CFO — were AI deepfakes. They had no idea." The grandparent scam. The urgency. The family code word.
  • Power concentration: "Seven organizations control the AI systems billions of people use. In 2025, two of them captured 14% of all global venture capital across every sector in the world. Individual billionaires currently have more influence over AI development than any government or democratic process."
  • The regulation gap: EU AI Act is real. The US has no comprehensive law. "The most consequential technology decisions of our era are being made largely outside democratic accountability."
  • Social fabric: AI companions, teen mental health, credentials erosion. Not to scare — but because these are documented effects unfolding now.
  • Close: "None of this is inevitable. Every one of these problems has policy solutions, personal mitigation, and collective responses. We'll get to those."
Watch the room here. People in jobs that are threatened need to be acknowledged, not dismissed. Validate before pivoting to solutions.
33:00–40:00
Personal Navigation — A guide for where you actually are
7 minutes
  • Acknowledge the range in the room: "I know there are people here at very different stages of life and very different relationships to this. Let me try to address several of you directly."
  • Parents: College prep. AI doesn't invalidate education — it changes what matters. Durable skills. The right question is: "What does this produce that AI can't verify?" Use the College Scorecard.
  • Mid-career: The three-category audit. "What AI does better, what it assists, what it can't touch. Your 10–20 years of context are your competitive advantage — but only if you identify it clearly."
  • Retirees: Tools and threats. Medical information before appointments. Legal document explanations. And the scam protection: the family code word. Do this tonight.
  • Small business owners: "The competition is already using AI. The barrier to entry is near zero. You can match capabilities that previously required teams. The question is which tasks, not whether."
  • Everyone: The three-question anxiety check. "Is this worry about now or a projected future? Is it within my control? What's one thing I can do in the next 30 days?"
  • Transition: "Research consistently shows people who understand the technology they fear are less anxious than those who avoid it. You've just done a significant piece of that work tonight."
This section needs to feel personal. Make eye contact. Slow down. The audience needs to feel seen, not just informed.
40:00–43:00
What You Can Do — Specific, not general
3 minutes
  • This week: One AI tool on a real task. One family code word established. Tell one person one thing from this talk.
  • 30 days: Five real AI experiments. Review your privacy settings. Read one article a week on AI in your industry.
  • 6 months: One structured course. Map your job's three task categories. One conversation with your employer about AI.
  • 1 year: Support one accountability organization. Make one career decision informed by AI trends.
  • Policy: "The window for meaningful influence on AI policy is open right now. It will not be open forever. Vote on AI. Call your representatives about specific bills. The rule-writing happening now will shape AI for decades."
Keep this tight and energetic. Action lists can drag. Get through them at pace.
43:00–45:00
Closing — The framing that matters
2 minutes
  • The closing frame: "The question people most often ask is: 'Should I be scared?' My honest answer is: not scared, but attentive. This is a genuinely transformative moment. Attentive people who take specific actions in moments like this shape what happens next. Scared people who freeze do not."
  • "The version of AI we live with in 10 years depends significantly on choices being made right now — by people in rooms like this, by the representatives they elect, by the companies they support. You now have a map. Use it."
  • Hand out the resource sheet. Point to the companion report for the full research.
  • "I'll take questions now."
End on agency, not uncertainty. Leave people feeling capable, not overwhelmed.

Part 3

Speaker Notes — Stories, Transitions, Key Language

These notes are for delivery, not content. The research is in the slides. These are the "how to say it" notes — the stories that land, the transitions that connect, the exact language for the moments that need to be precise.

Opening — Don't start with what you're going to say

The biggest mistake in talks like this: opening with a definition or an overview. Start with the number. "100 million people in 60 days." Pause. Let people process it. Then: "For context: Instagram took 2.5 years to reach that. The internet took 7 years." Then the kicker: "And that was three and a half years ago."

You're trying to establish a tone immediately: "I'm going to be specific. I'm going to give you numbers and names and dates — not vibes and generalities." That tone needs to be set in the first 90 seconds.

Transition to timeline

"I want to tell you a story. It's about three and a half years and a technology that went from curious novelty to the infrastructure your children will work on. Bear with me for eight minutes — I promise it'll help everything else make more sense."

The DeepSeek moment — make the stakes land

This story needs two beats to work. Beat one: "The assumption for two years was that building frontier AI required a trillion-dollar company, or close to it. You needed a Microsoft or a Google. You needed NVIDIA chips — there's a global shortage of those — stacked in massive data centers. That was the moat."

Beat two: "In January 2025, a Chinese AI company released a model matching OpenAI's best — as open source, free for anyone to download. Built in a fraction of the time and cost. NVIDIA's stock fell 17% in a day. $600 billion in market capitalization — gone in 24 hours. The assumption that only massive capital could do this? It's not true anymore."

What this means for the room

"This matters to you not because you care about NVIDIA's stock price. It matters because it means AI is going to be more available, more competitive, and faster-moving than anyone expected. The pace is not going to slow down."

The hallucination section — make it concrete immediately

Abstract explanations of hallucination don't land. Use the lawyers case: "Two practicing attorneys — experienced lawyers — submitted a brief to federal court citing six previous cases as precedent. The problem: those cases didn't exist. ChatGPT made them up. The lawyers hadn't verified the citations. The cases were fabricated — plausible-sounding, properly formatted, completely nonexistent. The lawyers were sanctioned. This case is now standard training material for bar associations."

Then the flip: "These weren't careless people. They were people who didn't yet understand that AI's fluency is completely independent of its accuracy. The confidence is a style of output. It's not evidence that something is true."

The intern analogy — how to deliver it

Set it up as a question: "Has anyone here ever managed a brilliant intern?" Pause. "The kind who was incredibly smart, would tackle anything, worked insane hours, had read everything — but you still had to check their work, because occasionally they'd be confidently wrong about something important, and they wouldn't always tell you they weren't sure?" Let the room laugh. "That's AI. Exactly that."

The amazing section — pacing and emotional permission

Some audiences will feel it's not legitimate to be excited about AI given the concerning parts. Give them permission explicitly: "I want to make a case that it's possible — and intellectually honest — to be simultaneously concerned about AI's risks and genuinely moved by some of what it's made possible. These aren't contradictory positions. The world is complicated enough to hold both."

The AlphaFold story: "For 50 years, understanding a single protein's 3D structure — the shape that determines what it does in your body — could take a PhD student their entire dissertation. Think about that. Five years of doctoral research to understand one protein. There are 200 million known proteins. In 2024, AI solved this problem. Not for one protein — for all of them. The drug discovery pipeline is fundamentally different now."

The rare disease story — how to deliver it for maximum impact

Slow down here. "A 12-year-old. Three years of unexplained symptoms. Fourteen specialists. No diagnosis." Pause. "His mother entered all his records into a medical AI. The AI flagged a pattern consistent with Mitochondrial Disease — a combination of enzyme markers that no single doctor had synthesized across all his records. Testing confirmed it. Treatment began." Another pause. "The average rare disease diagnostic journey in the US takes 4 to 7 years. AI pattern recognition is beginning to shorten that. Not for everyone. Not reliably yet. But the direction is unmistakable."

The concerning section — acknowledging those affected before pivoting

When you get to the jobs section, watch the room. If you see people tensing, slow down and acknowledge directly: "I know this section is harder to hear for some people than others. If you're in a role that feels exposed — writing, legal research, customer service, data analysis, entry-level coding — I see you. The concern is legitimate and I'm not going to tell you it's not real."

Then: "What I can tell you is that the picture is more complicated than the headlines. The WEF projects 92 million jobs displaced and 170 million created. That's a net gain of 78 million jobs worldwide. The problem isn't the net number — the problem is that the jobs created are different jobs, requiring different skills, often in different places, and the transition is not automatic or painless."

The Arup deepfake case — deliver this as a wake-up

"Here's a story that I want you to remember the next time someone calls you in apparent distress asking for money. A finance professional at a major international firm received a video call. His colleagues were there. His CFO was there. He could see them clearly. They discussed a confidential financial matter. He transferred $25.6 million in 15 transactions. Every single person on that call was an AI-generated deepfake. He had no idea." Pause. "The technology to do this now costs around $60 a month. This is not an isolated case. This is the new baseline."

Closing — the agency frame is everything

The talk lives or dies on whether people leave feeling capable or paralyzed. The closing is the most important moment. You've given them a lot. The final framing needs to do one job: orient them toward action, not toward anxiety.

The language that works: "Every major technology transition has produced a generation of people who chose to engage with it thoughtfully — who understood the risks and worked to shape them, who used the tools while advocating for guardrails, who treated complexity as an invitation to participate rather than a reason to retreat. Those are the people who shaped what the technology became. They didn't have to be billionaires or engineers or politicians. They had to be attentive, and willing to act."

The final line — deliver this slowly

"The version of AI we live with in 10 years depends significantly on choices being made right now — by people in rooms like this, by the representatives they elect, by the companies they choose to support. You now have a map. I'll take your questions."

Part 4

Q&A Preparation — 18 Anticipated Questions

These are the questions that reliably come up in lay audiences across life stages. The answers are calibrated — not dismissive, not alarmist. Evidence-backed where possible, honest about uncertainty where applicable.

Q1 Is AI going to take my job?
Honest answer: depends heavily on your specific job and how you respond. The pattern is that AI is displacing specific tasks within jobs, not entire jobs wholesale — at least initially. The most exposed workers are those doing routine cognitive tasks in large organizations: document processing, basic research, data entry, standard customer service interactions. The most protected are workers whose value is relationship-based, physical and variable, or built on 10+ years of hard-to-replicate judgment. The strategic response: identify the irreplaceable part of your work and invest there, while using AI to be more productive on the routine parts. What you should actually worry about: being the last person in your field who doesn't use AI tools, not being the first person whose job was automated.
If pressed for specifics: "AI Engineer" is the fastest-growing US job title, up 143% year-over-year. The skills creating new roles: AI oversight, judgment on AI outputs, domain expertise + AI fluency combined.
Q2 Should my kid study computer science to stay safe?
Not necessarily, and not only. The better question is: what skills compound over a career regardless of AI? Critical thinking, genuine domain expertise, learning agility, and social intelligence are durable across technological transitions. CS is valuable, but not inherently safer than other rigorous fields — entry-level programming is among the disrupted roles. What matters more than the major: developing the ability to collaborate with AI tools, reason critically about AI outputs, and identify problems worth solving. A nurse with AI health-monitoring fluency is better positioned than a programmer who only writes boilerplate code. The College Scorecard (collegescorecard.ed.gov) lets families compare earnings outcomes by specific program — use it.
Q3 Can I trust AI for medical questions?
As a starting point for understanding — yes, carefully. As a substitute for professional diagnosis — no. The most reliable use: "Explain this medical term my doctor used in plain language" or "What questions should I ask my doctor about this diagnosis?" The least reliable: "Do I have [condition]?" based on symptoms. Hallucination rates on specific medical queries are high; AI can't examine you, doesn't have your full history, and can't account for drug interactions or contraindications only a clinician would know. The best posture: AI as preparation for a medical appointment, not a replacement for one. In regions without healthcare access, the calculus shifts — imperfect AI information may be genuinely better than nothing.
Q4 Will AI become conscious or sentient?
Genuinely unknown, and the question is harder than it sounds. We don't have a scientific consensus on what consciousness is or how to detect it in systems very different from biological brains. Current AI systems don't show evidence of subjective experience in the way the question usually implies — there's no credible evidence they "feel" anything. What they do is generate outputs that sound emotional because they were trained on human-generated text. The more practically important question isn't "will AI become conscious?" but "how do we navigate AI systems whose inner states we fundamentally can't verify?" That's a governance question, not a philosophy question, and it has practical answers.
Q5 Isn't this all just hype? Won't there be another AI winter?
This is the most reasonable skeptical question. Here's why previous AI winters don't apply this time: those cycles were driven by AI systems failing to deliver on specific promised capabilities (understanding language, beating chess, autonomous driving on deadline). Current AI systems demonstrably deliver on general cognitive tasks at professional levels across multiple domains — not just in demos, but in deployed products used by hundreds of millions of people daily. The deployment base is now self-reinforcing: companies using AI to accelerate AI development, which accelerates investment, which accelerates capability. The more apt historical analogy is the early internet circa 1995: significant hype, yes, but also a real infrastructure shift that is now irreversible.
Q6 How do I know if a scam call is really my grandchild or AI?
You often can't, which is why the family code word is critical. The playbook: (1) Establish a code word or phrase with close family members in advance — something not publicly known, that anyone in a real emergency would know. (2) If you receive a distress call from a family member you haven't spoken to in a while, hang up and call them back on a number you already have stored. Don't call the number they called you from. (3) The presence of urgency — "you have to do this right now" — is the primary red flag. Any genuine emergency can wait 3 minutes for you to verify. (4) No emergency requires gift cards, wire transfers, or cryptocurrency. Ever.
Q7 Should I be worried about AI taking over or becoming dangerous?
The honestly calibrated answer: the risks that deserve attention are not primarily science-fiction robot scenarios. The near-term and documented risks are: AI systems amplifying misinformation at scale, AI enabling fraud, AI-driven economic displacement without adequate safety nets, AI-reinforced discrimination in high-stakes decisions (hiring, lending, healthcare), and extreme power concentration in a small number of companies and individuals. The longer-term risks some serious researchers worry about — AI systems developing goals misaligned with human interests — are worth taking seriously but are speculative, not imminent. The most useful risk framing: the danger isn't AI "going rogue." It's AI being used very effectively by humans pursuing narrow interests at the expense of the rest of us.
Q8 What AI tools should I actually start with?
Start with one of the free, major-platform tools: Claude.ai (Anthropic), ChatGPT (OpenAI), or Gemini (Google). All have capable free tiers requiring no credit card. Pick a real task you have this week and try it. Don't start with an abstract "let me explore AI" experiment — start with "I need to write this email I've been avoiding" or "I need to understand this contract clause" or "I need to summarize this 40-page report." Real tasks produce useful signal about what the tools actually do for you. Be skeptical of any tool that requires upfront payment to access, claims special or proprietary capabilities you've never heard about, or promises financial returns.
Q9 What about AI and elections? Should I trust what I see online?
80%+ of countries saw observable AI use in electoral contexts in 2024. AI-generated content and deepfakes appeared in multiple elections. Practical guidance: (1) If a piece of content makes you feel a strong emotional reaction and you want to share it immediately — that's the moment to pause and verify. Emotionally manipulative content is designed to bypass verification instincts. (2) Check claims against multiple reputable sources before sharing. (3) Authentic video of public figures saying surprising things is more likely to be real than fake — "deepfake" has become a reflexive defense. (4) The deeper risk is targeted amplification of real content to specific audiences, not fabricated content — AI makes existing manipulation tactics faster and cheaper.
Q10 Is it ethical to use AI at work without telling anyone?
This depends heavily on context, and the norms are actively forming. Some useful distinctions: using AI to draft something you then substantially edit and verify is similar to using a spell-checker or search engine — generally unproblematic. Using AI to produce work you represent as entirely your own judgment in a context where that judgment is specifically what's being paid for — legal advice, medical diagnosis, financial planning — is problematic. Submitting AI-generated work in academic contexts where original thought is the explicit point violates academic integrity norms. The baseline rule: disclose when you're unsure. If disclosure seems awkward, that's information about whether the use is appropriate.
Q11 How do I talk to my teenager about AI for school?
Start from curiosity, not prohibition. "What AI tools are you using?" and "Show me how" positions you as a curious adult rather than a suspicious gatekeeper. The core educational concern: AI use that bypasses the cognitive work assignments were designed to build doesn't just violate policy — it undermines the student's own development. The useful framing with teens: "If you use AI to write this essay, you know less how to write and think than when you started. Is that actually what you want?" Separately: practical digital literacy conversations matter — how to spot AI-generated misinformation, how deepfakes work, why confident-sounding AI can be wrong. These are important skills for their generation specifically.
Q12 What's the government actually doing about this?
In the EU: the most serious AI regulation in the world. The EU AI Act is in force, with real penalties (up to 7% of global revenue). It bans the most harmful applications and requires documentation and oversight for high-risk ones. In the US: no comprehensive federal law. The Trump administration revoked the Biden AI Executive Order in January 2025. Agency-level regulation (FTC, EEOC, FDA) exists but is piecemeal. Colorado has the most substantive state AI law. Internationally: significant aspirational frameworks, no real enforcement. The gap between EU and US approaches is the most significant regulatory story in tech right now. The good news: the window for influencing US federal AI law is genuinely open — contact your representatives.
Q13 Should I be worried about AI reading my messages and listening to me?
Some warranted concern, some overconcern. Warranted: the apps you've consented to collect data often use that data in ways you didn't fully understand when you clicked "agree." Health apps, smart speakers, and social media collect behavioral data that AI can use to make inferences about you — health, financial stress, political views — that are then used in decisions affecting you. Not warranted: most current AI models don't have persistent access to your microphone in real time without explicit activation. The practical steps: review app permissions regularly (Settings → Privacy on iOS/Android), read what you're consenting to when apps update their terms, be thoughtful about what health and financial apps you give data to.
Q14 I'm close to retirement. Should I be learning AI tools at my age?
Yes, for two reasons that have nothing to do with your career. First: AI tools are genuinely useful for daily life tasks that matter — understanding medical documents, explaining financial statements, learning new things at your own pace, drafting difficult correspondence. The access benefit is real regardless of career stage. Second: the scam protection is critical and requires some AI literacy to navigate well — understanding that voice cloning is real and how it works is protective knowledge. The right entry point: start with one very specific task you've found annoying or difficult. Not "let me explore AI." "Let me understand what my doctor's letter actually means." That's the door in.
Q15 Won't AI create more inequality — rich people getting better AI than poor people?
The pattern is mixed and the direction depends on policy choices being made now. In the near term, AI is actually one of the more democratizing technologies: the best AI tools are free or nearly free to access. The crop disease app in Kenya is free. The legal appeal drafting tool is free. Access to what previously cost expert hourly rates is now open to anyone with internet. The counter-risk: the productivity gains flow primarily to capital owners and highly skilled workers unless policy intervenes. Infrastructure access (reliable broadband, computing devices) remains unequal. And the global digital divide — including the gender gap in device ownership — means AI's democratizing potential doesn't reach everyone equally. This is a real risk requiring specific policy intervention, not an inevitable outcome.
Q16 How do I know which news about AI to trust?
Several signals that reliably indicate quality coverage: Does the article cite specific sources, studies, or named experts — not just "AI experts say"? Does it acknowledge uncertainty where it exists? Does it distinguish between lab demonstrations and deployed products? Does the journalist have a track record covering this beat specifically? Reliable outlets: MIT Technology Review (rigorous, technical-accessible), The Verge, Ars Technica, 404 Media (independent), Wired. Sources to be more cautious about: tech company press releases, outlets with financial interests in AI companies, content that consistently validates whatever you already believe about AI. The rule: be most skeptical of content that confirms your existing view without friction.
Q17 What's your biggest personal worry about AI?
This is an invitation to be a human being rather than a presenter. Answer honestly. One possible honest answer: "The power concentration. A very small number of people and organizations are making decisions with enormous consequences for everyone, with limited accountability to anyone outside their immediate stakeholders. I don't think any of them have bad intentions. But good intentions with concentrated power and limited accountability is a historically recurring recipe for outcomes that harm people who had no say in the matter. That concerns me more than any specific capability."
Adapt to your genuine view. Authenticity here builds more trust than any polished answer.
