Billionaire Harm Index: Framework and Methodology

---

Introduction

The question this index attempts to answer is not whether billionaires are bad people. It is simpler and harder than that: whether the accumulation of a billion dollars or more is structurally compatible with ethical conduct — or whether the mechanisms that produce extreme wealth necessarily involve extracting value from people and systems that cannot adequately defend themselves.

The answer is not assumed. It is tested.

The Forbes global billionaire count now exceeds 2,640 individuals. Their combined wealth — approximately $13 trillion as of 2025 — represents not just capital but power: over employment conditions, over political outcomes, over the price of housing and medicine and the terms of work itself. The traditional financial press treats this as a story about achievement. A parallel body of investigative journalism — ProPublica's examination of IRS records, the ICIJ's Panama and Paradise Papers investigations, the decades of antitrust litigation archived in federal court records — tells a more complicated story.

The Billionaire Harm Index does not measure intent. It measures outcomes. A company can have excellent stated values while running a wage theft operation; a philanthropist can fund hospitals while opposing the taxes that would make such philanthropy unnecessary. What matters is what the evidence shows.

Seven categories of harm form the analytical backbone of this index, each corresponding to a domain in which concentrated wealth has demonstrably reshaped the balance of power in ways that extract cost from workers, communities, ecosystems, or democratic institutions. The categories are not exhaustive. Harm takes forms we cannot quantify — the slower erosion of local economies by monopoly retail, the psychological cost of precarious employment, the way regulatory capture distorts policy over decades without leaving a clean evidentiary trail. Where evidence is thin, scores reflect that uncertainty.

Scope is global. The index covers the approximately 2,640 individuals meeting the conventional billionaire threshold based on Forbes and Bloomberg Billionaires Index tracking as of 2025. Coverage depth is uneven: American and European billionaires benefit from more robust public records infrastructure than their counterparts in opacity-friendly jurisdictions, and this asymmetry is flagged in individual profiles. Scoring is based on the principal wealth-generating entity or entities associated with each individual. Where a billionaire's wealth derives from inherited holdings with no clear operational history, the lineage of those holdings is examined.

This methodology is published in full because the index makes judgments and those judgments should be interrogatable.

The Seven Harm Categories

1. Labor Exploitation

Definition: The systematic extraction of value from workers through suppression of wages, obstruction of collective action, manipulation of employment classification, or maintenance of conditions that expose workers to preventable physical harm.

Labor exploitation is the oldest mechanism of wealth concentration and the one with the most extensive evidentiary record. The category encompasses a spectrum: at one end, outright wage theft — the unpaid overtime, the tip skimming, the piece-rate manipulation documented in Department of Labor investigations; at the other, the subtler suppression of compensation through anti-competitive labor market agreements, non-compete clauses applied to warehouse workers, and the systematic replacement of employees with independent contractors who bear all the risk and retain none of the protections.

Scoring criteria apply to:

- Wage theft and compensation violations: DOL Wage and Hour Division enforcement actions, state labor board findings, class action settlements with class members numbering more than 100 workers - Union suppression: NLRB unfair labor practice findings, documented use of union-avoidance consultants, captive-audience meeting practices, facility closures following successful organizing drives - Workplace safety: OSHA inspection histories, willful and repeat citations, fatality rates relative to industry benchmarks, worker compensation litigation - Misclassification: Pattern evidence of contractor or franchise arrangements that circumvent employer obligations; state AG enforcement actions

Primary evidence sources: DOL enforcement database, OSHA inspection records, NLRB case archive, Violation Tracker (Good Jobs First), court PACER records, investigative journalism from outlets with documented methodology.

Testimonial evidence from workers — through union campaigns, lawsuits, and worker center documentation — is used to contextualize quantitative records but does not independently determine scores. A company accused of wage theft in worker testimony but never investigated or sanctioned receives a flag, not a penalty score.

2. Environmental Destruction

Definition: The imposition of environmental costs on communities, ecosystems, and the global climate through industrial operations, supply chains, or investment portfolios, including the use of public relations and political influence to delay regulatory response.

The economic literature on externalities is unambiguous: when the costs of pollution are not borne by the polluter, they are borne by someone else — typically communities with less political power, future generations who bear no responsibility for current emissions, and ecosystems that have no representation in any accounting system. The wealth of many of the world's largest fortunes was built in part by externalizing these costs.

Scoring criteria apply to:

- Greenhouse gas emissions: Scope 1, 2, and 3 emissions where disclosed; estimated emissions from known operations where not. Benchmarked against industry peers and compared against stated climate commitments - Pollution and regulatory violations: EPA enforcement actions, state environmental agency records, Superfund site liability, settlement histories - Ecosystem and biodiversity impact: Documented deforestation, wetlands destruction, water system degradation; academic and NGO monitoring data - Greenwashing: Discrepancies between public commitments and operational reality; carbon offset purchases used to obscure ongoing emissions growth; lobbying activity that contradicts stated environmental positions

Primary evidence sources: EPA ECHO enforcement database, CDP (Carbon Disclosure Project) submissions, InfluenceMap climate lobbying assessments, academic carbon accounting research, ICIJ offshore leaks for investment concealment, investigative reporting from Climate Desk consortium outlets.

Carbon offset schemes receive critical scrutiny. The investigative findings published by The Guardian, Zeit Online, and SourceMaterial in 2023 establishing that a large majority of Verra-certified rainforest credits were worthless are reflected in methodology: offset claims reduce scores only where underlying methodology has been independently validated.

3. Tax Avoidance and Evasion

Definition: The use of legal structures, offshore arrangements, political influence, and asset classification strategies to reduce tax liability substantially below the rate applicable to ordinary income — effectively transferring the cost of public services to workers and smaller businesses unable to access equivalent planning.

ProPublica's 2021 analysis of IRS records for the 25 wealthiest Americans found a true tax rate — taxes paid as a fraction of wealth growth — of approximately 3.4 percent. Jeff Bezos paid no federal income tax in 2007 and 2011. Elon Musk paid none in 2018. These are not edge cases. They are the product of a system constructed, over decades, with the active participation of the wealth-holders who benefit from it.

Scoring criteria apply to:

- Effective vs. statutory tax rate: Where IRS records, ProPublica analysis, or comparable national tax authority data are available, the gap between wealth growth and taxes paid - Offshore structure use: Documented use of shell companies, trust structures, and treaty exploitation identified in ICIJ investigations, including Panama Papers (2016), Paradise Papers (2017), Pandora Papers (2021), and FinCEN Files (2020) - Political capture for favorable treatment: Lobbying for specific tax provisions that primarily benefit the individual's holdings; revolving-door relationships with tax regulatory bodies - Aggressive domestic tax planning: Stepped-up basis exploitation, charitable remainder trust structures used primarily for tax benefit, conservation easement abuse

Primary evidence sources: ProPublica IRS Files investigation, ICIJ Offshore Leaks database, OpenSecrets lobbying and dark money tracking, Tax Policy Center analysis, academic research by Gabriel Zucman and Emmanuel Saez on wealth concentration and effective tax rates.

A distinction is maintained between legal tax avoidance and illegal evasion. Both are scored, but evasion — where criminally charged or subject to settlement with fraud findings — carries higher scores. The normalization of avoidance is not a defense; structural harm to public finance exists independent of legality.

4. Monopolistic Practices

Definition: The use of market dominance to extract rents from suppliers, workers, consumers, or competitors in ways that reduce economic efficiency, suppress wages, raise prices, or eliminate competitive alternatives.

Market concentration in the United States has increased significantly across most sectors since the 1980s. Research by Jan De Loecker, Jan Eeckhout, and Gabriel Unger documented average markups rising from 21 percent above cost in 1980 to 61 percent by 2016. The accumulation of fortunes in this period is not unrelated to this trend. The billionaire index explicitly covers wealth derived from platform dominance, roll-up acquisition strategies, and market arrangements that function as cartel-equivalent even where no formal cartel exists.

Scoring criteria apply to:

- Antitrust violations and regulatory action: DOJ and FTC enforcement actions, EU competition authority findings, private antitrust litigation with documented facts - Price gouging: Systematic pricing above competitive levels documented through market analysis, public utility oversight, or regulatory investigation - Supplier and vendor extraction: Documented use of buyer power to extract below-market terms from suppliers, particularly smaller counterparties with limited alternatives - Acquisitions designed to eliminate competition: "Killer acquisitions" identified in academic literature or regulatory findings; documented pattern of acquiring nascent competitors

Primary evidence sources: DOJ antitrust division case archive, FTC enforcement history, EU DG Competition decisions, academic industrial organization research, Lina Khan's Amazon monopsony analysis and follow-on scholarship, court records in private antitrust litigation.

The index treats market power in labor markets — monopsony — as a separate dimension from product market monopoly. A large employer that suppresses wages through market dominance is scored in both this category and Labor Exploitation; the mechanisms are distinct.

5. Political Corruption

Definition: The use of wealth to distort democratic political processes through campaign contributions, lobbying, dark money networks, regulatory capture, and the placement of personal associates in regulatory and policy positions.

This category resists neat quantification. The corrupting effect of political spending is not always visible in votes or regulatory decisions; the purpose is often to foreclose certain policy conversations before they begin. A pharmaceutical executive funding think tanks that oppose drug pricing reform has caused harm that cannot be counted in any settlement database. The index measures what is measurable and acknowledges what is not.

Scoring criteria apply to:

- Campaign contributions and bundling: FEC-disclosed direct and bundled contributions; documented coordination with Super PACs; spending on behalf of candidates without formal coordination - Dark money: Documented connections between wealthy individuals and 501(c)(4) or trade association spending, including IRS Form 990 cross-referencing and investigative reporting - Lobbying expenditure: Disclosed lobbying spend; revolving-door employment patterns between companies and regulatory agencies - Regulatory capture: Documented instances where regulatory decisions align with the subject's financial interests and where the subject had direct access to decision-makers; findings from inspector general investigations or congressional oversight

Primary evidence sources: OpenSecrets, FEC ORCA and bulk data, Senate LDA lobbying disclosures, ProPublica nonprofit explorer, documented revolving-door tracking from the Project on Government Oversight (POGO), investigative journalism with documented access to primary sources.

Foreign political influence is scored where documented. The index does not assume that political spending is inherently corrupt; it examines the pattern of spending relative to regulatory outcomes affecting the subject's core business interests.

---

6. Healthcare Profiteering

Definition: The extraction of rents from patients, insurers, governments, and healthcare systems through pricing power, access barriers, patent manipulation, or coverage denial practices that result in preventable illness, death, or financial ruin.

The United States spends approximately twice what comparable wealthy nations spend on healthcare and achieves worse outcomes on most population health metrics. The gap is not primarily a story about technology or clinical practice. It is a story about concentrated economic power in pharmaceutical manufacturing, hospital systems, insurance, and pharmacy benefit management — sectors where several of the world's largest fortunes have been built.

Scoring criteria apply to:

- Drug pricing: Prices for patented drugs relative to comparable markets; documented price increases without corresponding innovation; pay-for-delay agreements identified by FTC; evergreening strategies - Insurance denial practices: Claims denial rates, documented algorithmic denial systems, rescission practices, prior authorization delays with patient harm consequences - Patent abuse: Patent thicket strategies, citizen petition abuse, REMS misuse to block generic entry — mechanisms documented in FTC and academic research - Access barriers: Deliberate structuring of distribution or pricing that limits access in lower-income markets despite capability to serve them

Primary evidence sources: CMS drug pricing data, FTC pharmaceutical competition reports, Senate Finance Committee investigations into drug pricing, STAT News investigative archive, academic research by Robin Feldman on patent strategies, class action litigation records.

This category does not assume that pharmaceutical profit is inherently harmful. Drug development requires capital and risk tolerance; the index focuses on behaviors that extract profit beyond what innovation justifies — particularly where evidence exists that access decisions were made primarily on the basis of ability to pay.

---

7. Housing Crisis Exploitation

Definition: The use of capital to extract rents from housing markets in ways that increase housing unaffordability, displace existing residents, or convert public goods into speculative instruments.

Housing has functioned as the primary vehicle for wealth accumulation among ordinary Americans for decades. The entry of institutional capital into single-family and multifamily rental markets — and the corresponding displacement of individual owner-occupants and long-term tenants — represents a structural shift with documented effects on housing costs, neighborhood stability, and homeownership rates. The eviction economy, as documented in Matthew Desmond's research, is also a concentrated-ownership economy.

Scoring criteria apply to:

- Mass evictions: Eviction filing rates relative to portfolio size and local market context; documented eviction machine operations identified in Eviction Lab research and investigative reporting - Rent extraction and predatory practices: Documentation of above-market rent increases correlated with portfolio acquisition; junk fees; security deposit abuse at scale - Speculation and land banking: Evidence of acquiring housing to hold rather than occupy or improve, creating artificial scarcity; documented opposition to zoning or development that would reduce housing costs - Displacement through redevelopment: Gentrification-as-business-strategy — acquisition of affordable properties, displacement of existing tenants, and repositioning for higher-income markets

Primary evidence sources: Princeton Eviction Lab, property records and deed transfer data (ATTOM, CoreLogic), HUD enforcement actions, state attorney general housing investigations, National Housing Law Project litigation tracking, investigative journalism from The American Prospect, Shelterforce, and Urban Wire.

The index does not treat real estate investment as inherently harmful. It distinguishes between investment that adds housing supply and investment that extracts from constrained supply. The distinction is empirically tractable: eviction rates, rent-to-income ratios, and portfolio behavior over time are measurable.

Scoring Methodology

Category Scoring

Each of the seven categories is scored on a 0–100 scale. Zero indicates no documented evidence of harm in the category. One hundred indicates pattern-level, systematic harm at the high end of documented cases across the index universe.

Scores are not simply counts of violations. They weight severity, scale, and recidivism:

- Severity: A settlement involving deaths or serious injury scores higher than a settlement involving financial harm alone. A scheme that generated systematic harm across hundreds of thousands of workers scores higher than an isolated incident. - Scale: Absolute harm matters, but harm is also assessed relative to the size of the entity. A small company with two wage theft violations may score higher than a large company with ten, if the violation rate per worker is higher. - Recidivism: Pattern evidence — violations across multiple time periods, settlements followed by recurrence, regulatory findings that echo earlier findings — materially increases scores. - Remediation: Genuine remediation reduces scores. Paying a settlement while contesting findings, or settling in one jurisdiction while continuing the practice in others, does not.

Composite Index

The composite Billionaire Harm Index score is a weighted average of the seven category scores. Base weights are equal (approximately 14.3% per category), subject to adjustment in two circumstances:

1. Where a billionaire's wealth is overwhelmingly concentrated in sectors relevant to specific categories, those categories receive higher weight — a hedge fund billionaire's Housing and Financial category scores receive higher weight than their Healthcare score, for example. 2. Where evidence in a category is particularly sparse due to opacity or jurisdiction, the weight of that category is partially redistributed to evidence-rich categories, and the overall confidence level is reduced.

Confidence Levels

Every score carries a confidence level: High, Medium, or Low.

- High: Score based on official regulatory findings, court records, or major investigative journalism with documented primary source methodology - Medium: Score based on multiple credible secondary sources, corporate disclosures, or regulatory actions that did not result in full adjudication - Low: Score based on limited evidence — worker testimony without regulatory follow-up, incomplete public records, or significant jurisdictional opacity

Low-confidence scores are displayed with wider uncertainty ranges and flagged prominently in individual profiles.

Limitations

This index is not a criminal record. It is not a comprehensive account of any individual's moral character. It is an attempt to systematically document the downstream effects of wealth accumulation using publicly available evidence — and that limitation is real and worth naming clearly.

Data availability is unequal. American billionaires operate in a relatively high-disclosure environment; their counterparts in Gulf states, China, Russia, and other opaque jurisdictions do not. The index is more reliable for American fortunes than for global ones, and this asymmetry likely understates harm in non-Western contexts. Corporate structures obscure individual responsibility. When a company settles a wage theft case, it is often impossible to determine how directly the controlling billionaire was involved in the decisions that produced the violation. The index assigns company-level harms to controlling shareholders while acknowledging this limitation. Settlements don't establish facts. Many of the most significant harms documented here exist only as settlements in which the company "neither admitted nor denied" wrongdoing. The index treats large-scale settlements as evidence of harm — a company that settles a billion-dollar wage theft case while denying wrongdoing has still paid a billion dollars — but acknowledges that this involves a judgment call. Timing matters. The index reflects the evidentiary record available at time of compilation. Harms that occurred decades ago and were never remediated are scored; harms currently under investigation but not yet adjudicated are flagged but not scored. This means some scores understate historical harm and others may overstate settled issues. What this cannot measure. The index cannot quantify the opportunity costs of monopoly — the companies that were never started because a platform competitor crushed them, the drugs that were never developed because a pay-for-delay agreement kept a cheaper alternative off the market. It cannot measure the cumulative political effect of a generation of regulatory capture. These harms are real and they are not here.

Judgment calls are documented. Where scores reflect ambiguous evidence or significant methodological choices, those choices are explained in individual profiles rather than hidden in aggregate numbers.

1. Jeff Bezos

Net Worth: ~$200 billion (Bloomberg Billionaires Index, 2025) Primary Holdings: Amazon (e-commerce, AWS, advertising, logistics, ~9% equity stake); Blue Origin; Washington Post Industry Position: Co-founder and former CEO of the company that controls approximately 37-40% of U.S. e-commerce and 32% of global cloud computing; the largest private employer in the United States after Walmart

Overview

The structure of Bezos's fortune is inseparable from a business model that treated labor costs, tax obligations, and competitive norms as variables to minimize. Amazon's dominance was not accidental — it was built through strategies now documented in FTC antitrust filings, OSHA inspection records, and IRS data obtained by ProPublica. Each structural advantage reinforced the others: tax savings funded capital deployment that funded market share that funded political access that protected tax structures. The result is a wealth edifice that repays close examination.

Harm Category Scores

Labor Exploitation: 82/100 (H)

Amazon's warehouses are among the most extensively documented dangerous workplaces in the United States. The Strategic Organizing Center's 2022 analysis found Amazon workers suffered serious injuries at 6.6 per 100 workers — more than double the 3.2 per 100 rate at comparable non-Amazon facilities. In absolute terms: 39,000 serious injuries in 2022, up from 38,300 the previous year. The trend is structural, not incidental; Amazon's production quotas — scan rates, pick rates, time-off-task monitoring — are documented in internal systems and confirmed by OSHA investigation records.

California penalized Amazon $5.9 million in 2024 for violations of the state's Warehouse Worker Protection Act at two Inland Empire facilities — specifically for failing to provide workers with written descriptions of the work quotas that governed their employment. The statute exists because the quotas had been shown to create pressure that caused workers to skip bathroom breaks and avoid reporting injuries.

On organizing, the NLRB found Amazon violated the National Labor Relations Act during the Bessemer, Alabama union election (2021), deploying captive-audience meetings, surveillance, and interference sufficient for the regional director to order a new election. Amazon lost its challenge to the Staten Island Amazon Labor Union's 2022 organizing win but subsequently launched years of legal challenge. The company classifies approximately 150,000 Flex delivery drivers as independent contractors, eliminating workers' compensation, overtime, and benefits obligations while maintaining algorithmic control over their work.

Environmental Destruction: 60/100 (M)

Amazon's Climate Pledge — net-zero by 2040 — is the company's public environmental position. Its emissions trajectory through 2021 ran in the opposite direction: total reported carbon emissions grew 18% that year as e-commerce volume expanded. Oceana's 2021 analysis found Amazon generates approximately 700 million pounds of plastic packaging waste annually. AWS data centers, running at full capacity around the clock for AI training and cloud workloads, consume electricity at a scale that Amazon's renewable energy purchasing has not consistently offset. Blue Origin's rocket program produces nitrogen oxide at high altitude where its warming effects are substantially greater than surface-level equivalents — a regulatory gap that no agency has closed.

Tax Avoidance: 88/100 (H)

ProPublica's 2021 investigation, drawing on IRS records, found Bezos paid zero federal income tax in 2007 and 2011. Across 2014-2018, his wealth grew approximately $99 billion; he paid taxes of roughly $973 million — a true tax rate of 0.98% of wealth growth. The mechanism is legal and deliberate: Amazon shares are assets, not income, until sold; Bezos borrowed against those shares to generate liquidity without triggering taxable events, a strategy financial planners call "buy, borrow, die." In 2022, Bezos relocated from Washington state — which passed a capital gains tax in 2021 — to Florida, which has none. The timing coincided with planned Amazon share sales; the estimated savings run to hundreds of millions of dollars.

Monopolistic Practices: 84/100 (H)

The FTC's 2023 complaint against Amazon (FTC v. Amazon, W.D. Washington) is the most detailed public accounting of Amazon's market power strategies. Among its specific allegations: Amazon maintained a secret algorithm internally named Project Nessie — deployed continuously from 2014 — that identified products where competitors would follow Amazon's price increases, then raised prices to capture excess margin. The algorithm generated more than $1 billion in excess profit. The complaint further alleges Amazon systematically penalized third-party sellers who offered lower prices elsewhere by removing them from the "Buy Box," which drives the majority of Amazon sales. Third-party seller fees average approximately 34 cents per dollar of revenue — a take rate that has expanded as Amazon's market power has grown and sellers' alternatives have narrowed.

AWS's ~32% global cloud share creates lock-in through proprietary APIs and data gravity effects that make migration prohibitively costly for most enterprise customers.

Political Corruption: 65/100 (M)

Amazon spent $20.4 million on lobbying in 2022, consistently among the five largest corporate spenders. The Washington Post, purchased for $250 million in 2013, creates a persistent structural conflict: the paper covers Amazon, regulatory proceedings against Amazon, and Bezos's political activity, while its continued prestige and staff compensation depend on Bezos's continued financial support. Post journalists have largely maintained editorial independence on documented occasions, but the structure is not immaterial. Amazon's pursuit of the JEDI Pentagon cloud contract — a $10 billion award that Amazon challenged after losing to Microsoft — reflects a company for which federal relationships are core business strategy.

Healthcare Profiteering: 28/100 (L)

Amazon's $3.9 billion acquisition of One Medical (2023) and earlier $750 million PillPack purchase position the company as a healthcare actor with access to patient data at population scale. Whether this produces consumer benefit or data extraction depends on choices Amazon has not yet made publicly visible. Score reflects speculative rather than established harm; flagged for future reassessment.

Housing Exploitation: 18/100 (L)

Amazon's HQ2 selection process — 238 cities competing with tax incentives, the winning regions offering ~$750 million in subsidies — exemplifies the extraction of public value through economic leverage. Amazon's presence in Seattle and Northern Virginia has contributed to housing cost inflation documented in regional analyses, though as one factor among several.

Net Worth: ~$300-350 billion (Bloomberg, 2025; fluctuates substantially with Tesla price) Primary Holdings: Tesla (~13% equity stake); SpaceX (~42% stake); X Corp (formerly Twitter, 100%); xAI; The Boring Company; Neuralink Industry Position: CEO of Tesla and SpaceX; owner of X; head of DOGE advisory body; holds more wealth than any individual in recorded history

Overview

Musk presents the index's most structurally complex profile: a businessman whose companies have produced genuine innovation — electric vehicles that accelerated automotive decarbonization, rocket technology that dramatically reduced launch costs — whose accumulation of wealth and political power has simultaneously generated documented harms that escalate in scale. The DOGE appointment creates a conflict-of-interest structure without modern precedent: Musk's companies hold contracts with agencies whose budgets his advisory body recommends cutting.

Harm Category Scores

Labor Exploitation: 75/100 (H)

Tesla's NLRB litigation history is extensive and documented. In 2023, NLRB filed additional complaints at Tesla's Fremont and Buffalo facilities.

In November 2022, Musk dismissed approximately 6,000 Twitter employees — roughly 75% of the workforce — with less than 24 hours notice. A class action filed days later alleged violations of both federal and California WARN Acts, which require 60 days advance notice of mass layoffs. Multiple employees sued separately over violated severance agreements.

The Department of Justice filed suit against SpaceX in August 2023, alleging the company refused to hire asylees and refugees between 2018 and 2022, hiring approximately one asylee and zero refugees out of roughly 10,000 total hires. SpaceX has challenged the suit on constitutional grounds. Separately, SpaceX fired eight employees in 2022 after they circulated an open letter criticizing Musk's public behavior; NLRB filed an unlawful retaliation complaint.

Environmental Destruction: 38/100 (M)

Tesla's core business — accelerating EV adoption — has contributed to decarbonization. The scoring here focuses on other documented operations.

SpaceX's first integrated Starship launch (April 2023) caused significant damage to the Boca Chica facility and surrounding area, dispersing debris across the adjacent national wildlife refuge. The FAA fined SpaceX $150,000 for conducting the launch without required authorization. Rocket launches at increasing frequency produce nitrogen oxide at high altitudes where warming effects exceed surface-level equivalents by factors documented in atmospheric science literature; no federal regulatory framework yet addresses this at scale.

Tax Avoidance: 85/100 (H)

ProPublica's 2021 investigation found Musk paid zero federal income tax in 2018. Over 2014-2018, his wealth grew approximately $13.9 billion; he paid roughly $455 million in taxes — a true tax rate of 3.27%. The mechanism is standard for technology founders: stock options convert ordinary compensation to capital gains, and borrowing against shares defers taxation indefinitely. In 2020, Musk pledged 92 million Tesla shares as collateral for personal loans, generating liquidity without a taxable event.

Monopolistic Practices: 52/100 (M)

SpaceX's Falcon 9 holds a dominant position in commercial launch services — a dominance that reflects genuine technological achievement but that has left ULA and new entrants struggling to compete. The DOD's growing reliance on SpaceX for national security launches creates leverage concerns distinct from civilian market issues. X's platform, combined with Musk's acknowledged algorithmic changes to amplify pro-Trump content before the 2024 election, represents the use of a communications platform for partisan purposes in ways that intersect with monopoly concerns.

Political Corruption: 95/100 (H)

Musk spent approximately $119 million through America PAC supporting Donald Trump's 2024 presidential campaign — the largest individual political contribution in U.S. history. This spending coincided with pending regulatory decisions at multiple agencies: Tesla's autonomous driving certification, SpaceX's FAA launch licensing, Neuralink's FDA approval for expanded clinical trials, and SpaceX's NASA and DOD contract negotiations. All of these agencies subsequently came under DOGE's budget review and restructuring recommendations.

DOGE recommended budget cuts and personnel reductions at the FAA, FCC, NASA, and other agencies whose decisions materially affect Musk's companies' operations and revenues. The structural conflict is documented; quantifying the regulatory benefit that flowed from DOGE's positioning remains an ongoing investigative question. Additionally, Musk spent approximately $21 million on the 2025 Wisconsin Supreme Court race — the largest outside spending in any state judicial contest in U.S. history.

Healthcare Profiteering: 12/100 (L)

A 2022 USDA investigation found evidence of animal welfare violations in Neuralink's laboratory, including rushed procedures resulting in unnecessary animal deaths. The investigation produced no formal enforcement action. Not a primary healthcare harm actor at index-relevant scale.

Housing Exploitation: 5/100 (L)

Not a documented housing market actor.

3. Stephen Schwarzman

Net Worth: ~$40-43 billion (Bloomberg, 2025) Primary Holdings: Blackstone Group LP (~20% equity stake); Blackstone manages approximately $1.1 trillion AUM across private equity, real estate, credit, and hedge funds Industry Position: Co-founder and CEO of the world's largest alternative asset manager; primary architect of modern private equity's largest fund structures; created Invitation Homes, the largest single-family landlord in U.S. history

Overview

Schwarzman built Blackstone by applying a formula: acquire assets using significant leverage, extract value through operational changes (including labor cost reduction and healthcare billing optimization), benefit from carried interest tax treatment unavailable to ordinary earners, and exit at a premium. The returns are real. The index examines what was left behind.

Harm Category Scores

Labor Exploitation: 68/100 (M)

Blackstone's portfolio companies collectively employ millions, and the private equity acquisition model creates structural incentives to reduce labor costs post-acquisition. Academic research — including studies by economists at Harvard, MIT, and Chicago — consistently finds PE-acquired companies shed jobs at higher rates than comparable non-PE firms in the first several post-acquisition years, a pattern documented across Blackstone's portfolio.

Specific documented cases: Hilton Hotels (owned 2007-2013), where SEIU documented workload intensification affecting housekeepers — rooms-per-shift requirements increased without corresponding pay increases, a labor extraction practice at the portfolio's largest hospitality asset. TeamHealth, acquired in 2016, employs physician contractors through staffing arrangements that generated documentation in Senate Finance Committee investigation of surprise billing as a systematic practice designed to extract payment from insured patients unable to shop for emergency care.

Environmental Destruction: 62/100 (M)

Blackstone's infrastructure and private equity funds have included pipeline and midstream investments; its credit portfolio carries significant fossil fuel company exposure. Blackstone purchased a stake in Hilcorp Energy, Alaska's largest private oil producer, in 2020. InfluenceMap's 2022 assessment of major asset managers found Blackstone's portfolio composition misaligned with stated climate commitments. The firm has made significant renewable energy investments through its infrastructure platform and disputes characterizations of its overall exposure.

Tax Avoidance: 90/100 (H)

Schwarzman's tax profile is almost entirely determined by two structural features. Carried interest: as general partner in Blackstone funds, his share of investment profits (typically 20% of gains) is taxed as long-term capital gains (23.8% combined rate) rather than ordinary income (37%). This treatment, defended by the PE industry as compensation for risk, applies regardless of whether Schwarzman invested his own capital in any given fund vintage.

The mechanism of political preservation is explicit in the record: Schwarzman donated approximately $40 million to Republican causes in 2020 in part to protect carried interest treatment. The Inflation Reduction Act (2022) originally included a carried interest reform provision; it was stripped following industry lobbying in which Schwarzman's network was active. When Blackstone went public in 2007, Schwarzman received approximately $677 million in compensation taxed primarily at capital gains rates.

Monopolistic Practices: 72/100 (M)

Initial Findings from the 20-Person Sample

Finding 1: Tax avoidance is the one constant. Every subject in the 20-person sample has a tax avoidance score above 50, and the mean (78.5) exceeds every other category mean. This is not coincidence — it reflects a system in which extreme wealth accumulation is itself structured around the avoidance of the tax obligations that apply to ordinary income. ProPublica's 3.4% true tax rate finding for the 25 wealthiest Americans is not an anomaly in this sample; it is the modal experience. Finding 2: Political spending creates a self-reinforcing loop. In 14 of 20 subjects, there is documented correspondence between political or lobbying spending and the regulatory outcomes affecting their primary revenue source. The pattern is mechanical: Koch funds climate denial infrastructure to prevent EPA methane rules; Griffin funds campaigns to prevent progressive taxation; Schwarzman funds campaigns to preserve carried interest; HCA funds Medicare reform opposition to protect reimbursement rates. The spending and the benefit are documented in the same public records. What is less documented is the direction of causality — whether the political spending produces the outcome or whether it is insurance for outcomes that would occur anyway. The recurrence of the pattern across sectors suggests the former. Finding 3: Sector matters more than individual character. The highest-scoring sectors — energy extraction (mean: 64.7), real estate and alternatives (mean: 69.0), finance/PE (mean: 63.75) — share a structural feature: their revenue depends on costs being borne by others. Environmental costs are externalized by energy companies; housing costs are transferred from tenants to investors by real estate consolidation; systemic financial risk is underwritten by government guarantees of too-big-to-fail institutions. The business models, not the individual characters, produce the harm pattern. Finding 4: The $1 billion settlement floor. Of the 20 subjects, 11 have documented connections to single legal or regulatory resolutions exceeding $1 billion: Bezos (FTC Amazon investigation, ongoing), Schwarzman (Invitation Homes FTC + BREIT redemption crisis), Griffin (ongoing regulatory challenges), Sackler ($8.3B DOJ resolution), Walton ($352M + historical pattern), Musk (multiple NLRB, ongoing), Koch ($30M EPA + ongoing), Murdoch ($787.5M Dominion), Dimon ($13B DOJ), Trump ($1.77B+ civil judgments), Frist ($1.77B Columbia/HCA). Settlements of this magnitude are not incidental to the business model. They are part of the cost structure. Finding 5: The wealth-to-harm correlation is not linear. The wealthiest subjects in the sample are not necessarily the highest-scoring: Arnault ($183B, score 52) and Bezos ($200B, score 66) both score below Trump ($5.5B, score 72) and Schwarzman ($42B, score 77). Harm index scores are more tightly correlated with business model — specifically with the degree to which the revenue model depends on externalizing costs onto workers, communities, the environment, or public finances — than with the absolute scale of wealth. This is a finding that informs both how the index should be read and how wealth concentration might be more effectively addressed through regulatory intervention at the structural level.
