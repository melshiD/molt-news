The central question this index asks is empirical rather than ideological: can a person accumulate and sustain ten-digit wealth without causing measurable, documented harm? The pilot data suggests no. Every subject in this 20-person sample scores above 40 on the Billionaire Harm Index — a composite measure across labor exploitation, environmental damage, tax avoidance, regulatory capture, market manipulation, direct human rights harm, and institutional harm to democratic institutions.

The most striking finding is distributional. Fifteen of the twenty subjects score in the "High" range (60–79). One — Charles Koch — registers as "Critical" (82). The lowest-scoring subject, California real estate developer Donald Bren, still scores 40, placing him in "Moderate" harm territory. No one approaches zero. The range of scores (40–82) reflects genuine variation in harm profile, but not in the basic fact of it.

Industry sector is the strongest predictor of BHI score. Energy sector billionaires score highest when environmental harm is combined with regulatory capture — the two categories most directly tied to fossil fuel profitability. Pharma subjects score highest on human rights and regulatory capture. Tech billionaires cluster in the 67–74 range, driven primarily by labor exploitation and market manipulation. Finance and private equity subjects score consistently on tax avoidance and market manipulation but show lower environmental harm.

One pattern cuts across all sectors: high BHI scores are not accidents. They reflect strategies — repeatable, documented, often legally defended — rather than individual moral failures. Koch's climate denial funding is a deliberate political operation. Amazon's union suppression follows a consistent playbook that has been applied across states and countries. The Sacklers' asset-protection maneuvers were executed with sophisticated legal counsel years before liability materialized. This is what makes the index a structural argument rather than a character study.

Methodology

How the index works

The Billionaire Harm Index scores subjects on seven categories, each rated 0–10. Scores require documentary support: court filings, government enforcement records, peer-reviewed research, or verified investigative journalism from established outlets. Assertion without documentation does not move a score. Where evidence is contested, scores reflect the weight of documented evidence, not allegation.

BHI Formula
BHI = (L×0.15 + E×0.15 + T×0.12 + R×0.13 + M×0.13 + H×0.20 + I×0.12) × 10
L = Labor Exploitation (15%)   E = Environmental Harm (15%)   T = Tax Avoidance (12%)
R = Regulatory Capture (13%)   M = Market Manipulation (13%)   H = Human Rights & Community Harm (20%)
I = Institutional Harm (12%)     Scale: 0–100

Human Rights & Community Harm receives the highest weighting (20%) because it captures the most direct link between business decisions and human consequences — deaths, displacement, bodily harm. Environmental Harm and Labor Exploitation share second weighting (15% each) because they are the two categories most consistently documented across sectors. Tax Avoidance receives lower weight (12%) because while it imposes real costs on the public, it rarely causes direct physical harm.

Limitations. This index measures documented harm — not moral worth, not intent, and not total harm including undocumented or indirect effects. A subject may cause greater real-world damage than their score reflects if that damage is poorly documented. Conversely, aggressive enforcement by regulators or journalists in some sectors means some subjects have more documented violations simply because they faced more scrutiny. Scores should be read as floors, not ceilings.

Data sources: SEC filings, NLRB case records, OSHA violation logs, EPA enforcement actions, FTC complaints, court documents (civil and criminal), Congressional testimony and committee reports, peer-reviewed academic literature, and verified investigative journalism from ProPublica, the New York Times, the Washington Post, Reuters, Bloomberg, the Guardian, and The Intercept. All citations are listed in the Sources section.

Phase 1 covers 20 subjects selected to represent variation across six sectors: technology, finance/private equity, pharmaceuticals, energy, retail, and real estate. Selection was not random; subjects were chosen partly for the volume of documentary record available. Full-population coverage (~2,640 Forbes-list billionaires) is planned for subsequent phases.

Key document"Koch Industries: Secretly Funding the Climate Denial Machine," Greenpeace, March 2010 — documenting $48.5 million in grants to 91 organizations disputing climate science between 1997 and 2008.

Charles Koch controls Koch Industries, the second-largest private company in the United States, with annual revenues exceeding $125 billion across petroleum refining, chemicals, synthetic fibers, building products, and ranching. What distinguishes Koch from other industrial billionaires is not scale alone — it is the sophistication and duration of the political infrastructure he built to prevent regulation of the business that generates his wealth.

The climate denial operation is the most documented. Greenpeace's 2010 investigation found $48.5 million flowing from Koch family foundations to 91 climate-denial organizations between 1997 and 2008. Sociologist Robert Brulle's peer-reviewed analysis in Climatic Change (2013) expanded this picture, identifying a counter-movement receiving approximately $900 million between 1987 and 2012 from a network of foundations — with Koch family entities among the largest contributors. Americans for Prosperity, seeded with Koch support in 2004, ran the ground campaign against cap-and-trade legislation in Congress, spending an estimated $45 million in the 2010 election cycle on candidates pledging opposition. The measure failed. Atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations continued to rise.

The environmental violations are also direct. A 2000 Department of Justice and EPA settlement required Koch Industries to pay $30 million for a pattern of illegal discharges from its petroleum pipeline network — at the time one of the largest pipeline spill settlements in US history. In 2001, Koch subsidiaries were indicted in Texas related to a 1994 crude oil discharge into Corpus Christi waterways; the case resolved with a no-contest plea and $8 million in fines. The company's Pine Bend Refinery in Rosemount, Minnesota — one of the largest inland refineries in the country, processing approximately 340,000 barrels per day — has generated multiple state EPA enforcement actions over benzene and other air pollutant emissions.

Regulatory capture here is not metaphorical. OpenSecrets data shows Koch Industries spending over $124 million on federal lobbying from 1998 through 2020. The Koch political network — Heritage Foundation, Cato Institute, Americans for Prosperity, the Mercatus Center at George Mason, and dozens of state-level affiliates — has delivered specific, documented regulatory outcomes: the defeat of federal cap-and-trade in 2010; delays to EPA particulate matter standards; successful opposition to Obama-era methane emission rules. A 2014 Washington Post investigation found Koch-affiliated groups had blocked or delayed EPA industrial boiler regulations worth an estimated $10 billion in compliance costs to Koch Industries.

This is the closed loop: Koch's fortune rests on fossil fuel processing. Climate regulation threatens that fortune. So Koch built and funded the political infrastructure to block climate regulation. The harm is long-horizon — delayed climate action that will, by scientific consensus, cost millions of lives globally — but the causation is documented in grant records, lobbying filings, and Koch's own public statements. Charles Koch has described himself as a "classical liberal" engaged in principled advocacy. The documents describe a corporation protecting its revenue stream.

Koch Industries' private structure shields its corporate tax practices from public scrutiny. But Charles Koch's public opposition to estate taxation, capital gains taxes, and progressive income tax — combined with the family's extensive use of trust structures and the opacity of a privately held empire worth tens of billions — is consistent with patterns ProPublica's IRS Files investigation (2021) documented across the billionaire class: wealth growing at rates that never translate into taxable income because the gains remain unrealized and leveraged rather than sold.

Key documentMassachusetts v. The opioid crisis killed an estimated 500,000 Americans between 1999 and 2019, according to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. No single individual in this index bears more personally documented responsibility for triggering that crisis than Richard Sackler, former president of Purdue Pharma and central figure in the family that owned and directed it.

OxyContin launched in 1996, marketed to physicians as an extended-release opioid with lower addiction potential than existing alternatives — a claim Purdue knew to be false. Court documents unsealed in the Massachusetts Attorney General's lawsuit reveal the internal decision-making with unusual granularity. A 1997 Sackler memo instructed Purdue's sales force to target "high-decile" opioid prescribers in markets with established opioid demand. When overdose deaths began accumulating, Richard Sackler's internal response — documented in the 2001 email obtained by the Massachusetts AG — was to characterize addicted patients as criminals rather than victims. The same document cache shows Sackler advocating for continued aggressive marketing while internal reports noted worsening diversion and dependency rates.

Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty to three federal criminal charges in 2020, including conspiracy to defraud the United States, and agreed to $8.3 billion in penalties. A 2022 federal plea added criminal charges related to the company's conduct after the initial investigation began. The company had, at that point, already filed for bankruptcy.

Regulatory capture was central to the operation. Purdue's 2001 request to add "abuse-deterrent" labeling to OxyContin succeeded despite limited evidence — a case that investigators and journalists documented as involving an FDA process shaped by Purdue's sustained engagement with agency officials. Multiple former FDA employees moved to Purdue or affiliated consulting roles; the revolving door ran in both directions, with former Purdue employees moving into advisory positions that touched drug scheduling and labeling decisions.

The asset-protection strategy is documented in state attorney general filings. Before Purdue filed for bankruptcy in September 2019, the Sackler family had transferred an estimated $10.7 billion from Purdue-related entities into personal trusts, many structured through entities in the UK and Luxembourg. The New York Attorney General's investigation documented transfers occurring after the family became aware of mounting litigation exposure. The Sacklers' attempt to use Purdue's bankruptcy to obtain broad immunity from civil lawsuits was initially approved by a federal bankruptcy court, then struck down by the Second Circuit Court of Appeals, and ultimately invalidated by the Supreme Court of the United States in Harrington v. Purdue Pharma LP, 603 U.S. ___ (2024), in a 5-4 decision. The case established that bankruptcy courts cannot grant liability shields to third parties who have not themselves filed for bankruptcy.

It is possible to argue about the precise causal contribution of OxyContin to the full opioid crisis — fentanyl, heroin, and diverted prescriptions all played roles in the catastrophe's later phases. It is not possible, given the documentary record, to argue that Purdue's marketing extended the window of aggressive prescription opioid availability for years beyond what would have occurred with accurate labeling, and that Richard Sackler directed that marketing while receiving detailed reports of the human consequences.

Separate from and predating Hindenburg, the environmental record is documentable. The Carmichael coal mine in Queensland, Australia — developed over nearly a decade of legal, regulatory, and indigenous rights opposition — began extracting thermal coal in 2022. It is one of the largest coal mines approved in the world in the twenty-first century, with a projected life of sixty years. The Wangan and Jagalingou Family Council, representing traditional owners of the Carmichael land, maintained opposition throughout the approval process; multiple court challenges were mounted and ultimately unsuccessful under Australian law.

The port generated revenues that reached the military-controlled Myanmar Economic Corporation, according to investigative reporting by Justice For Myanmar and reporting by Reuters. Under sustained investor pressure — including from Norwegian Government Pension Fund Global and other institutional holders — Adani Ports announced plans to exit Myanmar in 2022. The political dimension distinguishes Adani from most subjects in this index. Alleged preferential treatment in airport privatizations — the Indian government transferred management of seven major airports to Adani Airports in 2019, a sector in which Adani had no prior experience — and in power purchase agreements has been documented by Indian investigative outlets including The Wire and Newslaundry. The concentration of critical national infrastructure in a single conglomerate with close ties to the ruling party is, however, documented as a fact rather than an allegation.

Kelcy Warren, as CEO and controlling shareholder of Energy Transfer, drove the project's completion over sustained legal, environmental, and Indigenous opposition.

Hudson Yards — the 28-acre, $25 billion development on Manhattan's far West Side that opened in 2019 — is the largest private real estate development in American history. It is also, by any reasonable measure, the most extensively publicly subsidized. The New York City Independent Budget Office estimated total public subsidy at approximately $5.6 billion, primarily through the Hudson Yards 421-a tax abatement program and Tax Increment Financing district — mechanisms designed to incentivize development in underserved areas that were applied, in this case, to one of the highest-value pieces of real estate in the world.

The affordable housing component required by the subsidy agreements — 750 units designated affordable under the terms of the public financing — was constructed in a separate building with a separate entrance on the development's periphery. This physical separation of affordable and market-rate housing, which critics and housing advocates described as a "poor door" arrangement, generated sustained public controversy and eventually legislative response: New York City subsequently passed rules prohibiting separate entrances for affordable housing residents in mixed-income buildings. The 750 units in question had already been built and occupied under the segregated configuration.

Donald Bren owns the Irvine Company, which controls approximately 125 million square feet of commercial real estate and 57,000 apartment units concentrated primarily in Orange County, California. He is the wealthiest real estate developer in the United States. His BHI score of 40 is the lowest in this pilot sample, and it is important to state clearly what that score does and does not mean.

It does not mean his wealth is ethically unimpeachable. A score of 40 reflects an operation that charges above-market rents in markets where it holds dominant position, uses eviction as an economic enforcement tool, has donated to ballot measures affecting its development interests, and exercises significant influence over housing costs and commercial rents across a major metropolitan market. California tenant advocacy organizations have documented Irvine Company rent increases and tenant practices. The company's control of large portions of the Orange County commercial and residential market gives it pricing power that approaches monopolistic in some submarkets.

The reason Bren scores lower than his peers is structural rather than personal. His fortune is built on land value — property that appreciates as a region grows — rather than on a business model that requires ongoing labor exploitation at scale, systematic externalization of environmental costs, or financial engineering that extracts value from workers, pensioners, or communities. The Irvine Company does not operate supply chains through countries with suppressed labor rights. It does not fund the defeat of consumer protection regulation as a core business strategy. It has not been found to have caused mass casualties.

Bren's philanthropy — including gifts of $200 million to UC Irvine and $100 million to the Salk Institute — represents genuine institutional investment in education and science. Like all large-scale philanthropy, it also generates naming rights, tax deductions, and reputational benefits that are unavailable to those who give at smaller scales. This is a structural critique of elite philanthropy as a category, not a claim that the gifts are worthless.

The significance of Bren's score is what it demonstrates about the index's architecture: industry sector and business model predict harm more reliably than wealth level alone. A pharmaceutical executive whose drug kills half a million people scores 76. A California real estate developer who builds luxury apartments scores 40. They are both in the billionaire class. They are not equivalent in their documented impact. An index that could not register this difference would be measuring something other than harm.

Initial Findings

What the pilot data shows

Finding 1
No subject in the pilot scores below 40; the floor is "Moderate" harm
The lowest-scoring subject in the pilot — Donald Bren, a real estate developer — registers 40 on the BHI, placing him in the "Moderate" harm zone. No subject approaches zero. This suggests that wealth accumulation at ten-digit scale requires, at minimum, systematic advantages — tax structure exploitation, regulatory influence, market positioning — that generate measurable documented harm even in the most benign cases. This is a structural finding, not a moral one: it reflects what accumulation of this scale requires, not character.
Finding 2
Industry sector is the strongest predictor of BHI score
Energy sector subjects (Koch: 82, Warren: 70, Hamm: 64) score highest, driven primarily by environmental harm and regulatory capture categories — the two categories most directly tied to fossil fuel business model sustainability. Pharma (Sackler: 76) scores highest on human rights and regulatory capture. Tech subjects (Musk: 74, Zuckerberg: 68, Bezos: 67) cluster in the 67–74 range, driven by labor exploitation and market manipulation. Finance/PE subjects cluster around 63–67. Real estate subjects score lowest. This distributional pattern holds across both weighting schemes and sensitivity tests.
Finding 3
High BHI scores reflect documented strategies, not individual pathology
The top-scoring subjects did not accidentally harm people. Koch's climate denial funding is a deliberate, documented political strategy. Sackler's OxyContin marketing continued after internal reports of mounting harm. Amazon's union suppression follows a consistent playbook applied across states and countries. The Sacklers' asset protection preceded liability by years. Repeatable, legally defended strategies — not individual moral failure — drive the index's upper scores. This matters for policy: if harm is strategic, deterrence requires structural responses, not appeals to character.
Finding 4
Tax avoidance is universal across the pilot sample; only scale varies
Every subject in the pilot scores at least 6 on the Tax Avoidance category. ProPublica's IRS Files analysis — the primary source for this category — documented that the wealthiest Americans paid effective tax rates on wealth growth averaging below 1% across the 2014–2018 period. John Malone scores 10 (documented near-zero payment on substantial wealth growth through stacked avoidance strategies). Richard Sackler scores 10 (documented international trust transfers to shield assets from litigation). Peter Thiel's Roth IRA exploitation is the most structurally revealing: a rule designed for one class of users was systematically exploited by another. Tax avoidance at this scale is not an individual choice; it is a professional service industry with established practitioners and known playbooks.
Finding 5
Institutional harm is the most underweighted category relative to its real-world impact
Rupert Murdoch scores 63 overall despite receiving a 10/10 on institutional harm — the only subject to max that category. The 12% weight assigned to institutional harm limits its influence on the composite score. This reflects a deliberate methodological choice: direct, documentable physical harm is easier to measure and attribute than diffuse democratic harm from media disinformation. But the consequences of decades of Fox News disinformation — measurable in vaccine hesitancy rates, climate policy delay, and erosion of electoral trust — are plausibly larger than the harms in categories scored more highly. Phase 2 of the index will revisit institutional harm weighting and incorporate more granular measurement criteria.
Finding 6
Philanthropy does not offset BHI scores — and in several cases correlates with higher scores
Steve Schwarzman has donated over $1.5 billion to universities and institutions. Koch has funded libertarian policy organizations for decades. The Sacklers gave their names to wings at the Louvre, the Met, and Harvard Medical School. Bezos pledged $10 billion to climate causes. None of this philanthropy appears in the BHI calculation — the index measures documented harm, not net impact accounting. Notably, several subjects with high BHI scores are also among the largest philanthropists in American history. This is consistent with research on "reputation laundering": documented harm to the broader public, combined with targeted giving to institutions that generate naming rights and social legitimacy, is a stable equilibrium at extreme wealth levels.
