Indiana's Data Center Freight Surge

Over $51 billion in data center construction is reshaping Indiana's rail network. This investigation tracks the steel, concrete, and oversized transformers flowing through Indiana's 8,700+ grade crossings — and examines the invisible public subsidy embedded in every train that makes Hoosiers wait.

Published June 4, 2026
Author Kishbrac
Read time 22 min read
Period covered 2022–2026
Summary

What David noticed, and what it means

David has lived near train tracks in Indiana for most of his life. Over the past two years he began noticing something different: longer trains, more frequent crossings, more varied cargo. His instinct was right. Indiana is in the middle of the largest single capital mobilization in its history — over $51 billion in announced data center investment from Amazon, Meta, Google, Microsoft, CoreWeave, and at least a dozen other operators, all of it requiring extraordinary volumes of construction materials that move primarily by rail.

This report tracks that supply chain from mill to server room. It documents the steel rolling out of Cleveland Cliffs' Burns Harbor plant in Porter County, the generators expanding out of Caterpillar's Lafayette facility, the transformers so large that only specialized rail cars can legally move them. It maps the rail corridors — CSX, Norfolk Southern, Canadian National — that carry this material through the state, and counts the 8,700+ grade crossings where Hoosiers wait, without pay, for the private infrastructure of trillion-dollar companies to pass.

The report also examines the policy structure enabling the boom: a tax exemption regime that has already handed more than $655 million in disclosed breaks to data center operators, a 2026 legislative fight that nearly stripped local communities of zoning authority, and the near-total absence of any mechanism for accounting for the public costs — in time, infrastructure wear, and emergency response risk — that Indiana communities absorb while the trains roll through.

Findings

Seven things the supply chain reveals

Finding 1
Indiana is ground zero for the national data center boom
With $51 billion in announced investment from 2024 through 2026 — Amazon's $26B north Indiana commitment, Meta's $10B LEAP District campus, Google's Fort Wayne and Morgan County builds, Microsoft's two St. Joseph and LaPorte County campuses, and a dozen more — Indiana has become the most aggressively developed data center state in the Midwest. These projects are not evenly distributed: clusters in Lake County, St. Joseph County, Boone County, and Allen County are each drawing billions in infrastructure, construction workers, and raw materials simultaneously.
Finding 2
Indiana already manufactures critical parts of its own data center supply chain
The supply chain is not entirely invisible or distant. Nucor Steel's sheet mill in Crawfordsville has been supplying data center structural steel and recently opened a transmission tower manufacturing plant in Indiana. Cleveland Cliffs' Burns Harbor facility in Porter County — the world's largest wide-plate rolling mill — sits 50 miles from the Hammond and Hobart data center zones. Caterpillar's Lafayette Large Engine Center is undergoing a $725 million expansion specifically driven by data center backup generator demand. Indiana is both a consumer and a producer of the data center supply chain.
Finding 3
CSX's Avon Yard expansion signals the system is already absorbing new freight volume
In January 2026, CSX completed a major upgrade to its Avon Yard classification facility west of Indianapolis — the primary hub for central Indiana freight. The hump lead was extended from 6,400 to 8,000 feet, adding capacity for 200 to 300 additional railcars per day and boosting traffic volume by up to 15%. The timing is not coincidental: Avon Yard is directly positioned to handle materials flowing into the Indianapolis-area construction corridors where Meta's Lebanon campus, Vantage's Pittsboro campus, and a cluster of IEDC-designated projects are all in simultaneous construction phases.
Finding 4
Transformers are the most rail-dependent — and most bottlenecked — component of data center construction
Large power transformers, which can weigh up to 474,500 lbs, cannot legally travel by truck. They require specialized Schnabel rail cars with 12 to 20 axles. Lead times have stretched to 3 to 5 years — up from 30 to 60 weeks pre-pandemic — and prices have risen 60 to 80% since 2020. Every data center campus in Indiana requires multiple of these units. Because they arrive by rail and no Indiana manufacturer produces them at scale (Hitachi, Siemens, Eaton, and Prolec GE plants are in Virginia, North Carolina, Tennessee, and South Carolina), they must cross Indiana's rail network and all its grade crossings to reach their destination.
Finding 5
Indiana's tax exemptions for data centers now exceed $655 million — with decades more to come
Indiana's data center sales and use tax exemption program (IC §6-2.5-15) grants qualifying operators tax-free equipment and energy purchases for 25 to 50 years. Disclosed breaks include $561 million to Amazon alone, $28 million to Digital Crossroads/CoreWeave, and millions more to a growing roster of operators. The June 2025 IEDC board approved $168 million in additional incentives for four anonymous projects. Total Indiana tax breaks for data centers could approach $1 billion, by one analysis — all while operators pay essentially nothing per job created (some IEDC deals cost over $930,000 per job).
Finding 6
Community backlash has produced moratoriums, but no mechanism for accounting for rail freight costs
At least 11 to 13 Indiana counties have passed moratoriums or prohibitions on data center development as of spring 2026, driven by concerns about water use, energy costs, noise, and the loss of agricultural land. A 2026 state bill (HB 1333) would have stripped local zoning authority over data center siting on farmland — its most controversial provisions were killed after public outcry. Yet in all of this debate, no state or local policy addresses the freight externalities: the crossing delays, road wear from construction traffic, and emergency response complications that communities near rail corridors absorb without compensation.
Finding 7
The communities bearing the freight cost are not the same communities getting the tax revenue
Grade crossings affected by increased freight are dispersed across the entire rail network — anywhere on the CSX main line, the Norfolk Southern Premier Corridor, or Canadian National routes through Indiana could see higher train volumes. But data center tax revenues flow almost entirely to the state (via sales tax exemption offsets) and the project's host municipality. A farmer in Tippecanoe County waiting at a CSX crossing as a flatcar carrying Nucor steel for the Meta Lebanon campus rolls past sees none of the tax revenue, absorbs all of the delay, and has no legal recourse.
The Boom

Who is building — and where

To understand why Indiana's rail freight is surging, start with the scale of what is being built. In the span of roughly 30 months — from Amazon's New Carlisle groundbreaking in September 2024 to Meta's February 2026 construction start in Lebanon — Indiana went from a minor data center market to the epicenter of the national AI infrastructure buildout.

The numbers are almost incomprehensible in a state accustomed to thinking about industrial investment in the hundreds of millions. Amazon alone has committed $26 billion: $11 billion for a 1,725 MW campus in New Carlisle (St. Joseph County) and $15 billion for a 2,400 MW campus in Hobart (Lake County). Meta has committed $10.8 billion for two campuses — a 13-building, 1,000 MW complex at the LEAP Innovation District in Lebanon (Boone County), plus an 800 MW facility at River Ridge Commerce Center in Jeffersonville. Google's Fort Wayne campus went operational in December 2025 and is expanding toward 1,600 MW. Microsoft is developing campuses in La Porte and Granger. Vantage, Prologis, CoreWeave, Sentinel, and Takanock are building or planning facilities in Pittsboro, Shelbyville, Hammond, Lowell, and Goshen. The IEDC has quietly approved four more anonymous projects (code-named Maize, Louie, Flo, and Redline) totaling $3.8 billion in additional commitment.

The Supply Chain

What actually moves on those trains

A data center is not a simple building. At its most basic, it is a structure engineered to hold extraordinary weight — 250 to 350 pounds per square foot of live load, compared to 50 to 100 pounds for an office building — and to move vast quantities of electricity and heat. Building one requires categories of materials that strain both quantity and logistics: reinforced concrete poured in depth, structural steel framed to support server row after server row, and electrical infrastructure so heavy and so precisely manufactured that it takes years to acquire and special vehicles to move.

Structural Steel

Steel is the backbone of data center construction. A single large hyperscale facility can require 20,000 tonnes of structural steel — roughly 30 to 40 pounds per square foot. Nucor Corporation, which supplies over 95% of the steel used in data center construction nationally, has described the data center boom as a "tsunami of earnings power" and forecasts data center construction surging 30% year-over-year to 60 million square feet in 2026. Nucor's Crawfordsville facility, one of the original Nucor minimills, sits on the CSX main line between Lafayette and Indianapolis. Steel beams rolling off that mill can be at a data center construction site in central Indiana within hours of loading.

Cleveland Cliffs' Burns Harbor operation in Portage, Porter County, is a more imposing supply node. Burns Harbor contains the world's largest wide-plate rolling mill — 160 inches wide — and produces nearly 5 million tons of raw steel per year, including structural plate, hot-rolled, and galvanized products. Located 50 miles from the Hammond and Hobart data center zones and served by both CSX and Norfolk Southern, it is arguably the single most strategically important manufacturing facility in Indiana's data center supply chain. When Amazon begins in earnest on the 500-acre Hobart campus, Burns Harbor is positioned to supply enormous quantities of structural steel with minimal rail miles.

Concrete

Concrete is the unglamorous foundation of the boom. Data center floors are typically 8 to 12 inches of reinforced concrete — twice or three times a normal commercial slab — because server rack loads can exceed a quarter-ton per square foot. The concrete package for a 200,000 square foot data center alone can run $8 to $15 million. Industry analysts estimate the U.S. will need approximately 1 million metric tons of cement to construct AI data centers by 2028. Most of this material — limestone aggregate, cement — moves by bulk hopper rail car. The aggregate for a large Indiana campus can arrive by the trainload, directly to siding-served batch plants near the construction site.

Transformers: The Rail-Dependent Chokepoint

No component of data center construction is more dependent on rail freight — or more supply-constrained — than large power transformers. These units, which step down utility-grade transmission voltage to levels usable by data center equipment, can weigh up to 474,500 pounds. They cannot be moved by standard truck. The largest units travel by Schnabel rail cars — specialized low-bed platforms with 12 to 20 axles that distribute the load across dozens of wheels — because no bridge on American highways can support that weight legally.

The supply situation is dire. Lead times for large power transformers have stretched to 3 to 5 years, up from 30 to 60 weeks before the pandemic. Prices have risen 60 to 80% since 2020. A WoodMac survey in Q2 2025 found average lead times of 128 weeks for power transformers and 144 weeks for generator step-up units. More than half of data center projects nationally may face delays due to this bottleneck. The manufacturers capable of closing this gap — Hitachi Energy (South Boston, Virginia), Siemens Energy (Charlotte, North Carolina), Eaton (South Carolina), Prolec GE (North Carolina and Louisiana) — are building or expanding U.S. plants, but none is in Indiana. Every transformer destined for an Indiana data center must travel by Schnabel car, crossing hundreds of Indiana grade crossings en route.

Generators: Built in Indiana, Shipped from Indiana

The backup power story is more locally grounded. Caterpillar's Large Engine Center in Lafayette, Indiana, has become one of the single most important facilities in the global data center infrastructure supply chain. The factory manufactures large diesel and natural gas generators — the backup systems that keep data centers running when grid power fails — and is in the middle of a $725 million capital expansion described by Caterpillar as the largest single manufacturing investment in the facility's history since it opened in 1982. A new 300,000 square foot building will expand production and testing capacity for large piston-driven engines. Data center engine production at the Lafayette facility is up 125% from 2023. Caterpillar has also formed a strategic alliance with American Intelligence & Power to deploy 2 gigawatts of dedicated power for hyperscale AI infrastructure. The Lafayette facility sits on CSX's Indianapolis-to-Chicago line — the same corridor serving much of Indiana's data center construction zone.

Rail Network

Three railroads, one bottleneck state

Indiana's 3,650-mile railroad network is served by three Class I railroads: CSX Transportation, Norfolk Southern, and Canadian National. Together they form an infrastructure backbone that was originally built to move coal, steel, automobiles, and grain — and is now, without any formal redesign or planning, absorbing the material requirements of the largest private infrastructure buildout in the state's history.

CSX: The Indianapolis Spine

CSX's primary Indiana line runs from Chicago southwest through Hammond, then southeast through Rensselaer, Lafayette, Crawfordsville, and into Indianapolis, where the Avon Yard classification facility sits west of downtown. From Indianapolis, CSX lines radiate east toward Cincinnati and south toward Louisville. This corridor is arguably the most important single rail freight route for central Indiana's data center boom: it connects Burns Harbor steel to Indianapolis construction, passes within miles of the Lebanon LEAP district, and terminates near multiple southern Indiana projects. The January 2026 Avon Yard expansion — extending the hump lead from 6,400 to 8,000 feet, enabling full-length train processing in a single cut — added 200 to 300 daily railcar capacity and boosted throughput by up to 15%. CSX said the expansion would "reduce railcar dwell time" and handle increased traffic. The expansion was years in planning, but its completion precisely at the peak of Indiana data center construction activity is notable.

Indianapolis also hosts a CSX Intermodal Terminal, and CSX moves over 150,000 railcars annually through the Indianapolis hub alone, supporting Indiana's $9.6 billion logistics industry. In 2022, the Association of American Railroads estimated that Indiana rail freight would have required 16.5 million trucks to replicate — a number that has only grown as data center construction traffic added to existing industrial volumes.

Norfolk Southern: The Northern Corridor

Norfolk Southern's Premier Corridor — a roughly 900-mile double-tracked main line from Chicago to northern New Jersey — runs directly through the heart of Indiana's northern data center cluster. The Premier Corridor passes through Hammond, Gary, Michigan City, LaPorte, South Bend, Elkhart, and Fort Wayne before crossing into Ohio. As many as 230 intermodal, bulk commodity, and general merchandise trains use portions of this corridor daily. NS has described the corridor as "a vital passageway for U.S. steel manufacturers, including mills in northern Indiana, the nation's largest steel-producing region."

The Premier Corridor is, in other words, both the supply line for Burns Harbor steel moving west and the delivery route for transformer and generator loads moving east. It passes within a few miles of Amazon's New Carlisle campus and directly through the corridor serving Microsoft's Granger and La Porte projects. Norfolk Southern also handles dimensional and oversized shipments including generators, transformers, wind turbines, and farm equipment — precisely the cargo types that data center construction generates. NS reported in 2025 that its customers had advanced 70-plus industrial projects representing $7.7 billion in new or expanded rail-served facilities.

Canadian National: The Silent Third Carrier

Canadian National's Indiana routes run through the northern tier of the state, roughly parallel to NS but somewhat south. CN handles significant intermodal and bulk traffic between Chicago and its U.S. network. While CN has a smaller direct footprint in the data center supply chain than CSX or NS, its northern Indiana routes contribute to the overall freight density in a region already absorbing hundreds of millions of dollars in simultaneous construction projects.

The Hoosier Impact

8,700 crossings, and who pays for them

Indiana has more than 8,700 highway-rail grade crossings, according to INDOT testimony to Congress in 2024. That places Indiana fifth among all 50 states in grade crossing count. Two-thirds of those crossings are public at-grade — meaning that when a train passes, motor traffic stops and waits. There are no tolls collected from the railroad. There is no compensation to the drivers. The crossing gates go down, the train rolls, the gates come up, and everyone who waited moves on without any exchange of value. This arrangement was entirely reasonable when crossing wait times were short and trains were carrying coal to power plants. It looks different when trains are carrying structural steel for a $10 billion private data center campus owned by a company with a $1.5 trillion market capitalization.

Indiana does not systematically track or publicly report crossing delay data by corridor or by cause. The Federal Railroad Administration maintains the National Highway-Rail Crossing Inventory (publicly searchable at railroads.dot.gov), which records crossing locations, traffic volumes, and safety events, but does not capture delay minutes per crossing or aggregate them to county or corridor levels. This gap in the public record is itself a policy problem: it makes it nearly impossible to quantify what the freight surge is costing communities, which in turn makes it nearly impossible to argue for compensation.

"A farmer in Tippecanoe County waiting at a CSX crossing as structural steel for Meta's Lebanon campus rolls past sees none of the tax revenue, absorbs all of the delay, and has no legal recourse."

What we can infer from available data: CSX's Avon Yard expansion to handle 200 to 300 more railcars per day means, roughly, 3 to 4 more trains per day moving through central Indiana corridors. Each additional train that crosses a grade crossing creates a closure event. At the low end — a 6,000-foot train traveling 25 miles per hour through an urban crossing — the closure lasts about 90 seconds. At the high end — a newer, longer train moving through a slower zone — closer to 3 minutes. Multiply those additional closures by the number of crossings on a corridor, by the number of vehicles waiting at each, and a rough economic cost emerges. The exact figures require data that Indiana does not publicly collect. That absence of data is, in a sense, the point.

There is also the emergency response dimension, which is harder to quantify but potentially more consequential. Indiana's crossing safety record has been the subject of INDOT attention: the state's Local Trax Rail Overpass Program has funded grade separation projects precisely because at-grade crossings create dangerous conflicts between emergency vehicles and freight trains. More trains, longer trains, and busier corridors mean more risk of an emergency vehicle being delayed at a crossing. In communities near active data center construction — where there are also large construction worker populations and frequent heavy equipment incidents — this is not a theoretical risk.

Community complaints about crossings in Boone County, Hamilton County, and the other primary construction counties have focused more on truck traffic (hundreds of construction vehicles per day) than on rail crossings specifically. This is partly because the LEAP district in Lebanon was designed around highway access to I-65, not rail access — Meta's campus and most central Indiana data center sites do not have on-site rail sidings and rely on trucks for the final delivery leg. But the rail freight burden is distributed upstream: the steel that left Burns Harbor by rail may have been trucked the last 10 miles, but it crossed dozens of grade crossings getting from Porter County to Boone County.

Policy

The legislation enabling — and resisting — the boom

The Tax Exemption Architecture

Indiana's primary tool for attracting data centers is the data center gross retail and use tax exemption, codified in Indiana Code §6-2.5-15. The program grants qualifying operators a full sales and use tax exemption on data center equipment and energy purchases. For investments under $750 million, the exemption runs up to 25 years. For investments over $750 million — which now describes nearly every major project in Indiana — the IEDC can award exemptions of up to 50 years. Local governments may additionally exempt personal property taxes on enterprise IT equipment for centers investing at least $25 million.

In June 2025, the IEDC board approved $168 million in combined tax incentives for four new projects — code-named Maize, Louie, Flo, and Redline — with committed investments totaling $3.8 billion. Each incentive was valued at roughly $42 million over 35 years. The cost per job created in those deals exceeded $930,000 — a figure that drew criticism from Indiana economic development observers.

Investigative reporting by WTHR and analysis by Good Jobs First has documented more than $655 million in disclosed state sales and use tax exemptions to data center operators. Amazon alone has received $561 million in exemptions. Digital Crossroads/CoreWeave received $28.4 million since 2022. Mirror Indy's analysis suggests total Indiana tax breaks for data centers could approach $1 billion when local property tax abatements and unreported 2025 exemptions are included. These are permanent forgone revenues — not temporary incentives — running for decades.

HB 1333: The Zoning Fight

In the 2026 Indiana legislative session, HB 1333 attempted to fundamentally restructure the relationship between data centers and local communities. As originally written, the bill would have classified data center development on "poor" farmland (Class 4-8 soil) as a permitted use, bypassing local rezoning requirements, public hearings, and community input. It would also have required data centers to pay at least 1% of their sales tax savings to local governments — a modest concession that came bundled with a dramatic reduction in community oversight.

The "permitted use" language provoked significant opposition from county governments, residents, and agricultural advocates who argued it would strip communities of the right to decide what gets built next to their homes and farms. By March 2026, the provision was effectively dead. Senate Energy Committee chair Eric Koch (R-Bedford) stated publicly that the permitted-use language would "not move forward." The 1% revenue-sharing component was also not enacted in its original form.

The legislative fight exposed a growing fault line in Indiana politics. On one side: the state economic development establishment and the tech industry, arguing that data center investment creates jobs, tax revenue, and economic diversification. On the other: a geographically and politically diverse coalition of county governments, farmers, and residents who argue that the terms of development — exemptions, zoning overrides, water use, energy cost increases — systematically favor distant corporations over local communities.

The County Moratoriums

The community response has been concrete, if patchwork. By spring 2026, at least 11 to 13 Indiana counties had passed moratoriums or prohibitions on data center development. Marshall County banned data centers outright. Starke County banned data centers over 5,000 square feet for one year. Boone County — home of Meta's $10 billion LEAP campus — was considering its own moratorium as of May 2026; the Area Plan Commission voted on May 6 to recommend one to the County Commissioners. Twenty-seven public interest organizations formally called on Indiana local governments to enact moratoriums.

These moratoriums are significant not primarily as policy mechanisms — most are temporary, and most of the biggest projects already have approvals in place — but as political signals. They demonstrate that Indiana communities are actively weighing whether the economic terms offered by data centers are, in fact, favorable to them. The answer, in an increasing number of counties, is no.

The Core Argument

The invisible subsidy: public infrastructure, private profit

There is a standard accounting of what Indiana gives data centers: the tax exemptions, the road improvements, the utility subsidies, the IEDC incentive packages. Meta received commitments for $60 million in state LEAP infrastructure investment. Amazon received $561 million in sales tax exemptions. Microsoft's Granger campus comes with public road and utility upgrades that Microsoft pays for, but the county committed to accept. These numbers are documented, debated, and increasingly contested.

What is not in that accounting is the rail freight externality. The 3,650 miles of CSX, Norfolk Southern, and Canadian National track that crosses Indiana were built over more than a century, subsidized at various points by federal land grants, public rights of way, and regulatory protections. The 8,700-plus grade crossings that interrupt those tracks are maintained by local governments and INDOT. When data center construction freight — steel beams, transformer Schnabel cars, generator modules — moves through those crossings, it uses infrastructure paid for by Indiana taxpayers. It delays Indiana drivers. It wears Indiana roads. It occupies Indiana emergency response planning capacity.

None of this cost is captured in data center tax incentive calculations. None of it flows into local revenue-sharing formulas. The IEDC's 1% payment proposal — which failed — would have addressed only a fraction of the tax exemption value, and nothing of the freight externality. Indiana has no policy mechanism, no regulatory framework, and no data collection system capable of measuring, much less compensating for, the aggregate time cost imposed on Hoosiers by data center supply chain freight.

The Invisible Math
What no one is counting

Meta's Lebanon campus: 13 buildings, millions of square feet of reinforced concrete, tens of thousands of tonnes of structural steel, dozens of large power transformers each requiring rail transport. Amazon's Hobart campus: 2,400 MW of planned capacity, 500 acres, enough steel and concrete to measure by the trainload. Every car of material that crossed a grade crossing to reach those sites imposed a cost on someone — measured in seconds, minutes, fuel, delayed ambulances — that appears nowhere in Indiana's data center cost-benefit analysis. That's not an oversight. It's a structural feature of how infrastructure externalities work: they fall on people who don't negotiate the contracts.

This is not, to be clear, an argument that data centers are bad for Indiana. The jobs are real. The investment is real. The local vendor relationships — concrete batch plants, construction subcontractors, electrical equipment suppliers — create economic activity that is not trivial. Indiana's steel mills benefit from the demand. Caterpillar's Lafayette expansion creates 100 jobs and likely anchors the facility's long-term survival. These are genuine goods.

The argument is narrower: that the public subsidy flowing to data center operators is larger than the official accounting admits, because it includes the uncounted, uncompensated time of every Hoosier who has waited at a grade crossing while the supply chain for a trillion-dollar corporation's private infrastructure rolled past. That subsidy should be measured. It should be part of the incentive negotiation. And communities along rail corridors — not just the communities hosting the campuses — should have standing to participate in that negotiation.

Looking Ahead

What's coming — and what it means for Hoosier rails

The construction wave has not peaked. Most of the projects announced in 2024 and 2025 are in early construction phases or still in planning. Amazon's 2,400 MW Hobart campus will begin serious site preparation in 2026. Microsoft's Granger campus breaks ground in late 2026. Prologis's Shelbyville campus is projected for 2028 delivery. The anonymous IEDC projects approved in June 2025 have deadlines running through 2029 and 2034. Indiana's peak demand is expected to climb up to 60% by the 2030s, driven primarily by data centers — and every percentage point of new capacity that gets built requires construction materials that move by rail.

The grid expansion required to power these facilities will itself generate substantial freight. MISO is beginning approval processes for 25 new power projects adding 11.4 gigawatts by August 2028. New transmission lines require structural steel towers — a category Nucor now manufactures at its new Crawfordsville facility, which began production in Q1 2026. NIPSCO is building 342 MW of battery storage. AES Indiana and Duke Energy are expanding generation capacity. Utility-scale infrastructure, like data centers, requires transformers, structural steel, and construction materials that travel by rail.

David's observation — more trains, more freight, more time at crossings — will not reverse in the near term. If anything, it will intensify. The question for Indiana policymakers, for communities near rail corridors, and for anyone interested in honest cost-benefit accounting of the data center boom is whether the invisible subsidy will ever be made visible. Whether the Hoosier waiting at the crossing is ever counted in the deal.

Methodology

How this was researched

This report was researched using publicly available primary and secondary sources including press releases, IEDC announcements, local news coverage, state legislative records, federal railroad data, corporate earnings calls and investor materials, and industry analyst reports. Data center project details were cross-referenced across multiple sources including Data Center Dynamics, Data Center Frontier, Cleanview.co, Baxtel, and local Indiana news outlets (IBJ, WTHR, WFYI, Mirror Indy, WNDU).

Rail freight data is drawn from CSX public announcements, Norfolk Southern investor materials, INDOT public testimony, and Association of American Railroads state-level data. Grade crossing data is from INDOT and the Federal Railroad Administration National Highway-Rail Crossing Inventory. Supply chain material quantities are based on published industry standards (Giatec, CEMEX, Construction Dive, Steel Market Update). Tax incentive figures are drawn from IEDC public records, WTHR 13 Investigates reporting, and Good Jobs First analysis.

Key limitation: No publicly available study directly quantifies the increase in Indiana rail freight attributable specifically to data center construction. Crossing delay data is not systematically collected or published at the corridor level in Indiana. Conclusions about the freight-crossing nexus are therefore structural and inferential rather than empirically measured — a gap that itself reflects the absence of policy infrastructure for accounting for this externality.
