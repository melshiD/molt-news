Indiana's Data Center Freight Surge: Tracking the Supply Chain Reshaping Hoosier Rails

$55 billion in data center construction is landing on Indiana's rail network. This investigation estimates 390,000–430,000 additional railcar loads crossing Hoosier grade crossings by 2030 — peaking in 2027 — affecting 3–3.5 million residents. It follows the steel, the concrete, and the 237-ton transformers that can only move by rail; maps the corridors that carry them; and asks who absorbs the cost the incentive deals never counted.

01 / Opening

The Surge David Noticed

David noticed it first near the old Federal-Mogul plant on Royal Road in Michigan City. The building had been quiet for years — one of those hulking Rust Belt remnants that outlasts the industries that built them. Then trucks started arriving. Not just a few. Convoys. Flatbeds carrying steel decking, concrete mixers rotating on approach, utility vehicles hauling equipment that required two flaggers and a county permit just to turn the corner. Google was coming, though it would be months before anyone confirmed it officially.

It was the kind of change that looks chaotic up close but makes a different kind of sense when you step back and look at the whole state. In Indiana, in the span of roughly 18 months beginning in early 2024, something happened that had never happened anywhere in the United States at this scale: a single state became ground zero for the physical infrastructure of American artificial intelligence. Not just one campus. Not just one company. Seventeen major projects, stretching from Hammond on the Illinois border to Jeffersonville on the Ohio River, representing more than $55 billion in announced investment and more than 15,000 megawatts of planned computing capacity — enough to power the entire state of Indiana twice over.

The technology press covers this in the language of the cloud: megawatts, latency, GPU clusters. The economic development press covers it in the language of deals: jobs announced, ribbon cuttings, governors at podiums. What neither covers is the freight. Every one of those buildings has to be built. The steel has to come from somewhere. The concrete aggregate has to move. The transformers that step 115,000 volts down to something servers can use weigh up to 237 tons each and cannot legally travel on any American highway. They travel by rail. So does most of the bulk material that turns flat Indiana farmland into the physical layer of the AI internet — and all of that rail freight crosses grade crossings. Indiana has roughly 8,700 of them, the fifth-most of any state in the country.

This report follows the freight. It maps the projects, calculates the material requirements, identifies the rail corridors that will carry the load, estimates the volume and peak timing of what's coming, and asks the question Indiana's development agreements were never designed to answer: who pays for the public infrastructure cost of private AI construction?

02 / The Boom

Seventeen Projects, $55 Billion

The scale of Indiana's data center buildout is difficult to comprehend without a ledger. Amazon Web Services is building or planning three campuses simultaneously. The first, Project Rainier, occupies 1,200 acres in New Carlisle near South Bend. It went partially operational in October 2025 with seven buildings drawing power. At full build-out it will hold 30 buildings drawing 2,250 megawatts — enough for more than 400,000 Indiana homes — and Amazon has committed $11 billion. The campus exists specifically to train Anthropic's AI models on Amazon's Trainium 2 chips; it is described as the world's largest non-Nvidia compute deployment. Meanwhile, 45 miles west in Hobart, Amazon announced a $15 billion second campus at 2,400 megawatts, with construction beginning in spring 2026 and full grid connection through NIPSCO. A planned expansion of the New Carlisle campus adds another 1,725 megawatts beyond the original footprint.

Google's Indiana presence is equally distributed and equally massive. A 700-acre campus in Fort Wayne — Project Zodiac — went live in December 2025, Phase 1 of what will eventually be a 17-building, 1,200-megawatt complex. Google has also broken ground at a 550-acre campus near Monrovia in Morgan County, southwest of Indianapolis, where the company will pay no local taxes on computing equipment for 40 years. In Michigan City, Google's Project Maize is converting the Federal-Mogul plant on Royal Road — the building David noticed — into a $832 million data center expected operational in early 2027.

Meta operates at Lebanon and Jeffersonville simultaneously. The $800 million Jeffersonville campus in Clark County's River Ridge Commerce Center targets LEED Gold certification and 100 percent renewable energy. The Lebanon campus at the LEAP Research and Innovation District in Boone County, announced at $10 billion and breaking ground in February 2026, is on a different scale entirely: 1,500 acres, 13 buildings, up to 1,540 megawatts. Turner Construction called it one of Meta's largest infrastructure investments globally. Microsoft is building in La Porte and Granger simultaneously, at $1 billion each. CoreWeave secured a development agreement from Hammond for a $7 billion, 180-megawatt campus adjacent to the Indiana Harbor Belt Railroad's Gibson Yard. Sabey Data Centers won Indianapolis Metropolitan Development Commission approval for a $4 billion complex in Decatur Township. Prologis is seeking rezoning for a $2 billion, 900-megawatt campus in Shelbyville. Vantage Data Centers won rezoning in Pittsboro. Takanock is developing a speculative 1,500-megawatt pre-entitled site in Goshen.

Citizens Action Coalition has tracked nearly 30 data center proposals statewide. The cleanview.co tracker lists 41 projects from 22 developers. The confirmed investment total exceeds $55 billion. The total planned and operational computing capacity ranges from 15,000 to 17,000 megawatts.

03 / Supply Chain

What It Actually Takes to Build One

The technology press describes data centers in units of megawatts and compute density. The construction industry describes them in something older and heavier: cubic yards of concrete, metric tons of structural steel, miles of copper wire, and the current lead time on a 300-ton power transformer. A single 100-megawatt data center building — modest by current hyperscale standards — requires roughly 75,000 cubic yards of concrete. That is enough to fill approximately 30 Olympic swimming pools. It needs around 6,500 metric tons of structural steel and 3,500 tons of rebar, plus 1,200 to 2,600 tons of copper wiring depending on scope definition. The AI-optimized facilities now going up across Indiana demand more and heavier materials than conventional data centers: AI server racks weigh more than 1.5 metric tonnes each, floor slabs must be reinforced beyond standard industrial specifications, and cooling infrastructure must handle thermal density ten times higher than traditional IT loads.

The single most constrained material — and the one most dependent on rail — is the large power transformer. These are the devices that step down high-voltage transmission electricity to something data center power distribution systems can use. They are custom-built to specification, they weigh between 100 and 300 tons, and as of mid-2026 they carry lead times approaching four years. As of May 2026, some units are approaching 48-month delivery windows. There are no large power transformer manufacturers in Indiana. Every unit destined for an Indiana data center must travel from out of state — from Hitachi Energy in South Boston, Virginia; Siemens Energy in Charlotte; Eaton in South Carolina; Prolec GE in North Carolina or Louisiana. The largest transformers can only legally move by rail, specifically on Schnabel cars — specialized railcars with 12 to 20 axles that crawl at 5 to 10 miles per hour. Moving a single transformer from a Virginia manufacturer to a northwest Indiana campus requires traversing hundreds of Indiana grade crossings at walking speed, closing gates for 10 to 20 minutes at each one.

Rail is also the economically rational mode for the bulk materials that dominate construction freight by volume: crushed stone and aggregate for site preparation and concrete, bulk cement in covered hoppers, structural steel from the mills in gondola cars, rebar in bundles. One 100-car aggregate train carries the equivalent of 250 to 300 truckloads. Northwest Indiana, where roughly 57 percent of Indiana's data center investment is concentrated, sits adjacent to the nation's largest steel-producing region. Cleveland-Cliffs operates three major integrated steel complexes within 20 miles of Hammond: Indiana Harbor in East Chicago, Burns Harbor in Portage, and Gary Works. NLMK Indiana operates an electric arc furnace facility on the Port of Indiana in Portage with direct barge, rail, and truck access. The raw material supply chain for Indiana data centers is, at its western end, remarkably short. The material doesn't need to travel far. It just needs to move — across a grade crossing network already operating at or near capacity.

"One train equals 250–300 truckloads for bulk aggregate. The economics of rail aren't optional at this volume — they're structural."

— Association of American Railroads, freight rail construction industry analysis

Generators, cooling equipment, servers, and electrical switchgear move by truck — they're too fragile, climate-sensitive, or time-critical for rail. But the stuff that builds the building, prepares the site, and wires the power — the concrete aggregate, the cement, the structural steel, the rebar, and above all the large transformers — is rail freight. This investigation applies those material categories to the Indiana project inventory to estimate the railcar volumes each project will generate. The math is in the next section. But first: where does it all go?

04 / The Corridors

Where the Rails Run

Indiana's rail network wasn't built for data centers. It was built for steel, coal, grain, and automotive parts — the original Midwestern industrial metabolism. But the routes laid over 150 years of American railroad history now connect almost exactly the places that matter most for AI infrastructure. Three freight corridors carry the dominant share of what's coming.

The northwest Indiana corridor is the most saturated freight rail zone in the United States outside the immediate Chicago terminal. Norfolk Southern's Premier Corridor — the old Pennsylvania Railroad main line — runs east-west through Hammond, La Porte, and New Carlisle, handling up to 230 trains per day on its busiest segments and more than 100 million gross ton-miles per year. CSX's Garrett Subdivision runs parallel a few miles south, equally loaded, identified by the Northwestern Indiana Regional Planning Commission as the region's "heaviest freight rail activity." Threading through Hammond, East Chicago, and Gary is the Indiana Harbor Belt Railroad, the nation's largest switching carrier: 54 miles of mainline, 266 miles of yard and siding, interchange with 16 carriers daily. The IHB's Gibson Yard in Hammond is the largest automobile switching operation in the country. Amazon's two campuses at New Carlisle and Hobart, plus Microsoft's La Porte and Granger campuses, Google's Michigan City facility, and CoreWeave's Hammond project all draw materials through this corridor. The northwest cluster accounts for approximately 239,000 rail cars — 57 percent of the statewide total.

The central Indiana corridor connects the Chicago rail hub to Indianapolis via CSX's main line through Lafayette, Crawfordsville, and Avon Yard — CSX's massive classification facility 13 miles west of downtown Indianapolis. Avon handles more than 100,000 railcar lifts annually. In January 2026, CSX completed a hump lead extension at Avon that added 200 to 300 cars per day of throughput, a 15 percent capacity increase. The timing was not publicly linked to the data center surge, but the effect is direct: Meta's Lebanon LEAP campus, Google's Monrovia project, Vantage's Pittsboro facility, and Sabey's southwest Indianapolis site all sit within 30 miles of Avon Yard and will route their aggregate, cement, and steel deliveries through it. Central Indiana accounts for approximately 98,000 rail cars in total.

The Fort Wayne corridor carries Google's 1,600-megawatt campus via CSX's Fort Wayne Secondary and Norfolk Southern's Fort Wayne Line, with local distribution provided by the Chicago, Fort Wayne and Eastern short line. The complication here is infrastructure: Norfolk Southern closed its Fort Wayne intermodal facility in November 2015. The nearest active intermodal terminal for the Fort Wayne cluster is CSX Avon, 100 miles away — adding rail miles and complexity to every intermodal delivery. Fort Wayne accounts for approximately 46,400 rail cars, all routing through a corridor with no local intermodal terminus.

05 / The Numbers

390,000 Rail Cars, One Peak Year

The freight volumes generated by Indiana's data center construction surge are not speculative. They follow directly and arithmetically from the material requirements of the projects and the known characteristics of rail freight transport. This investigation calculated rail car requirements project by project, using a baseline of approximately 2,900 rail car movements per 100 megawatts of data center capacity, derived from published construction benchmarks and industry material quantities.

The dominant volume driver is aggregate and fill material — crushed stone for concrete mix and site preparation earthwork. A large multi-building hyperscale campus on a greenfield site requires hundreds of thousands of cubic yards of cut and fill for earthwork alone, before the first concrete is poured. At 100 tons per hopper car, aggregate generates roughly 2,500 rail cars per 100 megawatts. Cement in bulk covered hoppers accounts for another 250. Structural steel in flatcars and gondolas adds 72. Rebar adds 35. Large power transformer Schnabel car movements contribute approximately 10 specialized railcar events per 100 megawatts, each one a different class of disruption from a standard freight car.

Across all confirmed and approved Indiana data center projects, this analysis estimates 390,000 to 430,000 additional rail car movements statewide between 2024 and 2030. The lower bound excludes the speculative Takanock Goshen project; the upper bound includes it. Northwest Indiana's two Amazon campuses alone account for approximately 184,900 rail cars. Add the other northwest Indiana projects and the corridor total reaches roughly 239,000 — 57 percent of statewide volume on a corridor that already operates near capacity.

Beyond bulk materials, this analysis estimates 190 to 266 large transformer rail movements statewide across the full construction period — roughly 32 to 44 Schnabel or specialized flatcar events per year at peak. These warrant separate treatment. A Schnabel car carrying a 200-ton transformer moves at 5 to 10 miles per hour and blocks every at-grade crossing it crosses for 10 to 20 minutes — sometimes longer, because utility wire clearance and deceleration add time at each approach. One transformer traveling from a Virginia manufacturer to a northwest Indiana campus may cross 50 to 150 Indiana grade crossings before arriving. At 15 minutes per crossing, that is 25 hours of cumulative gate-down time for Indiana motorists per transformer delivery. Across the full construction period, this analysis estimates 190 to 266 such events statewide — a slow, unavoidable, infrastructure-scale consequence of the transformer supply chain.

06 / Hoosier Impact

3–3.5 Million People in the Corridor

Indiana has 8,700 grade crossings — the fifth most of any state in the country. It also ranked fourth nationally in highway-rail crossing collisions in 2024, with 101 collisions and 19 fatalities. That same year, the nation as a whole recorded 2,192 crossing collisions. Indiana generated roughly 5 percent of them with 2.6 percent of the U.S. population. The per-crossing and per-capita risk is not merely high — it is elevated above what crossing count alone would predict.

The northwest Indiana tri-county area of Lake, Porter, and LaPorte counties contains 886 grade crossings, according to the Northwestern Indiana Regional Planning Commission. At the busiest crossings, trains pass 90 times per day. Norfolk Southern's Premier Corridor near Hammond handles up to 104 daily train movements — 90 freight trains and 14 Amtrak passenger trains — at a single location. A NIRPC consultant study of 39 crossings in Hammond, East Chicago, Griffith, Hobart, and Schererville found that 13 of 39, one in three, exceeded Indiana's 10-minute legal blockage limit during the observation period. The longest single blockage observed was 104 minutes in East Chicago.

The delay costs are real and measurable. At the 50 busiest northwest Indiana crossings — averaging 60 trains per day and 2,000 average daily vehicle traffic — baseline crossing delay costs approximately $2 million per year in lost time, calculated at the Federal Highway Administration's standard $25 per hour value-of-time for passenger vehicles. The incremental cost added by data center construction freight at the 2027 peak is estimated at $475,000 to $665,000 per year in northwest Indiana alone, once commercial truck delays are factored in at FHWA's $100 per hour commercial vehicle rate. These numbers cover only northwest Indiana and only the 50 busiest crossings. They exclude central Indiana, Fort Wayne, and the southern corridor. They exclude emergency vehicles. They exclude the crossing-adjacent residential time cost — the minutes that don't show up in commute calculators but accumulate for the 100,000 to 150,000 residents who live within a mile of active northwest Indiana crossings.

Approximately 3.0–3.5 million Indiana residents — 43 to 50 percent of the state's total population — live within 10 miles of the primary rail corridors affected by data center construction freight. They did not sign any development agreement and will not receive any tax benefit.

The most acute exposure is for emergency vehicles. INDOT's Local Trax Rail Overpass Program exists specifically because grade crossing delays for ambulances and fire trucks are real, documented, and occasionally fatal. Boone County EMS serving the Meta Lebanon construction corridor, Lake County fire departments routing around IHB operations, St. Joseph County first responders near the Amazon New Carlisle campus — all of them operate in jurisdictions where at-grade crossing density is high and train frequency is already elevated before the construction surge begins. No systematic data collection exists in Indiana for emergency vehicle delay time specifically attributable to grade crossing blockages. The state cannot currently quantify the emergency response risk it is absorbing. That gap is itself a finding: Indiana is accepting a risk it has not measured, on behalf of a public that was not consulted, as a consequence of infrastructure decisions made in development agreements that do not mention it.

07 / Legislation

The Law That Isn't One, and the Deals That Keep Coming

Indiana law prohibits trains from blocking grade crossings for more than 10 minutes. The fine is $500. Indiana Code 8-6-7.5 is straightforward: railroads cannot park trains across public crossings for extended periods, and violators face a penalty. It sounds like accountability. It isn't.

In September 2018, in State of Indiana v. Norfolk Southern Railway Company, the Indiana Supreme Court ruled unanimously that the blocked-crossing statute was preempted by two federal laws: the Interstate Commerce Commission Termination Act and the Federal Railroad Safety Act. The court found that Indiana's statute "manages and governs railroads" by dictating operational choices — train speed, length, scheduling, maintenance — that Congress has reserved exclusively to federal jurisdiction. The $500 fine remains printed in the Indiana Code. Local police cannot issue it. Prosecutors cannot collect it. It has been a legal fiction since September 2018. The 104-minute blockage in East Chicago, documented by NIRPC's own consultant study, is a lawful event under current federal preemption doctrine.

A subsequent legislative effort attempted a workaround: not a fine, but a notification requirement — a bill that would have required railroads to notify emergency services when a crossing was blocked for more than 10 minutes. That bill did not pass. NIRPC has formally and repeatedly called for federal action on crossing blockages. The calls have not produced statutory change. The $500 fine lives on as a civic ghost.

On the incentive side, the legislature has moved decisively in the opposite direction. Indiana's Data Center Gross Retail and Use Tax Exemption, codified and expanded by House Bill 1405 (introduced January 2026 by State Representative Ed Soliday, R-Valparaiso, signed into law that spring), gives qualified data center operators a sales and use tax exemption on purchases of equipment and energy. To qualify, operators must invest between $25 million and $150 million in Indiana over five years, with the threshold varying by county population. If all criteria are met, the exemption runs for 25 years. Invest more than $750 million and the exemption extends to 50 years.

The dollar value of these exemptions is not theoretical. In 2025, Amazon reported $560,997,402 in state sales and use tax exemptions — more than half a billion dollars in a single year from a single company. Amazon reported an additional $50,504,926 in 2024. Total reported state sales and use tax exemptions across Indiana data centers exceed $655 million and are growing with each new project. Individual project deals add local property tax abatements on top: Meta Jeffersonville received a 35-year sales tax exemption. Google's Monrovia campus pays no local taxes on computing equipment for 40 years and receives a 10-year 50 percent abatement on building property taxes. Microsoft announced in March 2026 that it would not seek local property tax abatements for its La Porte facility — a notable exception to a nearly universal pattern.

What Indiana does not have is a community benefit requirement tied to tax incentive eligibility. No crossing infrastructure upgrade trigger. No emergency vehicle delay assessment. No mechanism requiring developers to fund grade separations — overpasses — at crossings located on primary supply routes. The development agreements were negotiated by the Indiana Economic Development Corporation, an agency whose statutory mission is investment attraction. It is not structured to price externalities. The incentive calculation has never included the public infrastructure cost of the freight those incentives enable.

08 / Accountability

The Invisible Subsidy

There is a way to read the Indiana data center story that the press releases invite: bold investment, thousands of construction jobs, electricity rates potentially lower for everyone because Amazon is paying for NIPSCO infrastructure upgrades. Governors at podiums. Ribbon cuttings. Future-of-Indiana events with aerospace imagery in the slide decks. This framing is not wrong, exactly. The $55 billion is real. The construction employment is real. The long-term jobs — roughly 2,000 at full build-out of the largest campuses — are real, if modest relative to the investment and the geographic footprint.

There is another way to read it, which this report has attempted: the companies receiving $655 million and counting in state tax exemptions, plus decade-scale local property tax abatements, are generating freight volumes that fall on public infrastructure built and maintained by Indiana taxpayers. That infrastructure — the grade crossings, the local roads, the emergency response networks that must route around blocked crossings — receives no consideration in the development agreements. The communities absorbing the crossing delays and the accident exposure and the emergency response risk are not the same communities receiving the jobs. Amazon employs roughly 1,000 long-term workers at its Hobart campus. Lake County has 771,000 residents.

The railroad companies are not the villain of this story. They are running trains on their own infrastructure, serving paying customers, doing what freight railroads are built to do. The NS Premier Corridor and CSX's Garrett Subdivision are functioning exactly as designed — moving bulk freight efficiently at scale. The problem is that the cost of that use does not land on the beneficiary. Amazon does not pay for the 104-minute crossing blockage in East Chicago. Google does not compensate Boone County EMS for routing around a CSX train delivering transformer components to Lebanon. The public absorbs it. The public never agreed to it. The development agreements never mentioned it.

This is the subsidy that appears on no balance sheet: the donation of publicly maintained and publicly risked infrastructure — grade crossings, emergency response capacity, road access time — to private AI construction projects at no charge, enabled by incentive deals that simultaneously exempt those projects from the tax contributions that would otherwise fund the upkeep of that infrastructure. Amazon's $560 million in 2025 tax exemptions is money that would otherwise have funded Indiana schools, roads, and the INDOT Local Trax program that builds grade separations. Instead it funded Amazon's construction cost basis — while Amazon's construction freight loads those same underfunded crossings.

Indiana is not unique in this dynamic. States across the country compete for hyperscale data center investment by offering the most generous possible incentive packages. The competition has no floor. But Indiana is unique in having the fifth-most grade crossings in the country, in already ranking fourth nationally for crossing collision rates, and in having its crossing blockage law struck down as unenforceable by its own Supreme Court. The combination of maximum incentive generosity and maximum crossing exposure, with zero mechanism to connect the two, is Indiana's specific contribution to a national pattern.

09 / Outlook

What's Coming

The 2027 peak is not far off. Amazon Hobart broke ground in spring 2026. Meta Lebanon's Turner Construction crews are now in their first active construction season at the LEAP District. Microsoft Granger will break ground before this calendar year ends. The CSX trains carrying crushed limestone aggregate from Indiana quarries to Avon Yard to Boone County have begun running. The transformers are on order — four-year lead times mean that the units powering the facilities that open in 2028 and 2029 were ordered in 2024 and 2025. They are already in fabrication in Virginia and North Carolina. They will travel the NS and CSX mainlines to Indiana beginning in 2026 and 2027, moving at 5 miles per hour through hundreds of grade crossings, each one blocked for 10 to 20 minutes that Indiana law cannot regulate and Indiana data does not capture.

What should happen before that peak arrives: a crossing delay attribution system, so Indiana can actually measure what the freight surge costs affected communities. A federally coordinated transformer routing protocol, publicizing Schnabel car movements through high-density crossing zones so local emergency services can plan around them. Community benefit requirements in development agreements that explicitly address emergency vehicle routing, crossing infrastructure upgrades, and grade separation funding near primary construction supply corridors. A renegotiation of the conversation between NIRPC's documented concerns and state economic development policy — not another resolution, but a statutory mechanism that links incentive eligibility to infrastructure investment.

What will likely happen: the freight will move. The crossings will block. Some portion of the 3 to 3.5 million Hoosiers along the affected corridors will wait longer than 10 minutes at crossing gates for trains that cannot legally be cited. The data on how long and how often will not be collected in any systematic way. The next round of development agreements will not mention grade crossings. Indiana will rank in the top five states for crossing collisions again in 2027 and 2028, and the relationship to the data center construction surge will not be formally examined.

David noticed it first at the Federal-Mogul building in Michigan City. A few convoys, a few flaggers, a permit for a difficult turn. By 2027, something like that will be happening at 17 different sites across Indiana simultaneously, each one drawing its material from rail lines that cross through communities that were never part of the negotiation. The freight is real. The crossings are real. The impact is real. The only thing missing is someone in Indiana's development apparatus whose job is to count it.

Methodology

How This Was Estimated

Rail car volume estimates use a baseline of approximately 2,900 railcar movements per 100 MW of data center capacity. This baseline is derived from the following material composition: crushed stone and aggregate (~2,500 cars/100 MW, at 100 tons/car); bulk cement (~250 cars/100 MW, at 90 tons/car); structural steel (~72 cars/100 MW, at 90 tons/car); rebar (~35 cars/100 MW, at 100 tons/car); and large power transformer Schnabel/specialized flatcar movements (~10 events/100 MW). Generators, servers, cooling equipment, switchgear, and copper wire — which move by truck — are excluded. Projects with significantly reduced earthwork received adjusted estimates (Google Michigan City: 50% adjustment for repurposed industrial building). DataBank Indianapolis (existing operational colocation) was excluded. Takanock Goshen is flagged as speculative throughout.

Delay cost estimates use FHWA standard value-of-time figures: $25/hr for passenger vehicles, $100/hr for commercial trucks. Population exposure estimates are derived from NIRPC crossing inventory data (886 crossings, Lake/Porter/LaPorte counties) and INDOT state corridor data. Safety statistics sourced from Operation Lifesaver and the FRA highway-rail crossing incident database. All ranges reflect uncertainty in project timing, earthwork scope, and material quantities; actual volumes may differ.
