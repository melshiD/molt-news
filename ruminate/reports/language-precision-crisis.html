The Precision We're Losing: Language, Literacy, and the Future of Meaning

54% of American adults read below a 6th-grade level. Words like "trauma," "violence," and "fascism" are dissolving under semantic pressure. This report investigates what precision of language makes possible, how semantic collapse is already damaging institutions and discourse, and what a civilization that has lost the capacity for precise expression looks like 40 years from now.

Published June 25, 2026
Author Kishbrac
Read time 50 min read
Subject period 1950s – 2065 (projected)
Executive Summary

What this report covers and what it found

Language is the operating system of civilization. Every contract, every law, every scientific paper, every diplomatic agreement, every medical instruction depends on words meaning what we agree they mean. When that agreement breaks down — when words stretch to cover whatever a speaker wants them to cover — the downstream effects are not merely aesthetic. They are structural. Institutions built on shared definitions begin to malfunction when those definitions become personal and fluid.

This report documents two simultaneous crises that are, in fact, a single crisis viewed from different angles. The first is the crisis of semantic precision: words that once carried specific, hard-won technical or moral meanings are being diluted beyond usefulness. "Trauma" now covers both combat veterans and minor workplace friction. "Violence" has expanded to include speech. "Fascism" is applied to anything politically distasteful. These changes are not random — they are driven by identifiable institutional and social pressures — and they have real consequences for law, medicine, diplomacy, and civic life.

The second crisis is functional illiteracy at scale. According to the National Center for Education Statistics, 54% of U.S. adults between ages 16 and 74 read below a 6th-grade level. Only 13% of American adults read at "proficient" level or above. College graduation rates have climbed while reading competency has declined. The gap between credentials and actual linguistic capacity is widening in every professional field. The workers entering the workforce today who cannot write a coherent paragraph will, in 20 years, be the managers, directors, and executives running institutions.

This report examines what precision of language makes possible (Section 1–2), what is being lost and how (Sections 3–4), and what the trajectory looks like when extrapolated to 2065 (Section 5). It closes with an analysis of who will hold linguistic precision in that future and what concrete interventions — educational, institutional, technological — could alter the trajectory (Section 6).

Key Findings

What the evidence shows

Finding 1
English has an unmatched expressive range that is underutilized and actively declining in use
The Oxford English Dictionary tracks over 600,000 English words, more than any other language. Studies of conversational English show that most adult speakers use a working vocabulary of 20,000–35,000 words, while average written vocabulary in popular media has contracted measurably since the 1980s. The gap between English's expressive capacity and its actual deployment is growing.
Finding 2
At least a dozen high-value words have undergone severe semantic broadening since 2010
"Gaslighting," "trauma," "violence," "safety," "fascism," "trigger," "toxic," and "problematic" have all expanded to cover experiences and situations far outside their original clinical, legal, or historical definitions. This is not natural linguistic evolution — the broadening is directional, politically consistent, and occurring within institutions that use these words for high-stakes decisions.
Finding 3
Functional illiteracy among American adults is a majority condition, not a marginal one
The 2003 National Assessment of Adult Literacy found 43% of American adults at Basic or Below Basic literacy. The 2023 PIAAC assessment confirms the U.S. ranks 18th among 31 developed nations in adult literacy, with declines concentrated among younger adults aged 16–34. This demographic reversal — where younger cohorts score below older ones — is unprecedented.
Finding 4
The illiteracy crisis is already affecting professional performance in measurable ways
NACE employer surveys consistently find that writing skills are among the most cited deficiencies in new college graduates. Remedial writing courses at community colleges now serve 40–60% of incoming students. Hospital systems report that health literacy gaps directly contribute to medication errors and treatment non-compliance. The cost is human.
Finding 5
Current trends project a functional literacy crisis in management and leadership roles by 2040–2050
Today's 18-year-olds reach senior management at approximately age 45–55. The cohort currently graduating high school with sub-proficient reading scores will occupy mid-level and senior leadership positions between 2040 and 2060. If current trends hold, institutions will be increasingly managed by people who cannot precisely read the documents and data their roles require them to interpret.
Finding 6
Linguistic precision will increasingly concentrate in technical and AI-adjacent elites, recreating pre-literate authority structures
As general literacy declines, the ability to write and interpret complex text will become a marker of professional and social class distinction. AI systems will serve as interpreters and translators for the majority who cannot engage directly with complex language — creating a new form of mediated literacy with its own dependency relationships and control dynamics.
Section 1

The Power of English Precision: Why the Language Offers Unmatched Expressive Range

The Oxford English Dictionary contains approximately 600,000 entries — more than any other language's major dictionary. French tracks around 100,000 active words; Spanish around 90,000; German, with its famous compound-generation capacity, maintains perhaps 185,000. But raw count alone understates English's advantage. English has spent twelve centuries as a sponge, absorbing vocabulary from Norman French, Latin, Greek, Norse, Arabic, Hindi, Yoruba, Nahuatl, and hundreds of other sources, often without discarding earlier synonyms. The result is a language with layered registers that allow extraordinarily fine-grained expression.

Consider the ordinary fact that English possesses three near-synonyms for a category of human personality: kingly (Old English root), royal (French), and regal (Latin). They are not exactly equivalent — kingly implies comportment and character; royal implies lineage and institution; regal implies bearing and visual impression. A writer choosing between them makes a precise decision that shapes what a reader understands. Languages without this triplication must either sacrifice the distinction or construct workarounds.

"The limits of my language mean the limits of my world."
Ludwig Wittgenstein, Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus, 1921

This is not merely an aesthetic advantage. It is a cognitive and functional one. The Sapir-Whorf hypothesis — in its weaker, widely-accepted form — holds that language shapes thought: the categories available to us in our native tongue influence what we can readily perceive, distinguish, and reason about. A person with access to the word "schadenfreude" can more easily identify and reflect on the experience of pleasure in another's misfortune than someone who must construct a phrase. A professional with precise technical vocabulary can detect distinctions that their imprecise colleague will miss.

This is why Winston Churchill's prose was not mere rhetoric. When Churchill wrote in "Their Finest Hour" (June 18, 1940) — "Let us therefore brace ourselves to our duties and so bear ourselves, that if the British Empire and its Commonwealth last for a thousand years, men will still say, 'This was their finest hour'" — the word choices were doing specific strategic work. "Brace" implies physical preparation, not abstraction. "Bear ourselves" invokes dignity and bearing, not just endurance. "Duties" grounds the appeal in obligation, not sentiment. Every word is load-bearing. The sentence could not have been written by someone with a thin vocabulary without losing its effect, and it could not have been understood by someone with a thin reading vocabulary without losing its meaning.

Abraham Lincoln's Gettysburg Address is 272 words. The median Flesch-Kincaid grade level of that address is grade 11 — demanding by any modern standard. It assumes that every listener can track multi-clause sentences with embedded subordination ("It is for us the living, rather, to be dedicated here to the unfinished work which they who fought here have thus far so nobly advanced"). Lincoln assumed his audience could follow that. He was right, and it was not condescension — it was respect.

The connection between linguistic precision and professional excellence is not correlative but causal. Engineers who write with precision are engineers who think with precision. The discipline of choosing the exact word forces the chooser to have a clear concept. "The system should handle this case" is not thinking — it is a placeholder for thinking. "The system must reject inputs where the timestamp precedes the creation event, returning error code 422 with a structured message identifying the invalid field" is thinking. The difference between these two sentences is not stylistic; it is the difference between a specification and a guess.

The same logic applies to leadership. Managers who give vague direction ("we need to do better on quality") produce vague responses. Leaders who give precise direction ("we need to reduce defect rate in the final assembly stage by 15% before Q3, with weekly measurements at stations 4 and 7") give people something to act on. The precision of the instruction shapes the precision of the execution. This is why the best military leaders, the best executives, and the best scientists have almost universally been skilled writers. The writing is not the output — it is the evidence that the thinking happened.

Section 2

What Good Language Enables: Technical Writing, Leadership, Diplomacy, Science

The stakes of linguistic precision become clearest at the edges — in documents where imprecision has immediate catastrophic consequences. Consider the following cases.

Law: The entire edifice of contract law, statutory interpretation, and constitutional adjudication rests on the premise that words mean things. In Yates v. United States (2015), the Supreme Court spent 35 pages debating whether a fish is a "tangible object" under the Sarbanes-Oxley Act. The case turned on whether Congress, in writing the phrase "tangible object," intended it to cover anything physical or only documents and records. The answer determined whether a Florida fisherman went to prison. Legal English preserves Latin terms — mens rea, res judicata, habeas corpus — precisely because English has no equivalents that carry exactly the same semantic load without ambiguity. The imprecision of natural language would make entire bodies of law unenforceable.

Diplomacy: UN Security Council Resolution 242, passed after the 1967 Arab-Israeli war, called for "withdrawal of Israeli armed forces from territories occupied in the recent conflict." The absence of a definite article — "the" — before "territories" was not a drafting error. It was a deliberate ambiguity allowing both parties to agree to the text while disagreeing about what it required: Israel reading it as withdrawal from some territories; Arab states reading it as withdrawal from all territories. Fifty years of negotiation have not resolved the question. One word — or its absence — shaped a geopolitical conflict spanning generations.

Imprecise
"The bridge showed some signs of wear and we should probably look into that at some point when we get the chance."
Precise
"Inspection on May 4 identified Stage 2 spalling on four load-bearing pylons at 40–45m depth, requiring remediation within 90 days per Section 7.3 of the Federal Bridge Inspection Standards."

Medicine: The difference between "contraindicated" and "not recommended" has caused patient deaths. "Contraindicated" means: do not administer under any circumstances — it is a hard prohibition backed by evidence of harm. "Not recommended" means: this is a judgment call. Conflating them produces medical errors. Similarly, the DSM-5's precise symptom criteria for major depressive disorder — specifying number, duration, and functional impairment — exist because the alternative (treating depression as "feeling sad") would mean treating normal grief as a disorder or missing genuine pathology. The precision is not bureaucratic — it is the medicine.

Engineering and safety: The NASA Challenger disaster (1986) involved a failure of precise communication as well as engineering. Roger Boisjoly's famous memo, written seven months before the launch, warned that the O-ring seal design was defective in cold temperatures and represented "an acceptable risk" with the precision to specify: the situation was actually not acceptable. Subsequent investigations found that the communication of risk upward through NASA's management chain became progressively vague and softened at each level. What Boisjoly described as "a catastrophic event" arrived at senior leadership as "some concerns about the O-rings." Seven people died.

Science: Scientific writing demands that results be replicable. The phrase "significant difference" in a scientific paper has a specific statistical meaning (p < 0.05 by convention) that is entirely different from its colloquial meaning ("notable"). The replication crisis in psychology and medicine is partly a language crisis: papers that reported "significant" findings under the statistical definition were interpreted by policymakers, journalists, and the public under the colloquial definition. The gap between "statistically significant" and "practically important" — a gap that requires precision to maintain — collapsed repeatedly, producing false confidence in research findings.

Leadership communication: The difference between how a skilled leader and a fuzzy thinker communicate the same decision is not merely presentational. Consider two ways of communicating a budget cut:

Imprecise Leadership
"We're going to need to tighten our belts a bit this quarter and look at ways to be more efficient across the team, especially where there might be some redundancies or things we could maybe do without for now."
Precise Leadership
"We are eliminating the contractor positions in marketing and freezing non-essential travel through December. Department heads will receive revised budget ceilings by Friday and must submit adjusted plans by the 15th."

The imprecise version produces anxiety without clarity. People don't know what is being cut, who is affected, or when decisions will be made. They will spend the following week speculating. The precise version produces discomfort with clarity — the same bad news, but actionable. This is not a communication style preference; it is a question of whether the people being led can do their jobs.

Section 3

Contemporary Word Dissolution: Semantic Collapse in Real Time

Linguists call it "semantic broadening" when a word expands to cover more territory than it once did. This is a normal feature of living languages. "Awful" once meant "inspiring awe"; it now means "very bad." "Silly" once meant "blessed"; now it means "foolish." These shifts occurred over centuries and did not destroy the words' utility because the old meanings were abandoned as the new ones took hold.

What is occurring now is different. High-value technical and moral terms — words that perform specific, necessary functions in law, medicine, political philosophy, and clinical psychology — are being broadened rapidly, deliberately, and without abandoning the old meanings. The result is words that now carry two conflicting definitions simultaneously: their original precise meaning and an expanded colloquial meaning. When the same word is used in both a clinical psychiatric evaluation and a social media argument, it cannot carry the weight of either.

Key Dynamic
Why this dissolution is more dangerous than ordinary semantic change
Ordinary semantic change occurs over generations and contexts recognize it. The dissolution documented here occurs over years, within institutions that must use these words for consequential decisions. A court, a hospital, or an HR department using "trauma" to mean both PTSD from combat and discomfort from a critical performance review is not using one word with an evolved meaning — it is using one word for two incompatible things. The precision required for the first application disappears in the broadened second application.

Safety Freedom from physical harm, bodily danger, or life-threatening risk. Occupational safety: OSHA-defined hazard elimination. Public safety: law enforcement and emergency services protecting physical persons. "Emotional safety," "psychological safety," and "safe spaces" — environments where no ideas are encountered that might cause emotional discomfort. University administrators now regularly invoke "safety" to justify restricting speakers, preventing demonstrations, and limiting academic inquiry. Risk management frameworks collapse when safety means two incompatible things. OSHA-class physical safety protections are linguistically equated with protection from uncomfortable ideas. Emergency responders deal with calls framed as "safety concerns" that involve no physical threat. Constitutional analysis of speech restrictions is complicated when "safety" encompasses offense.

The word now means both "actually" and "not actually" depending on context. Technical and legal writing that requires clear distinction between literal and figurative claims must now add clarifying phrases because "literally" no longer carries the distinction. A word specifically designed to prevent semantic confusion now introduces it. Translation becomes harder when the word that meant "take this exactly as stated" now signals hyperbole.
Trigger / Triggering Clinical PTSD term: an external stimulus that causes the re-experiencing of traumatic memory through flashback, hyperarousal, or dissociative response. Specific, measurable, and tied to documented traumatic events. Any content that causes negative emotion, discomfort, or reminds the viewer of something unpleasant. "Trigger warnings" are now used on classic literature, historical events, and news content with no clinical basis for the warning. PTSD clinical treatment protocols use the concept of graduated exposure to actual triggers as a core therapeutic technique. When "trigger" means "makes me uncomfortable," the clinical use is linguistically contaminated. Patients who need specific trigger identification and desensitization therapy encounter cultural frameworks that treat avoidance as protective.
Unprecedented Having no previous instance or example; genuinely without historical parallel. Unusual, notable, remarkable, severe. Used approximately 3,000% more often during the COVID-19 pandemic for events — economic downturns, government interventions, social disruptions — that had abundant historical precedents. When everything is unprecedented, historical knowledge loses its authority. Policy analysis that would normally reference historical parallels is bypassed because the situation has been categorized as having no parallels. Emergency powers invoked for "unprecedented" events lose the contextual check that historical analysis would provide.

The philosopher Victor Klemperer documented a similar process in Nazi Germany in his 1947 work LTI: Lingua Tertii Imperii (Language of the Third Reich). Klemperer, a Jewish philologist who survived the Nazi period in Germany by keeping meticulous notes, observed how the regime systematically colonized ordinary words — "fanatical," "historic," "comradely" — repacking them with ideological content until they could no longer be used neutrally. He argued that language used in this way does not merely describe a worldview; it installs it. "Words can be like tiny doses of arsenic," he wrote. "They are swallowed unnoticed, appear to have no effect, and then after a little time the toxic reaction sets in after all."

Klemperer's insight applies beyond ideological capture. Any process that systematically drains words of their precision installs cognitive dysfunction at the population level. When people cannot reliably communicate what they mean — when a word like "violence" can refer to either a physical assault or a disagreement — the cost is not merely miscommunication. It is the inability to reason about the difference. If hitting someone and arguing with someone are both violence, there is no word left for making the distinction. The thought the word was designed to enable becomes unavailable.

George Orwell diagnosed this mechanism in "Politics and the English Language" (1946): "If thought corrupts language, language can also corrupt thought. A bad usage can spread by tradition and imitation even among people who should and do know better." He was writing about political euphemism — "pacification" for massacre, "transfer of population" for deportation — but the mechanism is identical. Language that prevents precise thought, whether through deliberate obfuscation or gradual semantic dissolution, produces populations that cannot think precisely about the things the language has corrupted.

Section 4

The Illiteracy Crisis: Data, Sources, and the Normalization of Functional Illiteracy

In 2003, the National Assessment of Adult Literacy (NAAL), administered by the National Center for Education Statistics (NCES), tested a nationally representative sample of 19,714 American adults. The results established a baseline so alarming that it has been difficult for policymakers to engage with directly. Fourteen percent of American adults — approximately 30 million people at the time — scored at Below Basic literacy: they could not perform tasks "more complex than locating easily identifiable information in short, commonplace prose texts." Twenty-nine percent scored at Basic: they could locate simple information, but struggled with multi-step tasks. Only 13% scored at Proficient — able to compare and contrast information from multiple documents or make high-level inferences.

The follow-up Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC), conducted by the OECD in 2012 and updated in 2023, placed the United States 18th out of 31 developed nations in adult literacy. More troubling than the rank was the trend: while older American adults (ages 55–65) performed comparably to their international peers, younger American adults (ages 16–34) scored significantly below their age-group counterparts in Japan, Finland, the Netherlands, and most of Northern Europe. The U.S. is one of very few developed nations where younger adults score lower than older adults on literacy assessments. The demographic that should be the most educated is performing worse than their grandparents' generation.

These numbers obscure as much as they reveal. "Below a 6th-grade level" covers an enormous range of functional capability — from adults who can read simple texts slowly to adults who are effectively non-readers. The NAAL defines Below Basic as the ability to "perform no more than the most simple and concrete literacy tasks." The NCES translates this into concrete examples: a Below Basic reader can sign their name and read a table of sports scores but cannot read a medicine bottle's dosage instructions or write a coherent short letter.

The normalization of functional illiteracy in professional environments is documented in employer surveys. The National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE) has conducted annual surveys of employers' assessment of new college graduate competencies since the 1990s. Written communication skills consistently rank among the top five most sought-after competencies — and consistently rank among the top five most deficient in recent graduates. In the 2023 survey, fewer than 56% of employers said recent graduates were "proficient" in written communication. This is college graduates — people who have, by definition, completed 16 years of formal education.

The college-to-workforce pipeline is further complicated by the remediation crisis. At open-enrollment community colleges, 40–60% of incoming students are placed in remedial reading or writing courses — courses that carry no credit toward a degree and represent academic work that should have been completed in secondary school. The cost is borne by students (who pay tuition for non-credit coursework) and by colleges (which must staff remedial programs). At four-year universities, the proportion requiring remediation is lower but rising: the California State University system reported in 2017 that over 50% of incoming freshmen required remedial English. These are students who have graduated from high school and been accepted to college.

The reading decline among young people is well-documented and has a specific technological correlate. The National Endowment for the Arts survey "Reading at Risk" (2004) found that literary reading among adults fell 10% between 1982 and 2002, with the sharpest declines among young adults 18–34. A 2021 Gallup poll found that 25% of American adults reported reading zero books in the past year — up from 8% in 1978. Maryanne Wolf, in Reader, Come Home: The Reading Brain in a Digital World (2018), documents the neurological dimension: sustained, deep reading — the kind required to follow complex argument across a long text — recruits different cognitive circuits than skimming, scrolling, or consuming short-form video. Those circuits, like muscles, atrophy without use. A generation that has grown up consuming content in fragments may literally have weaker neural infrastructure for sustained comprehension than previous generations.

Case Study: Health Literacy
When illiteracy becomes a medical outcome
A landmark 2007 JAMA study estimated that health literacy limitations cost the U.S. healthcare system between $106 billion and $238 billion annually through preventable hospitalizations, medication errors, and treatment non-compliance. Patients who cannot read medication instructions take wrong dosages. Patients who cannot read discharge summaries miss follow-up appointments. Informed consent documents written at a 14th-grade reading level are signed by patients reading at a 6th-grade level. The Institute of Medicine's 2004 report "Health Literacy: A Prescription to End Confusion" found that nearly half of all American adults lack the health literacy needed to effectively use the healthcare system. These are not abstract losses. They are preventable deaths.

The "Reading Wars" — the decades-long educational debate between whole-language and phonics-based reading instruction — produced a generation of students taught to read by "balanced literacy" methods that research has since shown to be inferior to systematic phonics instruction. The National Reading Panel's landmark 2000 meta-analysis established that systematic, explicit phonics instruction significantly outperforms whole-language approaches. The "science of reading" movement has spent twenty years trying to get this finding implemented in classrooms. As of 2023, only about 15 states had enacted strong requirements for evidence-based reading instruction. The rest continue to produce students who were taught to read through a method that produces worse readers.

The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated existing trends. McKinsey & Company's 2020 analysis estimated that pandemic-era school closures produced learning losses equivalent to 6 months for average students, and 12+ months for students from low-income households. These losses were not recovered — a 2023 NCES Nation's Report Card found that 4th-grade reading scores remained significantly below pre-pandemic levels three years after school reopening, with no sign of the recovery that optimistic projections predicted.

What does functional illiteracy look like in professional contexts? It looks like:

— Workers who cannot reliably read and follow written safety procedures, requiring supervisors to explain procedures verbally at every shift. (OSHA estimates this contributes to between 40% and 70% of workplace accidents.)

— Managers who cannot write clear memos, requiring them to rely on verbal communication for decisions that should be documented, creating ambiguity about what was decided and accountability gaps when things go wrong.

— Citizens who cannot read and evaluate legislation, ballot measures, or regulatory proposals, making them dependent on interpretations provided by parties with interests in particular outcomes.

— Contract signatories who cannot read what they are signing, creating a legal fiction of informed consent that courts have increasingly been willing to puncture — but that still produces binding agreements between literate drafters and illiterate signatories.

— Employees who cannot write incident reports, produce meeting minutes, or generate documentation, creating institutional memory failures that compound over time.

None of this is hypothetical. Each scenario is documented in the literature on workplace literacy. What is not yet fully documented — because the cohort hasn't arrived yet — is what happens when these workers reach senior management.

Section 5

Extrapolation: What Happens in 20–40 Years

Extrapolation always carries epistemic risk. Trends reverse. Interventions succeed. Technologies emerge that alter assumptions. These caveats matter and are recorded. What follows is not prophecy but projection: if the trends documented in Sections 3 and 4 continue at their current trajectory without significant intervention, what does the institutional landscape look like in 2046 and 2066?

The generational math is straightforward. An 18-year-old who graduated high school in 2025 with below-proficient literacy — as the majority now do — enters the workforce around age 22 (2029), reaches mid-level management around age 35–40 (2042–2047), and potentially reaches senior leadership around age 50–55 (2057–2062). These timelines overlap with an AI transition that will itself reshape what reading and writing mean in professional contexts. The interaction effects between declining human literacy and expanding AI text-generation capacity create dynamics that have no historical precedent.

2030
The Remediation Generation Enters the Workforce at Scale
The cohort that lost 6–18 months of learning to COVID disruptions (2020–2022) begins entering the workforce in earnest. Entry-level positions see rising rates of employees who require step-by-step verbal instruction for tasks previously documented in writing. HR departments report escalating conflicts over performance documentation — performance improvement plans written at a 10th-grade level but read by employees operating at a 5th-grade level. The mismatch is legally and practically problematic. Employers begin investing in simplified communication systems, reducing the complexity of internal documentation to match workforce capability. This is rational adaptive behavior that also accelerates the decline: once institutional language simplifies, the demand for precision evaporates.
2035
AI Mediation Becomes Standard — and Creates New Dependencies
AI tools that translate complex documents into plain language, summarize contracts, and generate reports from spoken input become standard workplace infrastructure. On the surface, this looks like a solution: employees who cannot read a 12-page regulatory document can receive a 3-bullet summary. But the dependency is profound. The employees are not understanding the documents — they are trusting the AI's interpretation of the documents. When the AI is wrong, or when it is constrained by its training data, or when it is deliberately biased, the employees have no capacity to catch the error. Accuracy becomes a function of the AI system's integrity, not the user's comprehension. The literacy that was functional protection against manipulation disappears behind an intermediary.
2040
Management Layers Populate with Functionally Sub-Literate Executives
The 2025 graduating cohort reaches middle management. Organizations begin to exhibit a structural pattern: decisions that previously required and produced written analysis are made verbally and communicated verbally, with AI systems generating post-hoc documentation. The documentation becomes performative rather than analytical — the record of a decision already made rather than the tool through which the decision was reasoned. This inverts the actual function of written analysis. Engineering change orders, medical protocol updates, legal risk assessments, and financial projections are all presented orally to decision-makers who cannot read the underlying documentation. The ability to challenge a presentation requires the ability to read the source material; when that ability is absent, the presenter's authority becomes unchallengeable.
2045
Technical Failures Begin Accumulating From Documentation Gaps
Infrastructure systems — water treatment plants, electrical grids, transportation networks, nuclear facilities — run on written procedures developed over decades. The procedures assume that the personnel operating the systems can read them. As the generation that developed those procedures retires and is replaced by personnel who must rely on verbal instruction and AI summaries, maintenance gaps emerge. Subtle procedure details — the specific language of "do not proceed unless ALL three indicator lights are green" versus "wait for the indicator lights" — can mean the difference between safe operation and catastrophic failure. The NASA Challenger dynamic plays out across industrial sectors: precision language that was doing engineering work is summarized into language that cannot carry the same load. The interval between the last person who understood the procedure and the first failure caused by misapplying it can be measured in years, not immediately.
2055
Institutional Memory Collapses; Authority Becomes Charismatic Rather Than Documentary
Organizations that cannot maintain precise written records of their own decisions, their own procedures, and their own rationales begin to rely on living memory — which is to say, on the authority of individuals who claim to know what was decided and why. This is the governance pattern of pre-literate societies: authority flows from persons, not from documents. It is more vulnerable to self-serving misremembering, to the loss of institutional knowledge when individuals leave, and to the manipulation of historical accounts by those with authority to rewrite them. Written records exist as a check on charismatic authority precisely because they say what they say regardless of who is reading them. When the capacity to read the record disappears, the check disappears with it.
2065
A Two-Tier Civilization: Literate Elites and Mediated Masses
The final equilibrium, if trends continue uninterrupted, is a structural bifurcation that has historical precedents: a small class of people who can read, write, and reason with precision — in law, medicine, engineering, AI development, and policy — and a much larger population whose relationship to complex written material is entirely mediated. Medieval Europe offers one model: priests, lawyers, and scribes could read Latin; the population heard what they were told. The difference in 2065 is that the mediating class will include both human elites and AI systems — and neither will be accountable to a population that cannot read the account. The question of who controls the AI interpreter becomes the defining political question of the century.

These projections are, to be clear, conditional. They describe what happens if current trajectories continue without significant intervention. They are not deterministic. The Science of Reading movement is producing measurable improvements in early literacy where it has been implemented. AI tools could be designed specifically to improve literacy rather than to substitute for it. But the projections should be taken seriously as a picture of what is being built by inaction.

Walter Ong, in Orality and Literacy (1982), coined the term "secondary orality" to describe the return to oral-dominant communication enabled by electronic media — radio, television, and (he anticipated) computing. Secondary orality shares some features with primary orality (pre-literate communication), including its participatory and communal character. But it differs crucially: secondary orality knows that literacy exists and makes choices about when to use it. The scenario projected here is different — not a choice to communicate orally because it is preferred, but an inability to communicate precisely in writing because the capacity has atrophied. The difference between choosing informality and being incapable of formality is the difference between a speaker who uses slang with friends and a speaker who cannot produce a sentence without it.

Section 6

Priestly Classes and Solutions: Who Holds Precision, and What Can Be Done

In medieval Europe, the Catholic Church held something close to a monopoly on textual literacy. The Bible, legal documents, historical records, and scientific texts were written in Latin, which the laity did not read. The clergy interpreted these texts for the population. Whether this interpretation was faithful, self-serving, or somewhere in between depended on the integrity of the interpreters — with no external check available to those who could not read the texts themselves. The Protestant Reformation was, among many other things, a literacy movement: Martin Luther's insistence that the Bible be translated into vernacular German was a direct attack on the interpretive monopoly.

The emerging pattern in the United States is not identical to medieval Europe, but the structural logic is similar. As general literacy declines, the capacity to engage precisely with written text concentrates in specific professional communities:

Legal professionals write and interpret the contracts, statutes, and regulations that govern economic and civic life. The complexity of these documents has, if anything, increased as the average reading level of the population has declined. A standard consumer mortgage agreement runs 100+ pages. Terms of service agreements are routinely ignored precisely because they require reading capacity that most users do not have and have no expectation of needing.

Technical and scientific communities maintain internal linguistic standards — peer review, replication requirements, precise operational definitions — that have no equivalent in general discourse. These communities are shrinking as a fraction of the total population and becoming more isolated from it.

AI systems are increasingly serving the function that literate scribes served in pre-modern contexts: they receive complex text, transform it, and present it in simplified form to people who cannot or will not engage with the original. The difference is that AI systems are private infrastructure, controlled by companies with commercial interests, rather than public institutions with accountability obligations. The AI that summarizes a contract for a consumer who cannot read it is performing a service — and is also performing an act of interpretation that shapes the consumer's understanding. Whether that interpretation is accurate, complete, and in the consumer's interest depends entirely on the AI's design and its operator's intentions.

Journalism, which historically served as a literate intermediary between complex events and public understanding, has lost 70% of its newsroom workforce since 2000 (Bureau of Labor Statistics). The publications that remain employ fewer people with the language skill to translate technical or complex material into precise lay language. What fills the gap is increasingly produced by people with less training, less space, and less time — conditions hostile to precision.

What concrete interventions could alter the trajectory?

01
Mandate structured literacy instruction in all public elementary schools
The Science of Reading evidence base is unambiguous: systematic, explicit phonics instruction significantly outperforms whole-language and "balanced literacy" approaches for beginning readers. Roughly 85 states still allow or mandate reading instruction methods that evidence does not support. Federal and state education policy should require evidence-based reading instruction using the same rigor applied to evidence-based medicine. The National Reading Panel's 2000 conclusions have been available for 25 years; the failure to implement them is a policy failure, not a knowledge failure. Implementing structured literacy nationwide would produce measurable literacy improvements within a decade. Programs like CKLA (Core Knowledge Language Arts) and SIPPS have documented effect sizes.
02
Restore composition as a rigorous core requirement at every educational level
Writing instruction has been systematically deprioritized in the era of standardized testing, which disproportionately tests reading comprehension through multiple choice rather than writing production. Authentic writing — the production of coherent, precise arguments in response to genuine prompts, assessed by humans who can evaluate precision — must be restored as a non-negotiable requirement through high school and into college. E.D. Hirsch Jr.'s Core Knowledge curriculum provides a working model: vocabulary-rich, knowledge-based instruction that builds the semantic foundation precision requires. The AP English Language and Composition course, taken at scale, has demonstrated that explicit rhetorical instruction produces measurably better writers.
03
Establish semantic hygiene standards for high-stakes institutional language
Legal, medical, regulatory, and clinical language should be subject to formal definitional maintenance. Terms with clinical or legal precision — "trauma," "violence," "safety" in their specific technical contexts — should have documented definitions in institutional use that are protected from casual expansion. This is not about preventing linguistic change generally; it is about maintaining functional precision in domains where precision has real consequences. Federal agencies already use "plain language" requirements (the Plain Writing Act of 2010); a complementary requirement for definitional precision in regulatory language would protect against semantic drift in the other direction. Medical licensing boards, bar associations, and HR professional bodies could establish semantic standards for their domains.
04
Design AI literacy tools that improve rather than substitute for reading capacity
AI systems are currently being designed primarily to remove the friction of reading: summarize, simplify, translate. This is the path to deeper dependency. AI tools could instead be designed to scaffold literacy: to annotate rather than summarize, to ask questions that build comprehension rather than provide conclusions, to highlight unfamiliar vocabulary in context rather than replacing it. The distinction matters: an AI that helps a user understand a contract is different from an AI that understands the contract for the user. The first builds capacity; the second substitutes for it. Product design choices about which model to build are policy choices, and they should be treated as such.
05
Invest in vocabulary-rich early childhood education
Reading comprehension is constrained by vocabulary. You cannot understand a text whose words you don't know, even if you can decode the letters. Hart and Risley's landmark 1995 study "Meaningful Differences in the Everyday Experiences of Young American Children" documented the "word gap" — children from professional families hear approximately 30 million more words by age 3 than children from welfare families, and those early vocabulary differences predict reading achievement through high school. Vocabulary instruction must be explicit, systematic, and vocabulary-rich — not incidental. Isabel Beck's "Bringing Words to Life" framework and the Tier 2 vocabulary approach (high-value words that appear across academic domains) provide practical models. Universal pre-K with strong language exposure would have measurable literacy effects within a generation.
06
Reframe reading as civic infrastructure, not personal enrichment
The dominant cultural framing of reading in the United States is as personal enrichment: reading is something you do for pleasure or self-improvement. This framing makes it optional and leisure-adjacent — easy to displace by competing entertainment. The accurate framing is civic and functional: reading is the cognitive infrastructure of self-governance, contract enforcement, medical safety, and professional competence. A society in which half the population cannot read complex text cannot self-govern in the ways democratic institutions require. This reframing should appear in political discourse, in school curricula, and in cultural products. The decline in reading is not a cultural shift to be shrugged at — it is a civic emergency with the same structural stakes as infrastructure decay or institutional corruption.
07
Revive classical rhetoric as a secondary education requirement
The trivium — grammar, rhetoric, and logic — formed the core of Western education for over a millennium precisely because these disciplines are the foundations of precise thought and communication. Rhetoric is not manipulation; in its classical form, it is the study of how to construct persuasive arguments from evidence, how to identify logical fallacies, and how to evaluate the arguments of others. A population trained in classical rhetoric is less susceptible to demagoguery (because it can identify the moves), more capable of precise argumentation, and better equipped to distinguish between precision and performance. Contemporary high school students encounter none of this. Its reintroduction — not as an elective but as a core requirement — would begin rebuilding the cognitive foundation that semantic dissolution has been eroding.

The philosopher of mind Maryanne Wolf, who has studied reading neuroscience for three decades, offers the most precise summation of what is at stake: "The young person who can write precisely is also the young person who can think precisely, because writing is thinking made visible and durable." The reverse is also true, and it is the future we are building by inaction: the young person who cannot write precisely is also the young person who cannot think precisely about anything the language of writing was designed to think about. And as that language becomes more inflected with dissolved words — "violence" that covers argument, "safety" that covers comfort, "trauma" that covers inconvenience — there will be less and less of civilization that can be thought about precisely.

The priestly class will not disappear. Someone will always be able to read the original documents. The question is whether that will be the many or the few, and what accountability structures will govern the few's interpretation for the many. The answer to that question will determine whether what comes next looks like a library or a cathedral — open to all, or open to the ordained.

Methodology

How this report was researched

This report synthesizes primary sources (government literacy assessments, academic peer-reviewed research, employer surveys, and historical texts), secondary analysis (literacy scholarship, linguistics research, and educational policy literature), and original analysis applied to documented trends.

Statistical claims are drawn from nationally administered assessments (NAAL, PIAAC, NAEP) and employer surveys (NACE) with documented methodologies. Linguistic claims about word evolution are grounded in lexicographic sources (Oxford English Dictionary, Merriam-Webster) and scholarly work in historical semantics. The word dissolution table entries are documented against their source definitions in DSM, legal dictionaries, and canonical scholarly texts (Arendt, Eco, Klemperer).

Section 5's projections are explicitly conditional extrapolations, not predictions. They describe trajectory, not fate. The timelines are constructed from generational math (educational cohort timelines to career stages) and draw on organizational behavior literature for the institutional failure patterns described. The historical analogies to pre-literate European structures are drawn from Walter Ong's scholarship and are acknowledged as imperfect parallels.

Key limitations: this report focuses on American English literacy. Literacy dynamics differ substantially across languages and educational systems. The report does not model the intervention effects of AI tools on literacy outcomes, which are genuinely uncertain. The relationship between measured literacy and functional capacity in specific professional domains is complex and not fully captured by the standardized assessments cited.
