Understanding Embedding Models:
A Visual Journey Through Vector Space

Embedding models transform language into geometry. This interactive guide shows exactly how text becomes numbers, why those numbers capture meaning, how to navigate 3D word space, and what separates a great embedding model from a mediocre one.

Overview

Language as geometry

When you search for "cozy mystery novels" and get back results about "whodunit books," something remarkable is happening. The model doesn't match keywords — it understands that these phrases occupy nearly the same position in a mathematical space where meaning is distance.

That mathematical space is what embedding models produce. They take a piece of text — a word, a sentence, a document — and map it to a point in a high-dimensional vector. Similar meanings land close together; unrelated concepts land far apart. The result is that language becomes a space you can do arithmetic in.

This guide follows the structure of a canonical question: how do you actually choose an embedding model for your use case? To answer that, you need to understand what they do, how they differ, what the benchmarks mean, and what trade-offs matter for your specific workload. We'll build that understanding visually, layer by layer.

Key intuition

An embedding is a function that converts meaning into position. Words and sentences with similar meanings get similar coordinates. "Automobile" and "car" end up close together. "Quantum" and "recipe" end up far apart. Everything follows from this.

How it works

From tokens to vectors: what happens inside

Modern embedding models are transformer-based neural networks. The architecture is the same family as language models like GPT — the key difference is the training objective and how you use the output.

1
Tokenization
Text is split into subword tokens. "Embedding" might become ["Embed", "ding"]. Each token maps to an integer ID.
2
Token embeddings
Each token ID is looked up in a learned embedding table — an initial vector representation, randomly initialized then trained.
3
Transformer layers
Self-attention lets every token "look at" every other token, building up contextual meaning. "Bank" in "river bank" gets different representations than "bank" in "savings bank."
4
Pooling
The last layer's token representations are pooled into a single vector — usually by averaging, or using a special [CLS] token designed to represent the whole input.
5
Normalization
The vector is normalized to unit length. This converts dot product similarity into cosine similarity, making comparisons consistent regardless of input length.

Training is where the magic happens. Models are trained with contrastive loss: similar texts should produce similar vectors, dissimilar texts should produce different ones. A dataset of millions of (text, similar-text) pairs teaches the model what "similar" means.

The geometry of meaning

Why vector arithmetic works

The most famous demonstration of embedding geometry is the analogy test: king − man + woman ≈ queen. This is not magic — it emerges from how the model is trained.

Consider what the model learns about "man" and "woman" and "king" and "queen." The difference between "man" and "king" (adding royalty) is the same geometric relationship as the difference between "woman" and "queen." So the vector king − man points in a direction that means "add royalty," and adding it to "woman" moves you to "queen."

This only works because the training process forces regularities. If royalty is always described similarly across genders in the training corpus, the model learns a consistent "royalty direction" in vector space. That direction is the same whether you start from "man" or "woman."

Why this matters practically

Vector arithmetic isn't a party trick — it's how retrieval works. When you embed a query and search for nearest neighbors, you're doing geometry: finding which stored vectors are closest in direction to your query vector. The better the geometry, the better the retrieval.

Measuring similarity

Cosine similarity: the ruler in vector space

The standard way to compare two embeddings is cosine similarity — the cosine of the angle between the two vectors. It ranges from −1 (opposite meanings) through 0 (unrelated) to +1 (identical meaning). Because vectors are normalized to unit length, this equals the dot product.

Why cosine and not Euclidean distance? Because we care about direction, not magnitude. A short document and a long document about the same topic should match — and after normalization, they do. Their vectors point in the same direction, giving high cosine similarity regardless of how long the original text was.

Dimensionality

From 1536 dimensions to 3D: the compression problem

Real embedding models output vectors with hundreds or thousands of dimensions. OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small outputs 1536 dimensions. Cohere's Embed v3 outputs 1024. Voyage AI's models go up to 4096.

We can't visualize 1536-dimensional space directly, so researchers use dimensionality reduction algorithms — PCA, t-SNE, UMAP — to project down to 2D or 3D while preserving neighborhood relationships as much as possible. Nearby points in 1536D should stay nearby in 3D.

The animation below simulates this collapse: starting with points scattered through high-dimensional space, then watching the structure emerge as PCA finds the principal axes of variance and projects everything down. The clustering you see is real — it exists in the high-dimensional space; reduction just makes it visible.

Choosing a model

What actually separates the good models from the great ones

The benchmark everyone references is MTEB — Massive Text Embedding Benchmark. It tests models across 56 tasks in 8 categories: retrieval, reranking, clustering, classification, and more. A high MTEB score means the model's geometry generalizes across many task types.

But MTEB score is not the only dimension that matters. In production, you balance several trade-offs:

Trade-off 1
Quality vs. speed
Larger models (higher MTEB) embed more slowly. If you're indexing millions of documents in a batch job, you can afford a slow model. If you're embedding user queries at request time, latency matters enormously. OpenAI's ada-002 is fast; Cohere's Rerank is accurate.
Trade-off 2
Dimensions vs. storage
More dimensions generally means better quality — up to a point. But a 4096-dimension vector takes 4× the storage and RAM of a 1024-dimension vector. At hundreds of millions of vectors, this becomes a real infrastructure cost. Matryoshka embeddings (OpenAI's text-embedding-3) let you truncate to fewer dimensions without retraining.
Trade-off 3
Domain fit vs. general capability
A model trained on general web text may underperform on legal documents, medical records, or code. Domain-specific fine-tuning can dramatically improve quality for specialized corpora. Voyage AI offers domain-specific models for finance, law, and code that beat general models on those tasks.
Trade-off 4
Multilingual vs. English-only
If your corpus or users span multiple languages, you need a multilingual model. Cohere's Embed v3 Multilingual covers 100+ languages. English-only models (like most OpenAI embeddings) will cluster Spanish and English text separately even when they mean the same thing.
Trade-off 5
API vs. self-hosted
API models (OpenAI, Cohere, Voyage) are easiest to start with but have per-token costs and data-privacy implications. Self-hosted open models (BGE, E5, Nomic) run on your infrastructure. For high-volume or sensitive workloads, self-hosted often wins on cost and compliance.

Practical guidance

Decision framework for choosing your embedding model

Start here

For most RAG applications with English text: text-embedding-3-small for cost efficiency, text-embedding-3-large for maximum quality, voyage-3 if you need very long context windows. Evaluate on your own data before deploying.

1
Define your task type first
Semantic search, clustering, classification, and reranking each have different quality profiles. A model that excels at retrieval may be mediocre at classification. MTEB breaks this down — look at task-specific scores, not just averages.
2
Measure context length requirements
If your documents are longer than the model's context window, you'll need to chunk them. Models with 8K+ context (voyage-3's 32K, GTE-Qwen2's 131K) handle long-form content without losing context across chunk boundaries.
3
Estimate volume and cost
Calculate token volume at your expected scale. At 50M tokens/month, the cost difference between models can be thousands of dollars. Self-hosted models have zero per-token cost but non-zero infrastructure overhead.
4
Build an evaluation set
Collect 50–200 (query, relevant-document) pairs from your actual use case. Run all candidate models. Measure recall@10 — do the true answers appear in the top 10 results? This single metric reveals more than MTEB for your specific domain.
5
Consider Matryoshka models for flexibility
OpenAI's text-embedding-3 models and Nomic Embed v1.5 support Matryoshka Representation Learning — you can truncate to 256, 512, or 1024 dimensions for 4–6× storage savings with modest quality loss. Useful when storage or RAM is a constraint.
Methodology

About this report

This report synthesizes canonical pedagogy from public tutorials on embedding models, technical documentation from OpenAI, Cohere, Voyage AI, and Hugging Face, and the MTEB leaderboard (June 2026 snapshot). The 3D visualizations show geometrically accurate relationships between word categories — the positions reflect the structural properties of real word2vec-style embeddings, simplified to three principal components for visual clarity.

The interactive demos use simulated vectors to illustrate concepts. Real embedding models would produce similar outputs on these examples but are not called live. MTEB scores and model capabilities evolve rapidly; verify current benchmarks at huggingface.co/spaces/mteb/leaderboard before making production decisions.
