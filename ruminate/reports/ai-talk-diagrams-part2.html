Context Window Degradation

A full context window doesn't just slow down — it causes answer quality to collapse. Models work well when the context is under 70–80% full. Beyond that, they start repeating themselves, losing track of instructions given early in the conversation, and producing subtly incorrect answers. The cliff is sharp.

This is partly because the model must "attend" to everything in context equally. A crowded context dilutes attention, and the model's earliest instructions — often the most important ones — get the least focus.

The degradation curve shape is illustrative — exact behaviour varies by model, task type, and how early the key instructions appear in context. The cliff effect is well-documented across frontier models.

Training vs. Inference

"Training" and "inference" are the two major phases of an AI model's life — and they look nothing alike. Training is the expensive, slow, one-time process of building the model. Inference is what happens every time you actually use it: fast, cheap by comparison, and run thousands of times per second across millions of users.

A useful way to think about it: training is building the factory; inference is running the factory. The factory costs hundreds of millions to build and takes months to construct. But once it's running, you can crank out products very quickly.

The model sees trillions of tokens of text and adjusts billions of internal numbers (parameters) to minimise its prediction errors. When training ends, those numbers are frozen. The model is now a fixed artifact — the "weights file."

You send your prompt. The frozen model runs it through its layers of parameters and predicts the most likely next token — over and over, until the answer is complete. The model doesn't "learn" from your query. It just runs.

Cost figures are approximate and vary widely by model size, hardware, and provider. GPT-4-class training costs are estimated at $50M–$100M per run; Llama-class open models are cheaper by one to two orders of magnitude.

What is a Parameter?

You'll hear numbers like "70 billion parameters" or "1 trillion parameters" thrown around, but what does that actually mean? A parameter is simply a number — a connection strength between nodes in the network. If you imagine the model as a giant web of interconnected neurons, every single wire between every single neuron has a weight. That weight is a parameter.

During training, the model adjusts all those weights billions of times until they collectively encode a useful model of language, reasoning, and knowledge. More parameters means more capacity to encode complexity — but beyond a certain point, what matters more is the quality and quantity of training data and how you train, not just the count.
