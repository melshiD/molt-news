Historical Context

Hubble Opened the Door First

The story of citizen astronomers and space telescopes begins not with Webb, but with a remarkable and little-known chapter from Hubble's early years — a program that proved amateur astronomers could produce genuine, peer-reviewed science with the world's premier observatory.

In December 1985, leaders of major U.S. amateur astronomy organizations met with the Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) — invited by the institute itself. STScI's founding director, Nobel laureate Riccardo Giacconi, had publicly announced his intention to devote a portion of Director's Discretionary Time to amateur astronomer proposals. The professional astronomy community would share its crown jewel.

  • The Amateur Astronomer Program
    December 1985
    Amateur organizations invited to STScI. Leaders of major U.S. amateur astronomy societies meet with institute leadership. Director Giacconi commits Director's Discretionary Time to non-professional astronomers.
    1986
    Amateur Astronomers Working Group formed. Participating organizations develop their own peer-review procedure. Proposals reviewed by a committee of amateurs and only accepted if they demonstrate genuine scientific merit, don't duplicate professional proposals, and specifically require Hubble's unique capabilities.
    April 1990 — launch & first amateur time
    Hubble launches; amateur program begins execution. The first of thirteen amateur astronomers are awarded observation time. These were not hobbyists taking snapshots — they submitted formal proposals that cleared a full scientific peer review, with selection criteria identical to professional programs except in who was allowed to review them.
    1990–1997
    Thirteen amateurs receive Hubble observation time. Their programs produce real science, including a study of post-eclipse brightening on Io (Jupiter's volcanic moon) and a second study published in Icarus, one of planetary science's leading journals. The program demonstrates conclusively that non-affiliated observers can contribute peer-reviewed discoveries.
    1997
    Program ends. Budget reductions at STScI make administrative support unsustainable. No formal dedicated amateur track has existed since — but standard GO proposals have remained technically open to any person worldwide, regardless of affiliation.
The Legacy: What Hubble Proved

Those thirteen Hubble proposals demonstrated something the professional astronomy community had debated: that scientific merit — not institutional pedigree — is what determines whether an observation is worth making. Two peer-reviewed publications resulted. Both appeared in Icarus. The science was real. When Webb launched in 2021, it inherited this precedent: open access by policy, not just in theory.

Access Pathways

Three Ways In — No Credentials Required

Webb offers three distinct pathways for citizen participation — ranging from directly observing the universe yourself (via a competitive proposal) to analyzing data that's already been collected, to classifying images as part of organized citizen science projects. All three are real contributions.

Primary Pathway
🔭
General Observer Program
The primary competitive allocation. Anyone worldwide — no institutional affiliation required — may submit a GO proposal using the Astronomer's Proposal Tool (APT). Proposals are reviewed through dual-anonymous peer review; panelists don't know who submitted it. Cycle 5 saw 2,930 proposals; 254 were accepted (8.9%). Webb's Cycle 6 will open for proposals later in 2026.
Lower Barrier Entry
📊
Archival Research Proposals
Propose original science using JWST data already collected and publicly released in MAST. No need to design observations or justify telescope time — only your scientific analysis. This is a meaningful entry point for citizen astronomers: compelling science using existing Webb images, spectra, or time-series data. U.S.-based proposers can request STScI funding to support the work.
Urgent Science
⚡
Director's Discretionary Time
Up to 10% of Webb's annual observation time is reserved for the STScI Director's discretionary use — no TAC review, rolling submission year-round. Appropriate for genuinely unexpected, time-sensitive targets: new comets, novae, supernovae, sudden outbursts, or rapidly evolving phenomena. Not a workaround for science that could have been proposed in a standard cycle.
Dual-Anonymous Peer Review: The Level Playing Field

In 2018, STScI analyzed 18 years of Hubble proposal outcomes and found women PIs had systematically lower success rates — an effect attributable to reviewer bias, not science quality. The institute introduced Dual-Anonymous Peer Review (DAPR): neither proposers nor reviewers know each other's identities. All JWST cycles have used DAPR from the beginning. The immediate result when introduced on Hubble: women PIs had a higher success rate than men for the first time.

For citizen astronomers and first-time proposers, DAPR is significant: your proposal is judged on the quality of the science, not on whether you work at Caltech. Your proposal PDF must be fully anonymized — no names, no institutions, no first-person references to your previous work. The playing field is genuinely level.

Success Stories

Citizen Scientists Who Made It

The most documented case of citizen scientists earning time on JWST came from volunteers on Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 — a Zooniverse project that asks members of the public to scan NASA's WISE infrared survey data for cold objects near the Sun. Three volunteers became co-investigators on an approved JWST program.

JWST Program 2124  ·  Approved Cycle 1
Backyard Worlds: Three Citizens Reach Webb
The approved program, "Explaining the Diversity of Cold Worlds," will study twelve brown dwarfs with identical surface temperatures but dramatically different infrared brightness — a key test for understanding cold-world atmospheres and exoplanet analogs. Three Backyard Worlds volunteers contributed discoveries that earned them co-investigator status on this program. All three were members of the public with no institutional affiliation at the time of their discoveries.

Proposal Writing

How to Write a Competitive Proposal

The TAC (Telescope Allocation Committee) receives thousands of proposals and accepts fewer than 10%. But 46% of accepted Cycle 5 programs were from first-time JWST PIs — the process rewards good science, not established names. Here is what the review criteria actually specify, and how to use them.

What Reviewers Actually Score

Every JWST GO proposal is evaluated on three explicit dimensions: (1) Impact within your subfield — does this significantly advance the specific scientific area? (2) Out-of-field impact — does this matter to astronomy broadly? (3) Suitability for Webb — is JWST uniquely required, or could this be done another way? Proposals that score high on all three — and that justify each dimension explicitly in the text — receive the highest ratings. Do not assume reviewers will infer the importance of your work. Write it out.

Science Justification
State the Importance Explicitly
Don't assume reviewers will infer why your science matters. Panelists may not be experts in your exact subfield. Write a direct, quantitative statement of what this observation will achieve and why that matters to astronomy. Include specific predictions: "We will constrain X to within Y% accuracy, sufficient to discriminate between models A and B."
Technical Justification
Justify Every Hour Requested
Use Webb's Exposure Time Calculator (ETC) to validate all exposure times. Over-requesting hours is penalized — reviewers know the ETC well. Justify your target selection, the number of targets, your instrument choice, filter selection, and any special scheduling constraints (e.g., time-sensitive, parallactic angle). If you need a specific configuration, explain why alternatives won't work.
JWST Uniqueness
Prove Webb Is Required
The most common fatal flaw: failing to argue that JWST specifically is needed. Why not HST? Why not a ground-based adaptive optics system? Why not Chandra? Webb's TAC is deciding whether to spend one of the most expensive telescope-hours in history. If the science could be done another way, they will ask why it shouldn't be. Preempt that question in your text.
Anonymization
Fully Anonymize Your PDF
Dual-anonymous review requires your proposal PDF to contain no identifying information. No names. No institution names. No "in our previous work (Smith et al. 2022), we showed..." — this reveals authorship. Reference prior work in the third person: "Previous observations (Smith et al. 2022) demonstrated..." Anonymization violations can result in disqualification before review.
Study Accepted Programs
Read What Got In
All approved JWST programs are publicly listed at STScI's approved programs page — search by cycle. Many PIs post their accepted proposal PDFs. Reading 10–20 accepted proposals in your target area will reveal the level of detail, the structure of arguments, and the kinds of justifications that pass review. This is the single most efficient preparation step.
Resubmission
Rejection Is Normal
At ~9% acceptance, exceptional proposals are routinely rejected simply due to competition. Proposals that receive reviewer feedback often succeed on resubmission after addressing concerns. Cycles happen annually — a rejected Cycle 5 proposal can be revised and resubmitted for Cycle 6. Persistence, not credentials, distinguishes successful proposers from unsuccessful ones in a competitive but fair process.

The Astronomer's Proposal Tool (APT)

APT is the free software through which all JWST (and Hubble) proposals are written, validated, and submitted. It bundles your science justification PDF with precise instrument configurations — filters, exposure times, dithering patterns, scheduling constraints. It includes a built-in time calculator and validates technical feasibility before submission. APT is available for free download from STScI and runs on Windows, Mac, and Linux. Learning APT before your cycle deadline, not during it, is strongly recommended — the interface has a learning curve.

Citizen Science

Participate Without a Proposal

For those who want to contribute to real astronomy without navigating the GO proposal process, citizen science projects offer immediate participation. Several active projects use JWST data specifically, and prior citizen science contributions have directly led to JWST observation time — the Backyard Worlds example is the clearest proof of that pipeline.

Zooniverse · Active
Galaxy Zoo — Webb Edition
The original Galaxy Zoo launched in 2007; it now incorporates over 500,000 galaxy images from JWST's COSMOS-Web survey — the largest galaxy morphology dataset in history. Volunteers classify galaxies by shape (spiral vs. elliptical, number of arms, bar presence, merger signs). An earlier JWST pilot using CEERS data helped establish the existence of stable disc galaxies at redshift 7.4 — just 700 million years after the Big Bang. NASA promotes this as accessible to anyone ages 10 and up.
500,000+ Webb galaxy images available for classification
Zooniverse · Active
Backyard Worlds: Planet 9
Volunteers scan NASA's WISE infrared survey data for cold objects near the Sun — brown dwarfs, rogue planets, and faint companions. This project produced the Caselden, Sainio, and Thevenot discoveries that earned JWST Program 2124 co-investigator status. The project has generated 20+ peer-reviewed papers with 30+ citizen scientist co-authors, and earned time on Spitzer, IRTF, and Keck before JWST. Brown dwarf discoveries from this project are now being observed by Webb.
20+ peer-reviewed papers, 30+ citizen scientist co-authors
Zooniverse · Active
Planet Hunters TESS
Analyzing NASA TESS light curves for transiting exoplanets. As of early 2023, it had found 284 exoplanet candidates and 15 confirmed exoplanets. While no dedicated "Planet Hunters JWST" project exists yet, several exoplanet candidates discovered by citizen scientists through TESS data have been selected for JWST atmospheric follow-up — another direct pipeline from citizen discovery to space telescope observation.
284 candidates found · 15 confirmed exoplanets
Hubble / Legacy
Hubble Citizen Science Archive

Galaxy Zoo: Hubble: 86,000 volunteers, 40.6 million assessments on 120,000 galaxies. Andromeda Project: ~30,000 contributors identified 2,753 star clusters. Public Archive

MAST: Webb's Data Is Already Yours

All JWST data eventually becomes publicly available at the Mikulski Archive for Space Telescopes (MAST). No account required to search, explore, or download. Downloads from the AWS cloud are completely free. Webb science data began becoming public on July 12–13, 2022 — the same day as the first image release. Any data older than its 12-month proprietary period is immediately accessible.

What's Available at MAST

MAST holds all Webb data at every processing stage: raw uncalibrated exposures (Stage 0), detector-corrected data (Stage 1), flux-calibrated individual exposures (Stage 2), and fully combined final science products — mosaics, spectra, time series (Stage 3). High-Level Science Products contributed by science teams are also available. AWS Public Dataset provides bulk downloads for free at s3://stpubdata/jwst/ — no AWS account required.

Downloaded FITS files can be processed using STScI's open-source JWST Calibration Pipeline (Python), PixInsight for imaging, or standard astronomy tools (DS9, Astropy). Multiple community tutorials cover the full workflow from archive download to final processed image. This is how citizen astronomers produced some of the most widely shared processed versions of Webb's public images.

Your Next Steps

Where to Begin

Six concrete starting points, ordered from lowest barrier to most ambitious. You don't have to work through them in sequence — but each one is a real entry point into Webb's scientific ecosystem.

01
Browse MAST Right Now
No account needed. Go to archive.stsci.edu and search for any target — a galaxy, a nebula, an exoplanet system. Download the public FITS files. STScI's JWST Calibration Pipeline (Python, free, open-source) will let you process them into final images. This is where several of the most widely circulated Webb image reprocessings began.
archive.stsci.edu →
02
Classify Galaxies on Zooniverse
Galaxy Zoo's JWST edition has over 500,000 Webb images awaiting human classification. You're literally extending the scientific reach of COSMOS-Web — the largest JWST survey ever conducted. Thirty minutes of classification contributes to real science. Ages 10 and up. No background required.
Galaxy Zoo →
03
Hunt Brown Dwarfs
Backyard Worlds: Planet 9 is the project that produced JWST co-investigators from the public. It's still active. You'd be scanning NASA WISE infrared data for cold objects near the Sun. Past volunteers have discovered objects that are now being observed by JWST. This is the clearest documented pipeline from citizen discovery to Webb observation time.
backyardworlds.org →
04
Download and Learn APT
The Astronomer's Proposal Tool is free from STScI and can be installed today. Before any cycle deadline, having APT running and a draft proposal underway is the prerequisite. The tool has a learning curve — working with it before you need it is the most consistent advice from veteran proposers. The JWST Docs site at jwst-docs.stsci.edu covers proposal workflows in detail.
Download APT →
05
Study Accepted Programs
STScI publishes the full list of all approved JWST GO programs by cycle. Many PIs share their accepted proposal PDFs publicly. Reading 10–20 accepted proposals in a science area you care about is the most direct preparation for submitting one. The JWST Peer Review Guide is also publicly available and describes exactly what panelists are asked to score.
Approved Programs →
06
Submit an Archival Proposal
If you're not ready to propose new observations, the Archival Research category lets you propose a scientific investigation using data already in MAST. Write a compelling case for original analysis of existing Webb data. The review criteria are the same — scientific impact and merit — but there's no instrument configuration to justify and no observing time to request. A lower-risk path to your first accepted JWST proposal.
